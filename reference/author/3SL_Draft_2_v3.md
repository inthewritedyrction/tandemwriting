# CHAPTER 1: SILAS

The first time Silas Graves killed somebody, he’d thrown up for an hour.

The seventh time, dinner stayed down.

By the nineteenth, he could drink water afterward without tasting blood.

The twenty-third time, he hadn’t blinked.

Tonight would make twenty-four.

Jerrick’s blood hit the dirt between them.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and came forward again, dragging his right foot just enough to confirm what Silas had noticed four minutes ago. Something was wrong with his knee. Each time Silas shifted right, the man turned his whole body instead of trusting that leg to carry him.

The crowd hadn’t noticed yet. They were too busy screaming.

“Graves!”

His name hit the Gutterlight Ring’s stone walls and came back louder. Boots rattled the metal stands. Veilmarks struck palms behind the chain-link. Somewhere near the eastern Bookkeeper box, a woman kept shouting for Jerrick to get his ass up, though he hadn’t fallen yet.

Silas would’ve respected the faith if she wasn’t about to lose money behind it.

Jerrick spat again. “You ain’t tired?”

Silas rolled his left shoulder. It had gone stiff around the eighth minute, right after Jerrick caught him with an elbow he shouldn’t have landed. His ribs hurt too. Nothing felt broken, but morning was still finna be rude as hell.

He raised his hands.

Jerrick laughed through the blood covering his teeth. “Ain’t got shit to say now?”

Talking wasted breath, and Silas had already given this man twelve minutes more than he deserved.

A red ward burned around the lamps above the Ring, marking the match steel-legal. Both fighters had been searched before entering. Two blades each. Nothing dipped in poison, dressed in Silencecraft, or carrying a death tether that could follow somebody home. The Cage Wardens checked for all three and still missed something every fight night.

The official Pit rules prohibited killing. The Floor Judge had repeated that twice before the gate opened, loud enough for witnesses and the record charm hanging above his head.

Now his chair faced the Bookkeepers.

Jerrick lunged.

Silas stepped outside the punch and drove two knuckles into the soft space beneath his ribs. Jerrick folded around the hit. Silas caught the back of his neck, brought his knee up, and felt cartilage give beneath it.

A scream tore through the crowd as Jerrick stumbled away holding his face. Blood poured between his fingers, but he stayed upright. Lowtorch men knew how to stand through pain. They spent their lives beside furnaces hot enough to blister skin through a work coat, and burn scars climbed from Jerrick’s collar to his jaw, shiny and tight beneath the red light.

Jerrick was used to hurting and staying upright. Silas quit counting on pain to slow him.

The bad knee was still useful, though.

Jerrick wiped his nose and turned toward the raised platform across the Ring. Mother Rae sat between two Bookkeepers with one leg crossed over the other, black fabric covering her from throat to ankle. Silver bells circled her wrist, quiet despite the noise around her. She hadn’t cheered or touched the glass at her side. Her eyes followed Jerrick without giving him a damn thing to read.

He shouldn’t have looked at her.

Silas hit him before he turned back around. His shoulder drove into Jerrick’s chest and sent him back three steps. Jerrick swung wild. Silas ducked beneath it, caught his wrist, and twisted until the knife hidden beneath his sleeve dropped into the dirt.

One blade the Wardens had somehow missed.

Silas kicked it beneath the chain-link. A runner snatched the weapon before it stopped moving and disappeared into the crowd. Jerrick’s eyes jumped toward the gate, but no one moved to help him.

“You know what this is,” Silas said.

Jerrick’s face tightened. “Wasn’t my fault them little niggas took a dirty route.”

The runner nearest the gate went still. He couldn’t have been much older than the boys Jerrick had sold out. His brown route coat hung too wide across his shoulders, and a burn mark crossed the front where somebody had tried to remove the block seal stitched there.

Silas turned back to Jerrick. “You sold it.”

“I sold information. What they did after ain’t got shit to do with me.”

Three runners had disappeared along that line. Remy had spent two nights checking burial lists while Jude tracked every holding ward between Shadowmere and Stoneveil. Fifteen, seventeen, and nineteen.

Jerrick had still taken the match.

Maybe he thought the crowd would protect him. Maybe somebody told him Silas wouldn’t kill under Gutterlight with this many witnesses.

Folks heard rules and forgot to ask who paid for them.

Across the Ring, Mother Rae tapped two fingers against her knee.

*Make it clean.*

Jerrick caught the signal too. His hand went for the blade at his waist.

Silas’s came out first.

He crossed the space between them and struck Jerrick’s wrist before the knife cleared its sheath. Bone snapped beneath Silas’s grip. Jerrick screamed and swung with his other hand, catching Silas across the mouth before Silas stepped behind him and locked an arm over his chest.

Jerrick fought hard once he understood.

His boots dug trenches through the dirt. His good hand clawed at Silas’s forearm. The back of his head caught Silas near the cheek, sending a sharp ache beneath his eye, but Silas held on.

“Wait,” Jerrick gasped. “Wait, man.”

Silas set the point of his blade beneath Jerrick’s ribs.

“Graves—”

The knife went in clean.

Jerrick jerked against him. Silas kept his arm firm across the man’s chest until his boots stopped scraping. Warm blood spread over Silas’s hand and gathered beneath his nails. Jerrick’s breath came short twice more before his body sagged.

Silas lowered him to the ground.

The silence lasted until Jerrick’s head touched dirt. Then the crowd surged against the fence.

Veilmarks flew overhead. People climbed onto benches to see the body while the Cage Wardens shoved them back with shock batons. Silas pulled his blade free as Mother Rae rose from the platform. Her bells rang once before the crowd swallowed the sound and she disappeared toward the exit.

The Quiet Staff came through the service gate with a stretcher and two bowls of salt. One worker crouched beside Jerrick while another waited for Silas to clear the blood before pouring salt over it.

The salt wasn’t only cleanup. Every fight ended with a rite to power the ward lines, and the Quiet Staff had already begun it. Silas had watched the process enough times that he barely followed their hands anymore. By the time they lifted Jerrick onto the stretcher, Gutterlight would already be making room for the next match.

Silas wiped his blade across Jerrick’s shirt and stepped away.

“Match concluded,” the Floor Judge announced once the Quiet Staff had control of the Ring. He lowered his attention to the record sheet before the words were fully out of his mouth and never looked at Jerrick again.

By morning, the official report would say Jerrick died after the match ended. Shadowmere paperwork could make damn near anything true if the right person held the pen, and somebody with the last name Graves usually did.

Silas left the lie to the people who got paid to write it and headed for the fighter’s gate.

Remy waited outside the Cage with a bottle of water in one hand and Silas’s route coat over his arm. His locs were tied back from his face, though one had worked loose near his ear. Behind him, the Quiet Staff carried Jerrick through the service entrance.

“You look terrible,” Remy said.

Silas took the bottle. “You say that every time.”

The first swallow burned his split lip. He finished half the bottle anyway, then poured the rest over his head. Water ran through his locs and carried blood down the side of his neck.

Remy shoved the coat against his chest. “Put this on before Camille see you.”

“I’m hot.”

“And bloody.”

Silas took the coat. Remy’s mama and Camille had known each other too long for him to pretend that wasn’t the actual threat. He slid one arm into the sleeve. Pain caught in his shoulder before he managed the other, and Remy noticed.

“That man caught you?”

“Once.”

Remy’s mouth pulled to one side.

Silas reached for the duffel beneath the bench before he could argue the number. “You find the runners?”

The joke left Remy’s face. “Two.”

“Alive?”

“One.”

The crowd inside had already started roaring for the next match. Silas tightened his grip on the bag. “Which one?”

“Seventeen. Block medic got him tucked away.”

“And the other?”

Remy shook his head.

Three names had gone onto Jude’s list. One could come off.

Silas pulled the strap over his good shoulder. “What about the youngest?”

“Still nothing.”

Through the open Cage door, the runner who’d grabbed Jerrick’s hidden knife was passing a bucket between the rows, collecting side bets like his hands hadn’t been shaking five minutes ago.

“Jerrick send anything before the fight?”

“Bookkeeper found a route token in his locker.” Remy lowered his voice. “Dawnveil stamp.”

Silas turned toward him. “Real?”

“Jude’s checking.”

A real Dawnveil token could move a person past two guarded tiers. Three runners had disappeared along a line Jerrick sold. If the token was real, somebody had put that kind of access in his hands. If it was fake, somebody had risked getting a whole family Muted to make the route look clean.

Either way, the youngest runner was still somewhere behind it.

Silas held out his hand.

Remy looked down at his palm. “Jude has it.”

“I wanna see it.”

“I know. He knows too.”

Which meant Silas wasn’t getting the token until Jude was finished with it.

He dropped his hand.

A bell rang inside the Ring, calling the next fighters from their Cages. Remy waited until the sound faded before stepping closer.

“North route got Shrouds posted near the tram. Two I saw. Could be more by Back Market.”

Shrouds didn’t waste time standing around Shadowmere. They came down, took who they wanted, and left the cleanup for everybody else.

“Take south,” Remy said. “Don’t use Mercer Passage.”

Silas adjusted the duffel strap. “Send me the names from Jerrick’s ledger.”

“I will. You go home.”

Silas started toward the corridor.

“And don’t double back,” Remy called after him. “Text me when you get in.”

Silas lifted one hand without turning around. “Yes, Daddy.”

“Fuck you.”

Cold water struck Silas’s face the second he stepped outside.

Rain collected along the cavern ceiling and slipped through cracks in the rock, falling hardest near Gutterlight where the heat kept the stone sweating. Violet mist covered the lower half of the street. Neon signs burned through it in broken patches of pink and blue, reflecting off puddles deep enough to hide a knife.

Silas left his route coat open while he walked. Closing it would hide the blood. It would also slow his hand if somebody tried him.

Food stalls still crowded the Back Market despite the hour. A woman stirred something dark in a metal pot while her daughter called prices beside her. Two relic sellers argued over a jar that kept rattling on its own. At the entrance to a shop, a threshold worker redrew marks over the stone and stopped every few strokes to wipe rain from the surface.

A black lantern hung above the northern alley.

Silas took the south route.

People gave him room without staring long. A few noticed the blood on his shirt and looked away. Shadowmere manners. Whatever a person carried home from the Pit belonged to them unless they dropped dead in the street.

The tram platform stood three blocks from Gutterlight. Half the lamps had gone out, forcing everyone beneath the ones that still worked. A woman pulled her little boy closer when she noticed Silas, and he shifted the duffel in front of the blood on his shirt before taking a place near the rear entrance.

The south tram arrived with a hard scrape against the rail. Its doors opened, and everybody boarded fast. Nobody wanted to be standing on a platform if the Shrouds decided to close the line.

Silas took the back seat and rested his injured shoulder against the wall. A metal panel covered the window beside him, polished enough to give him pieces of his reflection. One cheek had started swelling. His lip was split. Blood had dried beneath his beard.

Camille was gon’ raise hell.

His phone vibrated before the tram pulled away.

Rhea: You alive?

Silas: Yeah.

Three dots appeared, disappeared, then returned.

Rhea: Ma at your apartment.

Silas closed his eyes.

Another message came through before he could decide whether ignoring his mother counted as self-preservation.

Jude: Remy told me what you did.

Silas: What I do?

Jude: He dead ain’t he?

Silas: Check the route token.

Jude: Mind your business.

Silas put the phone away before he had to acknowledge how much Jude sounded like their mother.

The tram jerked forward. Buildings passed close on either side, their upper windows disappearing into mist. Colored lanterns marked the doors below. Amber meant the route was open. Blue warned that somebody was watching. Red told folks to keep moving unless they wanted to become part of the problem.

A speaker crackled above his head.

“Movement between tiers requires active authorization under the Doctrine of Descent. Present all route permissions when requested. Unapproved travel will result in immediate detainment.”

Across from him, the woman from the platform tucked the edge of her son’s coat over the route patch on his chest.

Nobody else reacted.

Twenty minutes later, the tram stopped at Silas’s destination.

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane sat between a tailor shop and a threshold worker who charged Upper Tier prices for Deep Tier work. The brick had gone black from years of rain. Somebody had painted over the old building number twice, but the three still showed beneath it.

Silas climbed the stairs to 3C and stopped outside long enough to listen. The radiator knocked against the wall. A pencil scratched over paper inside. Someone shifted in a chair near the kitchen.

Silas unlocked the door and found his mother at the counter in a threadbare robe and house shoes that should’ve been thrown away before Jude learned to walk. Her glasses rested on top of her head. A crossword lay open beneath one hand, and a cup of tea cooled beside it.

She didn’t look up when he came in.

“Seven letters. Ancient city buried beneath ash.”

Silas locked the deadbolt behind him. “Pompeii.”

“Too obvious.”

“Stabiae.”

Camille counted the boxes before writing it in. “Better.”

The apartment had started as his. Somewhere between his twelfth fight and Camille getting a key, half his things had been moved to make room for hers. Eucalyptus dried over the sink. Ward ledgers filled the shelf beside his unpaid tram notices. Spools of funeral thread sat in a wooden tray on the table, each one labeled in Camille’s small handwriting.

She had put a plant in his window too. Silas didn’t know what it was, but keeping anything that green alive in Shadowmere felt like showing off.

“Shoes,” Camille said.

He set his duffel beside the door. Blood darkened the edge of one boot. Silas took both off without arguing and reached for his coat.

“Good,” she said. “You finally learned not to talk back.”

“Don’t get excited,” Silas grunted. “I’m just tired tonight that’s all.”

Her pencil kept moving. “How many rounds?”

“One.”

“Mmm-hmm.” Camille moved to the next clue. “Nine letters. ‘To make a choice without force.’”

“Volition.”

“Something you apparently don’t believe in.”

Silas went to the sink and turned on the cold water. “I make choices every day.”

“You choose how to do what you already decided you was gon’ do.”

“Still a choice.” Silas shrugged.

Camille lifted her head from the crossword. “You kill him?”

Blood lifted from beneath Silas’s nails and disappeared down the drain. “Yeah.”

She studied his face for a moment, nodded once, and went back to the puzzle. Whatever else she thought about it could wait until she decided he needed to hear it.

Silas washed his blade next. Jerrick’s blood had dried near the handle where the water couldn’t reach, so he used the small brush Camille kept beside the sink for cleansing tools.

“You eat?” she asked.

“Protein bar.”

Camille made a face. “That ain’t food. I made stew.”

Silas almost told her the protein bar had technically contained food. He wanted to eat sometime tonight, though, so he kept that to himself.

He dried the knife and returned it to the sheath. A sharp sting cut across his knuckles when he flexed his hand, and the breath he sucked through his teeth brought Camille out of her chair before he could hide it.

“What happened?”

“He bit me.”

Her whole face changed. “Let me see.”

Silas gave her the injured hand before she started searching the rest of him. Teeth had opened the skin across two knuckles. The cut wasn’t deep, but the edges were starting to swell.

Camille caught his wrist and turned it toward the light. “People carry all type of nasty shit in they mouth.”

She opened the cabinet beneath the sink and pulled out a shallow tin. Silas recognized Jude’s ointment from the smell before she finished twisting off the lid.

“Not that.”

Camille looked at him. “Then let your hand rot.”

The dark ointment went cold the second she packed it into the cut. Silas tried to pull back, but Camille tightened her grip on his wrist.

“Damn, Ma.”

Camille gave him the same silence that had ended arguments between all three of her children since they were old enough to talk.

Silas shut up and let her finish.

When she released him, the chain beneath his shirt slipped free. One link had caught near his collar, dark with dried blood.

Camille’s fingers closed around it.

“Silas.”

“I didn’t see it.”

She turned the link between her fingers, checking both sides before her attention dropped to his wrapped hand.

“You open?”

“No.”

Killing Jerrick hadn’t required that. Steel had been enough.

Camille held his stare another second before letting the chain go. “Then how you don’t see blood on your neck?”

“It ain’t mine.” Silas took the chain from her and returned to the sink. He wet a cloth, cleaned between each link, dried it, then tucked the chain beneath his shirt again.

Camille watched him finish, then returned to the crossword.

“Fourteen across. ‘A song for the dead.’ Seven letters.”

“Requiem.”

Her pencil moved across the boxes while Silas lifted the lid from the pot. The stew was thick with potatoes and mushrooms, and the meat looked expensive enough that Camille would lie if he asked where she got it. He fixed a bowl and took the seat across from her.

“Where’s Jude?” she asked.

“With Remy,” Silas replied.

Camille filled another square without looking up. “They working?”

“Something like that.” Silas exhaled before putting a spoonful in his mouth. “I think he’s staying in Ashvein tonight.”

His mother’s pencil stopped halfway through the next letter. “Six letters. ‘The responsibility one carries.’”

“Burden.”

Camille made a quiet sound in her throat and wrote it in.

Silas glanced across the table. “You picked this puzzle for me?”

“I don’t control the newspaper.”

He almost smiled.

They let the apartment go quiet after that. Camille worked through three more clues while Silas ate, the radiator knocking behind them whenever a tram passed beneath the street. His shoulder had started throbbing in time with his pulse. He’d need to soak it before morning if he planned to lift his arm without cussing.

Camille tapped the pencil against the page. “Six letters. ‘One who arranges trade between two parties.’”

“Broker.”

“Could be.”

Silas stopped chewing.

Camille finally looked at him. “So is Mercer.”

There it was.

He set his spoon down. “What Daddy want?”

“I ain’t say he wanted anything.”

“You ain’t have to.”

Camille pushed her glasses higher on her head. “He wants you in his office tomorrow. Noon.”

Silas leaned back from the counter. “He ask?”

She gave him a look that answered before she did.

Dante Mercer asked Camille for things. He asked Rhea too, even when both of them knew she’d already made up her mind. Didn’t stop him from asking though. His sons, on the other hand, received times, places, and instructions.

“What’s it about?” Silas asked.

“Don’t know.”

Silas glanced toward the duffel by the door. “Anybody else gon’ be there?”

“He didn’t say.”

Jerrick’s route token had a Dawnveil stamp. Shrouds had closed the north line before the match ended. Now Dante wanted him in the office by noon.

Jude needed to hurry up.

Silas pushed his bowl away.

Camille turned the crossword around and slid it across the counter. “Eight down.”

He read the clue.

A formal summons. Four letters.

“Writ.”

“I know.”

Silas looked up. “Then why you ain’t fill it in?”

“Was waiting on you.”

Four empty boxes sat beneath the clue.

“I’ll be there,” he said.

“As if you had a choice.” Camille chuckled. “Hurry up. Stew’s getting cold.”

Silas took the pencil and filled in the first three letters.

The point snapped against the final T.

# CHAPTER 2: ISOLA

Tevin had said he wasn’t about to do shit six times, which gave Isola maybe four minutes before he did something.

She kept her eyes on the papers spread across the dining table.

Three work chits. Two letters from Micah’s foreman. One month of clean attendance, minus the morning he’d shown up late and convinced the man not to write it down. Danika wanted proof before she’d even consider sponsoring him into Ironcross. Proof Micah could keep a job, follow a curfew, and go thirty days without letting somebody else’s problem become his.

Today made thirty.

If Tevin went back out that door, Micah wouldn’t make it to thirty-one.

“I’m telling you,” Tevin said, pacing back and forth. “Something don’t feel right.”

He stood near the front window, both hands buried in the pouch of a brown hoodie two sizes too big for him. He wasn’t a small man. Six-one and solid, with the shoulders of somebody who’d grown up carrying coal crates before he was old enough to be paid for it. The hoodie made him look wider, but Isola knew she couldn’t say that or he’d throw a tantrum she didn’t have time for today. He’d retwisted the front of his locs too. The rest hung loose around his face, but those first two rows were neat, oiled, and parted clean enough to expose the little scar at his hairline.

“Keep your mouth closed next time.” Micah coughed into his fist from the couch. “Pacing aint gone help shit.”

Tevin cut his eyes toward him. “Shut up.”

Isola returned to the sponsorship packet before either one of them saw her mouth twitch.

The Wraithmoor front room was long and narrow, built into the same red-brown stone as the rest of their row. Omari had reinforced the walls with dark metal panels years ago, back when raids on the west block kept ending with doors kicked off their hinges. Every panel fit so cleanly against the stone that most people didn’t realize the house could seal itself until it already had.

The front window was thick whisperglass covered by black security mesh. It gave them a crooked view of the lane outside, where morning had arrived without anything resembling sunlight.

Ashvein didn’t have a sky.

It had work bells.

The first had sounded two hours ago, waking the miners and the children responsible for packing their food. The second sent the freight lifts groaning toward Lowtorch. By the third, the market stalls were open, the gambling boards were taking names for fights happening below them in Shadowmere, and coal smoke had settled over the streets in a dark film.

Torchlight flickered against the cavern walls beyond the window. Coded lanterns hung above every door. Amber for safe. Blue for watched. Black for a house in mourning.

The Wraithmoor lantern burned amber.

For now.

Isola pressed her thumb against the final work chit. The noon courier would carry the packet to Ironcross for twelve Veilmarks. Fourteen if the route inspectors were out. She’d counted the money twice before Tevin came in mad, and she’d have to count it again once he left.

After that, she wanted a few hours where nobody needed her.

She didn’t have plans for the day and that was the plan. Micah’s packet would be gone, Tevin could stay out of trouble for one afternoon, and whatever Selah had written on Mother Rae’s order slip could wait. Isola wanted to shut her bedroom door, eat something before it went cold, and hear her own thoughts without somebody calling her name from another room.

Tevin passed the table again. “You heard what he said?”

Isola kept the work chits lined beneath her palm. “I heard you the first time you told us.”

“And you cool with it?”

“I ain’t cool with nothing before breakfast.”

“Lo.”

She finally looked at him. Tevin had stopped pacing, which meant he’d moved past talking himself out of trouble and was waiting on her to give him permission to find some.

“What you want me to say?”

“That he can’t talk about Dani like that.” Tevin’s jaw tightened. “Nigga called her somebody’s kept bitch in front of everybody.”

Micah stopped smiling.

The pipes knocked behind the wall. Somewhere above the block, a drill cut into the cavern rock, sending a low vibration through the ceiling. Dust shook loose near the vent and drifted into the thin beam of amber light above the stove.

Isola folded the packet closed. “Does he know Danika?”

Tevin frowned. “He ain't got to.”

Isola frowned. “Then why the hell you about to fight him over a story he made up?”

“He said it in front of everybody.”

“And if you put hands on him, they gon’ repeat it in front of more.”

Tevin pushed away from the window. “Let ’em.”

“You think this about your pride?”

“It’s about family.”

“No. It’s about you being mad and wanting a holy reason to hit somebody.”

Tevin planted both hands on the edge of the table. A pale scar crossed his left knuckle. He’d been carrying that scar for years and had given the family three different stories about how he got it.

The back room latch clicked. Her father hadn’t stepped out yet, but he was listening now. Omari let people make choices first. He got involved after that.

Tevin heard the latch too. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped.

“So I’m supposed to let him disrespect us?”

“You supposed to think.” Micah sighed. “What good does it make if we can’t find your ass this time around?”

Tevin dragged a hand over his mouth. “I handle that boy, the rest of ’em learn not to try us.”

“The rest of ’em learn exactly how to move you.” Isola leaned back in her chair. “Say Dani name, you jump. Mention the money, you jump higher. Next time they want you somewhere, all they gotta do is run they mouth and wait.”

Isola had known Tevin long enough to catch the moment anger made room for sense. The trouble was getting sense to stay. He’d been her first real friend before either of them knew friendship and blood weren’t the same thing. He’d followed her through a collapsed mining path because she dared him and, years later, carried her three blocks after she twisted her ankle while complaining the whole way.

Tevin loved his people loud and Ashvein always heard him coming.

He held her stare for a few seconds before his eyes dropped. “I can’t let it slide.”

“You can and you will.”

His jaw moved once. “I hear you.”

A lie. He was going out the first chance he got.

If he left, Isola could send word to the aunties near the east gate before he got far. Taking his knife wouldn’t stop him. Tevin would borrow Micah’s clothes and find something else sharp enough to carry.

“Go be cool somewhere I can’t see you.” Isola waved her hand, still refusing to give Tevin the eye contact he was looking for.

Tevin snatched his coat from the back of the chair.

“Leave that.”

He turned. “For what?”

“You don’t need a route coat in the bedroom.”

His attention moved from the coat to Isola. Whatever argument crossed his mind must not have been worth Omari hearing from the back room because Tevin dropped it and walked down the hall.

The spare-room door shut hard enough to rattle the framed names beside it.

Four generations of Wraithmoors covered that wall in handwritten chains. Given names, mother-line names, godbindings, block names. Some were stitched in funeral thread. Others had been pressed into metal by Omari’s grandfather. Ashvein families had been keeping their own records longer than Isola had been alive because trusting the Synod to remember somebody was an easy way to lose them.

The Wraithmoors went farther.

Their names answered.

The portrait beneath the chains had tilted from Tevin slamming the door. Isola stood to straighten it and stopped beside Micah’s section of the wall. His household copy of the Ironcross packet was folded behind the narrow strip bearing his name. The official papers would leave with the noon courier. The copy stayed here.

“Kai.”

Micah looked over from the couch with a smile on his face. “It’s time?”

Isola nodded. “Hold still.”

Isola pressed two fingers to the metal.

*Micah Amari Wraithmoor.*

She spoke his full name in the cadence Omari had taught them, keeping the rhythm low and even. The words themselves weren’t anything special. Whether she said them right or not was the difference.

Warmth gathered beneath her fingers. The funeral thread wrapped through Micah’s name pulled taut for a breath before settling again. A brief pressure crossed her palm.

It held.

Isola tucked the retained copy more securely behind the strip and straightened the portrait.

Micah had already started getting up by the time she turned around.

He was taller than Isola and leaner than Tevin, built quick instead of heavy. His short locs had been cleaned up around the sides, and the white shirt beneath his lightweight jacket made him look innocent enough to fool somebody who hadn’t raised him. His eyes did the worst damage. Wide, dark, expressive. They could turn apologetic before Micah had even decided what he was sorry for.

Isola had watched him learn how to lie. He still wasn’t good at it.

She pressed one hand against his chest before he reached the hallway. “Stay out of his shit, Micah.”

Micah leaned against the couch instead of sitting back down. “Who said I was following Tevin?”

“You did when you put your shoes on.”

His eyes dropped toward them before he could stop himself.

Isola wiggled her fingers. Micah sucked his teeth, pulled the phone from his pocket, and slapped it into her palm. “This some bullshit.”

She set it on the table beside the Ironcross packet. “Whatever you say. Come. We need to help clear space for breakfast.”

Micah was half way into the dining room section of the front room when the back door opened. Omari stepped into the hall wearing a charcoal shirt, dark work pants, and the watch he’d owned longer than Isola had been alive. The thing lost eleven minutes every day but that ain’t stop him from winding it up every morning. His heavy keyring hung from one belt loop, carrying enough house, storage, and route keys to open half the block.

His eyes moved over Micah, the sealed packet, then the names on the wall.

“Everything hold?”

“Yeah,” Isola said.

Omari nodded once. “Problem?”

“Handled.”

“Good.”

He crossed the front room and checked the deadbolt. Once. Twice. His fingers lingered near the upper lock before he let it go and disappeared into the back room again.

Micah waited until the door closed. “He scary for no reason.”

Isola tucked the courier envelope beneath her arm and headed into the kitchen before he found another reason to get up.

The room smelled like scorched coffee, smoked roots, and the protective incense Selah lit every morning near the rear threshold. Bunches of dried eucalyptus and nightroot hung above the sink. Small packets of salt had been wedged into the corners of every window. A kitchen knife with a dark wooden handle rested beside the cutting board, sharpened so clean the edge disappeared beneath the light.

Her mother stood over the counter with a rag clenched in one hand, humming beneath her breath.

The same six notes, over and over.

Selah wore a long cream house dress beneath a brown apron. Her locs were wrapped in dark fabric tied the same way she’d tied it for as long as Isola could remember. The wrap exposed her round face, watchful eyes, and the protective charm resting at the base of her throat. She looked like an older version of Isola with broad hips and heavy arms that had carried babies, supply crates, injured neighbors, and once a grown man who’d gotten himself stabbed outside their door.

Something moved across her face that he couldn’t name and didn’t try to. Silas stepped away before she could say something else he didn’t want to hear. Behind him, the lock turned, then the thread drew tight against the frame. He waited until it went still.

Silas lowered himself onto the bottom step with his back to the door and his right hand loose near his knife. Rain dripped off the awning in a steady line. He kept his bad arm close to his ribs and watched the street.

At least last night, he’d known what he was fighting.

# CHAPTER 4: ISOLA

Isola didn’t like Shadowmere.

Nobody at home did, so she’d never had to explain it. If somebody made her, she’d have said this: in Ashvein, crews wore their colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were standing on before your feet hit it, and if you didn’t, somebody’s auntie would lean out a window, tell you, and then tell your mama you needed telling.

Shadowmere didn’t have aunties in windows. It had neon and violet mist and rain falling out of solid rock, which was ridiculous and a real nice reminder of exactly how deep underground they all were. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

*Running errands or something*, Selah had said about Asha this morning.

Errands. Sure.

Isola had a fake coin in her skirt that said otherwise. Whatever Asha was running, it ran through here, and before Isola did one thing for Rae, she was going to find out exactly what her cousin had left lying in that bag.

The assayer kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain couldn’t get at her scales. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Rae, and watched the woman weigh it on a brass scale with weights the size of baby teeth. She could weigh anything. Scrapings, filings, coins that had been through a fire. She’d put on her loupes and tell you what it was worth, and in all the time Isola had watched, she’d never once asked a single soul where anything came from.

Which was the whole point of coming here.

The woman was busy telling a man in a dock coat that his silver was mostly lead.

“It ain’t lead,” he said.

“It’s lead.”

“My uncle—”“Your uncle sold you lead.”

He argued. He lost. He left.

Isola stepped up, laid her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody walking by could see more than a corner. The assayer looked at the coin. She didn’t pick it up or reach for her loupes. She put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it.

“I don’t weigh High Heresy.”

At the next counter, the man selling charm wire stopped talking mid-sentence. His customer stopped listening mid-word.

“Get it off my counter.”

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

Oh. So she *was* fucked.

She made herself walk away slow. Hurrying in the Back Market was the same as climbing on a crate and yelling follow me. Her face was on fire and her heart was going like a work bell, but her feet strolled like she had all day. She’d known what it was since Asha’s room. But knowing a thing alone in your cousin’s bedroom and hearing a woman who never asked questions say it out loud in front of witnesses were two very different things.

High Heresy meant the square.

Isola had been eight the first time she’d witnessed it. A woman from the mining district was forced onto her knees in front of everybody, and the Synod took her breath slowly, so the whole square could watch. Her fingers scraped at the stones long after scraping could help her. Selah had clamped a hand on Isola’s shoulder so she couldn’t turn away, because Selah believed you looked at what the Synod did so you’d never be stupid enough to forget what it cost.

For one second, stepping out into the rain, the fingers on those stones were Asha’s.  
Isola stopped walking. She stood in the drip off the arch and let the rain hit her face until her stomach climbed back down out of her throat. The salt was the reason she was down here, and the only thing on her list today she actually knew how to do right.

To do it, she had to find Pell.

\#

His stall sat in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned out into thread sellers and the counters ate half the walking room. He sat under his heat lamp with three braids in his beard resting on his belly and a ring on every finger catching the amber light whenever he pointed at somebody’s purse. He also had a mouth that never shut, which was usually the only annoying thing about him.

Usually.

Rae bought her funeral grade from Pell because he kept it dry and didn’t cut it. Selah had made that crystal clear the first time she sent Isola down alone, and even clearer the day Isola came home and admitted what he’d charged her. Pell had a Shadowmere price and an Ashvein price, and he’d never once been embarrassed about either. By the time she reached his counter, he should’ve asked after Selah, complained about his knees, and tried to sell her something she hadn’t come for.

He didn’t ask about Selah.

“Rae’s girl.” He was already reaching. “What she need?”

Isola put the slip down. He read it, nodded, and stuck his scoop in the open barrel at his elbow.

She watched it come up. Grey. Coarse. Clumped where the damp had gotten into it, with dark grit caught between the grains. She’d been packing salt into Selah’s windowsills since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them, and she’d known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name: fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it had somewhere better to be.

Isola frowned. “That ain’t funeral grade.”

Pell didn’t look up. “It’s what I got today.”

“It’s quarry salt, and it’s wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop.” She put her hand flat on the slip. “Rae’s slip say funeral grade.”

“Rae can come get it herself.”

Isola blinked.

Pell would talk your ear off about the weather in a tier that didn’t have any. He’d been selling Rae salt since before Isola could walk. He did not send Rae’s girl home with wet quarry salt and a smart mouth.

*Something’s wrong.*

She pulled Rae’s cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he’d have to look at it. “Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today.”

Pell stopped scooping and looked past her. Pell’s face went as blank as a wall. Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end. The other stood right next to her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word.

Pell reached under the counter and pulled out an envelope. He set it down, and the man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking. The one at the end of the counter looked at Isola’s hand. The one still holding Rae’s token out in the open like a damn fool.

He kept looking. Long enough that she felt it crawl up her arm and settle on the back of her neck. She closed her fist over the brass. He followed his friend out of the stall, and the two of them strolled up the lane toward the bend like they had all day. Pell didn’t unlock the drawer for a long time after that. When he finally did, his hands were shaking so bad the key took two tries. Isola tucked the cord back under her shirt. *Nobody tells you who owns the street until you owe it something.*

Well. Pell owed it something.

He weighed out the real stuff, and when he folded the packet, a thin stream of it slid off the scale onto the counter. He watched it and didn’t say a word.

Pell. Silent. About salt on his own counter. She’d have bet the house money on that never happening, and she’d have lost the house money, which, after the courier, was about two Veilmarks.

“Who was that?” Isola asked.

“Who?”

She waited for him to stop acting stupid.

He didn’t. “I’ma put this on Rae account.” His letters wandered all over the line as he wrote it in his book. “Eighteen. Tell her I said hey.”

Eighteen.

Eighteen was robbery, and they both knew it. Any other day she’d have stood right there and argued him down to fourteen just to watch him sweat. Today she looked at the grey grit still sitting in the brown paper he’d tried to pass her, and the whole thing came clear.

Rae can come get it herself was as close to a warning as Pell could afford.

She couldn’t prove one bit of it. She took the packet anyway and didn’t argue, and that was all the thanks he was getting. He looked up the lane toward where the men had gone, and back down at his scale like it might have something there for her.

Isola followed his look up the lane.

Asha was standing at the bend.

Isola knew her by how she stood. Weight back. Head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her. The coat was new. Dark, fitted close at the waist, falling past her knees. A tote hung off her shoulder, stiff-sided and expensive, the kind of bag that held its shape whether there was anything in it or not.

Isola had never seen either one in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Bags like that cost money. And money like that came from somewhere nobody with the last name Wraithmoor had access to.

Worst part? It looked right on her.

Asha turned her head toward the crowd noise coming through the stone. She was about to go.

*Don’t you dare—*

“Move!”

A boy came tearing down the lane, elbows out, route coat flapping. Right in front of her, a man stepped out of his way and put his entire back into her.

He didn’t knock her down. She’d been knocked into by bigger, and her feet stayed where they were. The packet, on the other hand, went flying. She watched it go, turning once in the air like it was taking its sweet time, and burst on the wet stone at her feet.

Eighteen Veils of funeral grade hit the puddle and went grey.

One bumps. One runs. You find out what’s gone when you get home. Every story she’d ever heard about the Back Market went exactly like that. Her hand was in her skirt before she finished thinking it, fingers locked around the coin so hard the edge bit.

“Damn, my bad,” the boy called over his shoulder, already past the bend.

Isola looked down at the salt. She looked up at the man.

And up.

Lord. He was tall. Tall enough that she had to tip her head all the way back to get all of him, and she was too mad to be embarrassed about it. The first thing she noticed was that somebody had hit him recently. Swelling high under one eye. A split in his bottom lip. A bandage wrapped across the knuckles of his right hand. Rain had soaked his locs and was still running off them, down past his ear and into his beard, and he wasn’t even bothering to wipe it.

“You’ve got to be fucking kidding me.” She glanced over at the puddle. “That’s eighteen Veils dissolving in that puddle.”

“Eighteen?” He glanced past her at Pell. His voice was low and unbothered, like he had all the time in the world and hadn’t just cost her her whole afternoon. “You robbing folks in daylight now?”

Pell lifted both hands, rings flashing. “Price is the price, Graves. Ashvein orders come with a fee.”

Graves.

Isola raised an eyebrow.

Something moved behind him against the freight wall. A hooded figure, small, pressed flat to the brick. She got one look before he shifted. The hood was down low, but the lamp from the next stall caught a single curl that had slipped out at the edge.

The curl was red. Red like the inside of a coal.

“Pell,” the man said, without turning around. “Bag her another one. Put it on Camille.”

Pell’s eyebrows went all the way up his forehead. “Camille know she paying for this?”

The man dug into his coat, came out with four Veilmarks, and set them on the counter one at a time so the quicksilver caught the lamp. “Bag her another one.”

Pell looked at the coins, then at the man. “Don’t got another morning cut.”

“Give her the evening cut early.”

Pell sighed like he’d been asked to haul the whole tier up the cliff stairs on his back, and reached under the counter.  
Isola stared at those coins. Pell had put her down for eighteen not five minutes ago. This man had laid down four for the same salt, and Pell hadn’t argued so much as a copper.

She and Pell were going to have words. Not today. But they were going to have them.

She found herself looking at the man again.

And realized he was fine.

The kind of fine she was sure Danika had been talking about finding when she’s moved to Ironcross for Alexi three years ago and never looked back. He stood there and let her look like he knew good and well what she was doing and didn’t mind one bit. For one long, stupid second, Isola forgot what tier she was on.

*Absolutely not. You are looking for your cousin.*

She looked past him trying to look at anything but his face. He shifted half a step to the left and put his shoulder square in her line of sight. Isola knew a block when she saw one. She’d grown up with Tevin. Whatever was against that wall, this man didn’t want her looking at it.

Her chin came up. “I ain’t ask you to pay for it. I ain’t trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere. Everyone knows how y’all get down.”

The corner of his mouth twitched. She watched the split lip stop, then watched him decide the smile was worth the sting anyway, for about half a second. “I knocked it out your hand. Debt’s mine.”

Pell slapped a fresh packet on the counter, wrapped twice in waxed paper. Isola took it with her free hand. The other one stayed locked on the coin.

“Next time,” she said, “watch where you backing up.”

“Next time, stand somewhere else.”

Isola tucked the packet under her arm, stepped around the grey puddle, and went up the lane toward the bend without looking back. She wanted to look back. She could feel the exact spot between her shoulder blades where she’d know if he was watching, but she wasn’t about to give him the satisfaction of finding out. She had a cousin to catch.  
The bend was empty. Two runners crossed it with a box between them, arguing about which door they’d been paid to use.

Behind them…nothing. Just the lane curving off toward the noise of Gutterlight.

Isola stood exactly where Asha had been standing and turned in a slow circle.

Fifteen steps. She’d been fifteen steps away, and she’d spent every one of them looking at a man’s face.  
She checked the crossing, the doorways on both sides, and the mouth of the next alley, where the drainage ran ankle-deep and stank of dye. She walked the whole length of Gutterlight’s back wall, ducking into every doorway that would let a body stand out of the rain, until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her back teeth.

By then the crowd at the bend had doubled. Asha could’ve been anywhere in it or nowhere near it.

Isola stood in the rain so mad she couldn’t pick who to aim it at.

Asha first. Pell second. The two men in dark coats third. The boy with the elbows was definitely fourth.  
And him.

None of it made her feel one bit better about being fifteen steps from her cousin and spending all fifteen looking at his face.

It was a good face, though.

\#

She paid for the lift home. The salt wasn’t heavy. She paid anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might’ve decided to follow her. Plus the lift was faster, and if she didn’t get home soon, Selah was liable to send a search party.

Eleven people stood in a cage built for eight. Somebody’s elbow pressed into her ribs the whole way up. Isola kept the packet flat against her chest and her hand over her skirt while the operator called the levels without looking up from his lever.

“Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.”

Somewhere past Service Shaft, she caught her thumb working one of her rings around and around her finger. She made herself stop.

By Ashvein West, she was doing it again.

The rain on her coat had dried to dust by the time the gate opened. Coal smoke hung low over the lane. She’d missed at least one bell, and the boys who’d been posted under the lantern across the street all morning were gone.  
Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge.

Isola stopped.

Nobody in her house left a job half done on that step. Nobody. She stared at the little pile longer than Selah would have allowed her to before she looked up.

The lantern over the door still burned amber.

*For now.*

Micah was asleep on the couch, one arm hanging off the cushion, shoes still on. Tevin’s coat hadn’t moved from the chair, and his door was still shut. She listened at it as she passed and heard him breathing on the other side, slow and even, pretending to be asleep. Badly.

Asha’s door was open. Isola didn’t go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she’d left it. If Asha had come home, she’d come and gone without touching it. Selah was at the stove. She turned around when Isola came in and held out both hands for the salt before Isola got anywhere near the counter. Selah turned the packet over and looked at the double waxed paper. “This ain’t the morning cut.”

“Evening cut. He tried me with quarry salt first.”

“And?”

“And I told him no.”

Her mother untied the paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. She nodded once, but she looked at that double wrap a good while longer than she’d looked at the salt. “You find your cousin?”  
Isola’s stomach dropped. Selah had told her this morning don’t go looking. She wasn’t asking whether Isola had looked.

She was asking whether she’d found.  
Isola reached for a rag that didn’t need reaching for. “Saw her. Lost her in the Market.”

“Lost her how?” Selah asked, still stirring whatever blend she had going.

A coat Isola couldn’t have bought with three months of courier fees. A bag to match. A coin the assayer wouldn’t touch. Two men who never said a word. The list was so damn long, all she could get out was one word.

“Crowd.”

Selah stared at her so long that Isola broke first and started wiping the counter. After a minute, her mother turned back to the stove, and the humming started up again.

“Wash up,” Selah said over it. “Food almost ready.”

Isola went to the sink and held her hands under the water, watching the red dust from the stairs swirl down the drain.

*Whatever you got into, Asha, it better not drag me back down there.*

She said it three more times while she scrubbed. It didn’t help.

She’d be back in Shadowmere before first bell, and she knew it.

#  CHAPTER 5: SILAS 

Three hours on the step, and the only thing that had tried to kill Silas was the rain.

It came off the cavern ceiling in long, steady ropes, soaked through his collar, and found every place Jerrick had hit him. His left shoulder locked up a little more every hour. Gutterlight roared somewhere behind the rooftops whenever a match ended, and in between, the street in front of the house stayed empty enough that his hand kept drifting toward his knife without being told.

He’d counted everybody who walked past. A drunk Ring fan arguing with his own shadow. Two girls in work coats sharing an umbrella with a hole in it. A boy with a cart of empty bottles who looked at Silas’s face, looked at the knife hand resting on his knee, and found a reason to cross the street. Not one of them had been worth getting up for.

His phone buzzed against his thigh.

**Remy:** You home?

**Silas:** Working.

**Remy:** Work ain’t a place.

**Silas:** It is tonight.

**Remy:** Dante?

Silas didn’t answer that. Remy knew what no answer meant. Three dots showed up and went away twice before the next message came through.

**Remy:** Eat something. I’m bringing food in the morning.

**Silas:** You don’t know where I am.

**Remy:** Lorraine do.

Naturally, Lorraine did. Silas put the phone away before he had to think about how many people in this city knew more about his night than he did.

Jude hadn’t texted at all, which meant the token was still on his bench and Jude was still bent over it with his glasses on top of his head, ignoring everybody, including God. Rhea hadn’t texted either. That was normal. She texted when she wanted to know if he was alive and went quiet when she didn’t want him knowing she cared.

Behind him, the lock turned.

He was on his feet with the knife out before Camille’s thread finished loosening. The door opened about four inches. Melena stood in the gap, hood down and her hair out of its last pins, red as a warning lamp in a dark hall. She looked at the knife and he instantly put it away.

“You said you’d sit,” she said. “Didn’t think you’d sit for three hours.”

Rain blew in across the threshold and spotted the hem of the mourning dress, and she didn’t step back from it. Down the street, the Ring went up in another roar, and her eyes cut toward the sound.

“What *is* that?”

“Gutterlight.” Silas flicked his head in the direction of the arena. “Somebody’s losing a lot of money and probably a few teeth.”

Melena learned against the doorframe. “They do this all night?”

“Till somebody stops getting up.”

“I want to say something,” she said. “Before I lose my nerve.”

“Go ahead.”

“In your father’s office. You grabbed my wrist.”

“You were falling.” Silas raised an eyebrow. “You’d rather I let you eat the floor?”

“I’d rather you asked.” Her hand found the edge of the door and held it tight. “I have one rule. Nobody touches me without asking. It’s a family thing. Something my brother always made us follow.”

Silas looked at her. “And if you’re on fire?”

She thought about it longer than he expected. “Ask quickly.”

He almost laughed. “There a two?”

“I’ll let you know,” she replied before closing the door behind her.

The lock turned, and the thread drew tight against the frame. His shoulder settled against the doorframe, and he closed his eyes for exactly one breath. Pell’s lane came back before he could stop it.

*I ain’t trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere.*

Most people who got knocked into by a Graves in Gutterlight said sorry first and figured out why later.

She’d cussed him out and turned down his money.

He hadn’t stopped thinking about it all night. It was a problem, and he didn’t have time for problems with silver locs and a smart mouth. He had a girl from seven tiers up behind a door with rules, and a shoulder that wasn’t going to be worth a damn by morning.

An hour later, the step was warm and Silas figured he’d fallen asleep at some point.

He sat up off the doorframe. It wasn’t his body heat. He’d been sitting on cold stone for four hours, and it had stayed cold the whole time. Now it was warm under his thighs and getting warmer. A thin skin of steam was lifting off the wet stone around his boots.

Behind him, Camille’s thread snapped.

He felt it in his back teeth, a hard twang like a bass string going all at once. He was on his feet with the token out before he’d decided to stand. The lock plate took the brass. The ward checked him, slower than it should’ve, like something had already been through it and left it confused.

Smoke hit him in the face when the door opened.

The front room was empty. The cot was untouched. Down the hall, the back-room door hung open six inches, and where the lock used to be, a lump of brass the size of his thumb still glowed at the edges and dripped down the wood in a bright little line.

“Melena.”

Nothing.

He shoved the door open the rest of the way. The back room was exactly what Camille had built it to be: four walls, a bed, a lamp, and no windows. That was the point. No windows meant nobody could look in. It also meant the only other way out was the Mercer service door in the back wall, and a double line of funeral thread sewn across the seam.

The thread was ash. The lock had run down the iron like candle wax. The door stood open to the back lane, and rain was blowing in across the floor and hissing where it hit.

*Dante’s gonna kill me.*

He could hear it already. *I gave you one job, son.* And Camille wouldn’t say anything at all.

Silas ran a hand across his face before he could think about what Mother Rae was going to say.

\#

She wasn’t hard to follow.

Rain hit everything in Shadowmere and ran off it, but it didn’t run off the places she’d touched. On the brick at the mouth of the back lane, two handprints steamed like somebody had pressed hot irons there. Ten steps on, a drainpipe had a black scorch shaped like a palm. Farther down, in a puddle, the water had gone warm enough to smoke.

She’d gone left at the dye vats, away from the Ring. Toward the part of Gutterlight where the street widened and you could actually look up and see the cavern ceiling through the mist.

She was looking for sky. Same as in the alley outside Dante’s.

“Damn it,” Silas said, and ran.

His shoulder screamed every time his left foot hit the stone. He held his arm against his ribs and kept going, past the shuttered pawn counter, past the tailor row, through a gap between two buildings so narrow his coat dragged both walls. A woman leaning out a window above him yelled something about his mama. He didn’t stop to answer.

He came out onto Lamp Street and the noise went away.

One second there was rain hitting tin and a tram grinding somewhere south and the Ring roaring behind him, and the next there was nothing. The rain kept falling. He could see it coming down in the lamplight and bouncing off the stone, and it didn’t make a sound. His own boots landed silently. His breath went in and out of him without a single noise he could hear.

The lamps on Lamp Street dimmed to blue.

Everybody on the street knew what that meant, and everybody moved. A man selling skewers pulled his cart into a doorway. Two kids who’d been splashing in the gutter disappeared like somebody had erased them. A woman yanked her shutters closed without a sound. In ten seconds, the whole street was empty except for the rain.

And Melena.

She stood in the middle of the street, face up and  hood down. Rain ran through her hair, and Silas could have *sworn* her hands were lit. She wasn’t on fire yet, but the black under her nails had climbed to her knuckles, and a dull orange glow was coming through the skin of her palms like a lamp behind a curtain.

At the far end of Lamp Street, the Shrouds came around the corner.

There were two of them. Taller than him, in layered grey veils that hung to the ground and didn’t move right in the wind. Bone marks stitched down the front of them. Strips of paper hung from their heads and shoulders, hundreds of them, fluttering, and every strip had writing on it. Names. Silas had seen Shrouds before from behind a door through a crack. He’d never once wanted to see one closer.

They moved down the street without walking, veils never touching the ground.

Melena hadn’t seen a damn thing.

Silas had about four seconds to think about her rule.

He couldn’t ask. Nothing anybody said under Silence came out, and even if it did, the Shrouds would hear it before she did. He couldn’t call her name. He couldn’t do anything at all except what he was about to do. She was going to hate him for it, and he was going to do it anyway.

He crossed the street in six silent strides and took her.

His right arm went around her waist. He pulled her backward off her feet and into the recessed doorway of a closed threshold shop, and she fought him the whole way. She was stronger than she looked. She drove her elbow back at his ribs and found exactly the spot Jerrick had found. His vision went white for a second but be managed to hold on. Her hands were the problem. They glowed brighter now, the light spilling across the wet stone in front of the doorway like a sign that said *here*.

Silas grabbed both of her wrists in his left hand and pressed her palms flat against his own chest, under his coat, and closed the coat over them.

It burned.

It fucking *burned*.

It felt like grabbing a pot handle you forgot was on the stove, except you couldn’t let go. The heat went through his shirt and into his palm where it was clamped over the backs of her hands. He bit down on the inside of his cheek and didn’t move. Melena had gone still against him. Her back was pressed to his chest. Her pulse was going so hard he could feel it through her wrists, through his own burned hand.

The Shrouds passed the doorway.

Silas could see them over the top of her head. Up close, the name strips weren’t paper. They were thinner than paper and moved like they were breathing. One Shroud stopped. It turned its veiled head toward the threshold shop. Silas watched the name strips across its face lift and settle like something under there was tasting the air.

Melena’s hands got hotter.

He pressed them harder against his chest and put his mouth next to her ear even though she couldn’t hear him, and breathed out long. Longer than he breathed in. Same as at Pell’s. *Count it.*

He didn’t know if she felt it. He did it again anyway.

On the third breath, the heat dropped. Not all the way. Just enough that the glow under the coat went from orange to red to almost nothing.

The Shroud turned its head away and moved on.

He held her there until both of them were gone around the far corner. The Silence seal dragged after them, grey markings withdrawing across the puddles one line at a time. The lamps on Lamp Street went from blue back to amber, and the sound came back all at once like somebody had kicked a door open. Rain on tin. A tram. The Ring, roaring for somebody who’d just gotten hit in the face. Somewhere above them, a shutter opened and a woman said, “Lord have mercy,” to nobody.

Silas let go.

Melena turned around and shoved him hard enough that his back hit the door behind him. “What the fuck? I said ask—”

“Under Silence?” He held up his left hand. The palm was red and shining, a blister already lifting across the middle of it where the backs of her hands had been. “Nothing you say comes out under Silence.”

She looked at his hand.

He watched the fight drain from her face, fast, and something worse come in its place. She looked at the blister like she’d been the one holding the pot.

“I didn’t—” she started. “I didn’t mean to—”

“I know.” He closed the hand and put it in his coat. “You can be mad at me later. Right now, I need you to tell me why you burned through two Mercer locks and a Graves ward to come stand in the middle of the street.”

Her chin came up. A second later, it dropped. She looked past him, back up at the ceiling of the cavern, where the rain came out of the rock and the mist glowed violet and there wasn’t anything else up there at all.

“There are no windows,” she said.

Silas waited.

“In that room. There are no windows.” Her voice cracked. “I tried to lay down and I couldn’t — I couldn’t breathe. It’s a box. You put me in a box and you sat on the lid.”

He thought about the back room. Camille had built it like that on purpose, for people running from things. Those people usually just wanted walls. Silas hadn’t thought about her needing more than a window.

# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Isola had never asked anybody for help in her life, and she’d been practicing how to do it since four in the morning.

She’d practiced in bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to Asha not make a sound on the other side of the wall. She’d practiced in the kitchen, where Selah was already at the stove before first bell, humming into a pot of grits and pointedly not asking where Isola was going with her coat on. She’d practiced down the cliff stairs in the dark, across the chasm bridge and through the back lanes of Shadowmere with the coin riding her hip like a rock in her shoe. Every version came out sounding like Micah asking for money.

By the time she reached the bottom of Rae’s stairs, she’d given up. She’d walk in, put the coin on the table, and let it do the talking. It had already made an assayer shut her scale case in front of witnesses. It could handle her godbrother.

Mother Rae’s house sat behind a chandlery on the east lanes, at the top of a crooked iron stair that rang under your boots no matter how soft you walked. Rae liked it that way. Three small silver bells hung over the door, the same kind she wore on her wrist, and in all the years Isola had been climbing these stairs, she’d never once heard them ring.

The door was unlocked, like always.

The front room smelled like candle wax, salt, and the bitter orange peel Rae burned in a dish by the window when she wanted the air to behave. Everything sat where it had sat for twenty years: the black velvet chair nobody touched but Rae, the shelves of unlabeled jars, the long table under the window where Isola had done her letters as a girl while Rae worked route maps at the other end and never explained a single line. The only light came from the kitchen, along with somebody singing a tram-speaker song from last summer, off key and with most of the words wrong.

Isola leaned in the kitchen doorway and let him finish the verse, because some things were too good to interrupt.

Remy stood at the stove with his locs piled on top of his head and a dish towel over one shoulder, frying dough like it had insulted his mama. His green coat hung off a chair. Three paper sacks were already lined up on the counter, and a pot of eggs was boiling over on the back burner. He went for a high note and missed it so badly that a cat somewhere outside yelled back.

“That ain’t how it goes,” Isola said.

Remy jumped so hard he nearly put his hand in the oil, spun around with a fork raised like a knife, and lowered it when he saw her face. “*General.*” He slapped the towel against his chest. “You can’t be walking up on folks before first bell. Ma’s bells ain’t even move.”

“I came up the stairs like a normal person.” She pointed past him at the pan. “You burning your dough.”

He swore and turned back around.

Remy didn’t let anybody stand in his kitchen without feeding them, and arguing only meant he’d put the plate in front of you and stand over it until you ate out of embarrassment. Isola knew better. She sat down at the little kitchen table and let him set her up with fried dough, a dish of honey, a boiled egg, and tea he made without asking how she took it. Two sugars, no milk.

He went back to the stove while she ate, packing the sacks as fast as the dough came out of the oil. Eggs went in one, dough in another. Two flasks, one tea and one coffee. A jar of something orange. “Who’s all that for at first bell?” she asked.

The lamp over the table flickered. The thread on the doorframe started to hum, high, like a kettle. Silas started to stand, but his palm screamed at him, and he stopped halfway because he didn’t know what he’d do once he was up. He’d broken her rule once, and he’d seen her face after.

Remy reached into his coat. He came out with one of his mints, the cheap kind in silver wrappers he’d carried around since they were nine, and held it up between two fingers in the lamplight, out to the side, where she’d have to turn her head to see it.

“Hey,” he said. “What color is this?”

Melena turned her head. She looked at him like he was out of his mind. Silas had wondered about that himself more than once.

“Humor me,” Remy said. “What color?”

Her mouth worked before it found the word. “Silver.”

“Good. What color’s my coat?” He plucked at the wet sleeve.

She looked at it. “Green.”

“Ugly green. You can say it. What color’s his shirt?” He tipped the mint toward Silas, and her eyes followed it there and back.

“Black,” she said.

“Where are you right now?”

Her breath hitched. “A kitchen. In Shadowmere.”

“Good. Who’s in the kitchen with you?”

“Maven.” Her eyes went around the table. “Mrs. Graves. Silas.” They came back to him. “You. I don’t know your name.”

“Remy.” He set the mint on the floor between them and left it there. “Put your heels down for me. Flat. Press into the floor like you’re trying to push it down a tier.”

She did. Under the table, Silas watched Rhea’s old boots press into the boards.

Remy tapped two fingers on the floor beside him. *Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.* Six uneven beats, and the same six again, like a heartbeat that couldn’t make up its mind. He breathed out while he did it and after the second round, Melena was breathing with him.

The thread on the door went quiet. The smoke off her fingers thinned and stopped.

It took four minutes. Silas counted.

He’d sat outside that door for four hours.

Silas sat back down, realizing Rae was watching her son.

“Well,” Camille said quietly. “Would you look at that.”

Rae unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table. Her bells shifted on her wrist without making a sound. “Your ward won’t hold her,” she said to Camille.

Camille pushed her glasses up. “I know my ward won’t hold her. I’m the one who sewed it.”

“Dante’s houses won’t hold her. Synod walls won’t hold her, and the Shrouds will find her before the week’s out if she keeps lighting up streets.” Rae looked at Melena. “She doesn’t need walls that hold. She needs walls that eat.”

Camille froze. “No.”

Rae turned one hand over on the table, palm up, like she was setting something down in it. “Ora’s walls have eaten more grief than this whole tier has ever cried.”

“No, Renae.” Camille stood up so fast the crossword slid off the table. “You are not taking that child into a Hush den.”

“I’m not taking her to the den. I’m taking her to the walls.” Rae shot Camille a look. “Every line of thread in that bathhouse is grief somebody paid to get rid of. Sewn tight, three hundred lines deep. Whatever’s burning in her, it’s eating what she feels. You put her inside walls that are hungrier than she is, and it’ll go quiet.” She paused. “And if it doesn’t, Ora has other things.”

“Hush,” Camille said. The word came out like she’d spat it.

“One drop. Ora measures it herself.” Rae finally turned her head and looked at Silas. “You could have handled her yourself, but you let your Mama call me. You know good as I do that she needs something more than what anyone in this room can offer her.”

Silas felt heat crawl up his neck, but he didn’t say anything. Melena was staring at the table again, eyes unfocused as if she’d already been hollowed out. But he could see the tension in her jaw—the part of her waiting for a decision to be made, then bracing for whatever came after.

“Maven,” Camille finally spoke up. “You think they not watching every Hush den in Shadowmere to see where you going next?” Even Remy flinched at that. He rolled the mint between his fingers and stared at the ground, as if the next words might come crawling up out of it.

“Ora’s paid up for the year.” Rae stood up. She was a small woman. Silas had never noticed before, because she’d never stood next to his mother when Camille was this angry. “You want to know what she is? You come and watch, then. That’s as much as I’ll give you tonight. It’s more than I gave Dante.”

She went to the door. Remy got up off the floor, picked up the mint, and put it in Melena’s hand without touching her, closing her fingers around it from the outside of her fist, before he followed his mother out into the rain.

The bells rang once on the step outside.

Camille stood in the middle of the kitchen with the crossword at her feet for a long time.

“Ma,” Silas said.

“Try to sleep, Silas.” She bent down and picked up the paper. “I’m gonna sit right here and listen to my thread. And in the morning I’m gonna call your daddy, and he’s gonna wish he slept in his shoes.”

\#

Silas didn’t try to sleep.

He sat on the cot in the front room with his back to the wall and his bad hand open on his knee so the burn could breathe, as he laid the last two days out in his head the way Dante laid pins on the map-room glass.

Two men by Pell’s basin who’d stopped talking. Two Shrouds coming down Lamp Street under Silence, and one of them stopping at a closed threshold shop to taste the air.

Four sets of eyes in one day, on one girl who’d been in Shadowmere less than twenty-four hours. Nobody stumbled across somebody in Gutterlight that fast. Somebody had been waiting on her before she ever came down the stairs from Halflight.

And Rae had walked into this kitchen and asked for one more night of trust and handed out nothing to earn it.

Down the hall, Melena slept with the back-room door open and the lamp on. Camille had told her to leave it open. Melena had looked at the door, then at Camille, and said *thank you* like it was a foreign phrase in her mouth. In the kitchen, Camille sat with her crossword and didn’t fill in a single square. Every so often the thread on the frame hummed, and every time it did, her pencil stopped.

An hour before first bell, Silas got up and put his coat on.

Camille didn’t look up. “Where you going?”

“Pell’s.” He shrugged his coat straight over the bad shoulder.

She filled in a square, finally, and didn’t look up from it. “Pell don’t open till second bell.”

“He’ll open.” Silas checked the knife at his hip with his good hand. “Lock it behind me.”

\#

Shadowmere didn’t sleep so much as it changed shifts.

The rain had thinned to a drizzle that hung in the violet mist instead of falling through it. The Ring was dark. The last of the fight crowd was coming out of the tram tunnels in twos and threes, coats open, arguing about money. Down on the corner of Lamp Street, the dice game that ran under the tailor’s awning every night was folding up, and the man who’d been holding the bank all night saw Silas coming and lifted two fingers off his cup without saying anything. Silas lifted two back.

The coffee cart at the tram stop was already steaming. Old Booker ran it, the same as he had since Silas was small enough to ride on Dante’s shoulders to the Mercer building. He poured and capped a cup before Silas reached the cart.

“Graves.” Booker set it on the counter. He looked at the burned hand, then at Silas’s face, then back down at the cup like neither was his business. “Long night.”

“Long week.” Silas put a Veil on the counter.

Booker slid it back across. “The runner that lived, that’s my sister’s grandson. He around the corner.”

Silas hadn’t known that. He picked the coin up, put it in Booker’s tip jar instead, and took the coffee. Booker let him.

The runner he wanted was asleep in the doorway of the closed pawn counter where he slept most nights, curled up in a brown route coat two sizes too big with a burn mark across the front where somebody had tried to cut the block seal off. Silas had seen him at the Ring the night of Jerrick, collecting side bets in a bucket with his hands still shaking. Fourteen, maybe. Fifteen at the most. The same age as the kid nobody had found yet.

Silas crouched down and set the coffee next to his head. The boy woke up all at once, already half on his feet with his back against the door.

“Easy.” Silas didn’t move. “What’s your name?”

The kid looked at the coffee, then at Silas’s face. “Teo.”

Silas nodded at the burned seal on his coat. “You run, Teo?”

Teo’s eyes went to the coffee again. “For who’s paying.”

“I’m paying.” Silas took out three Veils and laid them on the step one at a time so the quicksilver caught what light there was. “Anybody asks about me and a girl, you remember their face.”

Teo was staring at the coins. “And then what?”

“And then you come find me at the dark house past the pawn counter. You don’t knock. You sit on the step.” Silas stood. “Three now. Three more when you bring me something real.”

Teo’s hand went over the coins before Silas finished the sentence. He looked up at Silas with his chin out, the way kids did when they were trying to decide whether something was a trap.

“You the one who did Jerrick,” he said.

Silas held his eyes. “I am.”

The kid nodded once, slow, and put the coins inside his coat. “He had it coming.”

Silas didn’t say anything. He left the coffee where it was and walked toward the market.

\#

Pell was already behind his counter when Silas came down the lane behind the freight sheds. He wasn’t open. The heat lamp was off and the barrels were still covered. He was sitting on his stool with his rings off, lined up on the counter in front of him, rubbing his knuckles. When he saw Silas, he closed his eyes like a man who’d known this was coming.

“We ain’t open, Graves,” he said, without opening his eyes.

“I know.” Silas leaned his good forearm on the counter. “Two men by the basin yesterday. Right before I knocked that girl’s salt out her hand. One of them was looking at the hood behind me.”

Pell opened his eyes and went back to rubbing his knuckles. “Lotta people looking at a lotta things in my lane.”

“These two stopped talking to do it.” Silas didn’t move his arm off the counter.

Pell picked up one of his rings and put it back down. He looked up the lane toward the bend, toward the empty place where the basin caught water off the awning. His hands had started to shake. Silas watched them. He’d never in his life seen Pell’s hands shake.

“They come Tuesdays and Fridays,” Pell said finally, low. “I give them an envelope. They don’t say nothing, I don’t say nothing. Been six months.”

Silas watched the rings on the counter instead of Pell’s face. “Who’s the envelope for?”

“If I knew that, I’d be charging them for it.” Pell rubbed his knuckles harder. “They don’t come for salt.”

Silas waited.

“The hood.” Pell lowered his voice even further. “And Rae’s girl.”

Silas went still.

“The salt girl,” Pell said. “Had Rae’s token out on a cord, holding it up in my face ’cause I gave her the quarry cut first.” He shook his head. “I was trying to get her gone before they walked up, and she wouldn’t go. Stubborn like her godmama. .”

Rae’s goddaughter.

The Ashvein girl was Rae’s.

*Fuck.*

“Her name,” Silas said.

Pell gave him a long look. “You want her name, ask Rae. I got enough problems.”

Silas straightened up off the counter. “She pays what I pay from now on.”

Pell’s eyebrows climbed. “Rae’s order? That’s eighteen Veils of funeral grade, I got a cost—”

“She pays what I pay.” Silas laid two Veils down next to Pell’s rings. “You got a problem with the difference, take it up with Dante.”

Pell looked at the coins and didn’t touch them. “You trying to get me killed?”

“You do good at that all by yourself,” Silas said, and walked back up the lane.

\#

The cavern lights were coming up grey for first bell when he got back to the dark house. Teo was already gone from the pawn-counter doorway. The coffee cup was gone too.

Silas sat down on the step and took out his phone.

*Silas: Where’s breakfast?*

Through the door behind him, he could hear Camille on her own phone. She wasn’t raising her voice. Then she said, “You can come or you can not come, Dante. But if you don’t come, I’m not coming home.”

The call ended. Silas didn’t go in.

Remy came around the corner from the tram stop twenty-five minutes later with three paper sacks stacked against his chest and his messenger bag banging against his hip. He looked like he hadn’t slept either. He also looked like he was about to enjoy himself, which on no sleep was a dangerous combination.

“Morning,” Remy said, and set the sacks on the step. “So. Funny story.”

“Rae’s goddaughter,” Silas said. “Sixth Ward. Salt for your mama’s order.”

Remy stopped with his hand still on the top sack. The grin left his face like somebody had pulled a plug. “Who told you?”

Silas reached past him for the top sack. “Pell.”

“*Pell*?” Remy straightened up. “I been holding that since four this morning. I had a whole thing.”

“I know you did.” Silas took the top sack and looked inside. Fried dough, still hot. “She came to your mama’s?”

“She came to see *me*.” Remy said it a little sharper than he meant to, and he heard it. He sat down on the step next to Silas with his elbows on his knees. “Ma wasn’t there.”

Silas tore a piece of dough in half and didn’t eat it. “She all right?”

Remy turned his head and looked at him.

“She’s handling something,” Remy said finally. “Family business. She asked me to help her with it, and I said I would.”

Silas waited for more. When it didn’t come, he asked for it. “What business?”

Remy looked out at the street. “Family business, Si.”

Remy had told Silas everything since they were six, including a whole lot Silas would’ve paid good money not to know. He’d never once in his life needed to be asked twice.

Silas put the dough down on the paper. “I’m starting to think it’s about to become my family’s business too.”

“How come?” Remy dug a mint out of his pocket and turned it over in his fingers. “You think it’s the same people?”

“I think Quietcasters are walking your mama’s routes, and now somebody’s watching who does your mama’s business.” Silas looked down the street toward Lamp Street, where the skewer man was setting up his cart in the drizzle. “I don’t think it’s three different people having a busy week.”

Remy didn’t say anything. He closed his fist around the mint without unwrapping it.

“Does Ma know?”

“Your mama knows everything.” Silas picked up his coffee cup, found it empty, and set it down again. “She just ain’t telling.”

Behind them, the door opened. Camille stood in the doorway in her robe, glasses on top of her head and the crossword folded under her arm. “Your daddy’s not coming,” she said to Silas. “So we’re going to Ora’s without him.”

She looked at the sacks.

“Remington. Bring the food in. That child’s gonna need to eat so the grief don’t take her.”
