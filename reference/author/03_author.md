CHAPTER 3: SILAS
Silas soaked his shoulder before leaving the apartment. It bought him enough movement to get dressed without help, which was about all the good it did. By the time he reached the Mercer building, rain had worked through his coat, and the muscle beneath it tightened again, one slow notch at a time.

He ran his hand over the three route marks cut into the stone beside the frame—Access, Passage, and Return—their edges worn smooth by the hands of people who knew this was their door. It was the only sign they gave. Most who needed to know weren’t in Shadowmere anyway.

He stepped inside one minute before noon and pulled the wet collar away from his neck. One minute early was the only kind of early that didn’t look like nerves, and the only kind of late Dante couldn’t call late.

A man stood at Lorraine’s desk with both palms flat on a route permission she hadn’t accepted. Behind him, a courier held a wardcloth package against her hip, keeping it clear of the water dripping off everybody’s coats.

“He said I could come through,” the man said to Lorraine.

“Then he can put his name on it.” Lorraine slid the permission back under his hands and held out her palm for the courier’s package. Her grey locs were wrapped high, leaving the old route marks along her neck bare. Silas had never asked about those marks. In this building, that was about as close to manners as you’d get. She checked the number tied to the cloth against her ledger, then set the package out of the man’s reach. “Move over. You’re blocking someone who handled their business before they arrived.”

The man stepped aside just far enough for Silas to pass. His eyes dropped to the bandage across Silas’s knuckles, climbed to the swelling under his eye, and went back to Lorraine. Silas couldn’t tell whether the man knew his face from seeing him fight or if he just had a staring problem.

Lorraine didn’t look up. “Mother Rae just left.”

Silas stopped wiping the rain off his face. He didn’t like being the second stop on anybody’s route, least of all hers. “She say what she wanted?”

“Now you know she don’t tell any of us a damn thing.” Lorraine turned a page in the ledger, signaling she was done with the conversation. She pointed the courier toward a broker waiting beside the inner hall, then caught the man reaching across her desk again without lifting her head. “That permission ain’t changed since I handed it back.”

Silas left her to it. A scanner chirped behind him as the broker checked the package. Farther down the hall, somebody argued through a half-open office door about a passage that had closed after payment, and the words cut off as the door shut. Silence-thread crossed the ceiling over his head, patched near the third office where the new stitching didn’t quite match the old. He’d learned years ago that pausing under it gained him nothing. Just Lorraine calling his name from the desk and a lecture about grown folks’ business.

He passed eight brokers’ offices before he arrived at Dante’s door.

“Come in.”

Silas shut the door behind him and reached for the lock. Halfway there, he felt somebody watching his hand and stopped moving instantly.

A woman sat on his father’s sofa, holding a crushed envelope against her stomach. She’d pulled her feet up under the heavy skirt of a mourning dress, though one ruined slipper showed at the hem. Black silk covered her from the wrists up to her throat. The stones sewn into her collar were finer than anything sold near Gutterlight—finer than anything he’d seen, actually. In the mess of her crimson hair, the last of her pins had mostly given up.

“Where were you this morning?” Dante asked.

His father stood behind the desk, tipping a bottle over a short glass. He’d kept his shoes on today. By noon they were usually under the desk, and his route coat would be thrown over whatever chair Silas normally sat in. The Mercer keys still hung from his belt. Dante didn’t keep his keys on when he planned to sit all afternoon. He’d either just come in or he was expecting to go back out. The woman’s presence had clearly interrupted his day.

“You know how your wife gets,” Silas said, glancing back at the woman. “She’s relentless.”

Her eyes were green enough to stay green under Dante’s dim-ass office lamps. Warm brown skin, full mouth. There was no way in hell she’d made it past Lowtorch without being noticed. She would’ve been spotted and sent to whoever made first contact. Shadowmere girls learned early to sit ready to move. She sat like nobody had ever needed her to clear the space when they walked into the room. She caught him looking and lifted her chin a little.

“Silas, this is Melena.”

Melena didn’t offer her hand. Silas wasn’t reaching for it anyway. “What happened to her shoe?”

Her mouth tightened.

Dante set the bottle down. “She walked.”

“From where?”

“Halflight.”

Silas looked at the slipper again. The sole had split near the toe, and a dark stain climbed the silk where her foot had bled into it.

Silas raised an eyebrow. “By herself?”

Melena stood before Dante could answer. The dress pulled against her knees, and she set her bad foot down carefully, as if she’d spent the last few hours learning exactly how much it would take. A dark red seal had broken across the fold of the envelope in her hands. He couldn’t make out the mark from where he stood. “You can direct your questions to me. I am standing in front of you.”

Nobody down here said *I am* when *I’m* would do. Silas looked up from her foot. “You came from Halflight by yourself?”

“Yes.”

“You get followed?”

Melena frowned. “No.”

“You check?”

Her knuckles whitened around the envelope.

Silas turned to Dante. “She got followed.”

“I did not.”

Dante took a drink before either of them could go another round. His silver ring clicked against the glass. “Rae opened the final routes, if you must know.”

If you must know was what Dante said when he was handing over the least information he could get away with. Rae had either walked the girl down herself or opened the doors ahead of her. Either way, she’d made sure she was gone before Silas could ask her anything about it. Melena let out a sharp breath, then took another, as if trying to force a rhythm that wouldn’t hold.

The incense smoke above Dante’s desk wavered as if somebody had opened a door.

Nobody had.

Heat spread through the office, too fast to have come from the lamp or the radiator under the window. Melena pulled one hand inside her sleeve. The silk darkened where her fingertips caught it, and a thin curl of smoke rose beside the mourning stones.

Everything Silas knew that could bleed heat into a room came from the space between living and dead—thanomancy, lament work. The kind of power that pulled memories from the dark. But all of that had a cold underneath, a mark you could find if you knew how to look.

This wasn’t that at all.

Silas drew his knife and stepped between her and the desk. His shoulder caught halfway through the movement, nearly knocking the blade from his grip. He kept it in his good hand and held it low, where she could see it without looking down. Held high, a knife started arguments. Held low, it only told somebody where the line they didn’t need to cross was.

“Take your hand out your sleeve,” Silas said.

Melena hesitated before dragging it free. Black lines had spread beneath her nails and over the ends of her fingers like charcoal. She stared at them, then shut her hand around the envelope again. “I’m not going to hurt anyone.”

Silas believed that she meant it. He’d also watched a lot of people mean things right up until they couldn’t help it.

“Put the knife away,” Dante said.

Silas didn’t turn. The heat worked its way under his collar. Stepping aside would put Dante in the line of whatever was happening, and he’d never be thanked for that. He stayed put. “You see her hands?”

“I see her.” Dante crossed to the wall and laid his palm flat against a seal. The shimmering in the silver doorframe resolved into a solid line. “She crossed seven tiers without burning any of them down. Give her some kind of credit, son.”

Silas counted up from Shadowmere without meaning to. Seven tiers. The number landed with the weight of a place he’d only heard about in warnings. She hadn’t just come from another tier; she’d fallen from another world.

He eased back a step. Melena’s eyes followed the knife, then went to the locked door, and another breath of heat crossed the office.

Silas scowled. “How did you get all the way here?”

“I wore a veil until Shadowmere.” Her voice stayed level. The envelope trembled between her hands. “I just listened to Ma—”

Dante picked up Rae’s bell and rang it once.

Melena gasped. The heat pulled back so sharply that Silas felt it leave his skin. Melena’s knees folded before she could catch the sofa. Silas dropped the knife and caught her wrist but her weight dragged him forward. His shoulder told him exactly what it thought, but he held her up long enough for her other hand to find the leather. Her skin was still hot. Under his thumb, her pulse hammered unevenly. He counted three unsteady beats before she found her footing.

“Let me go.” Melena yanked her wrist free, nearly hitting the floor.

Silas opened his hand as soon as she had her balance. He threw both hands up in surrender. “All right. Just thought you didn’t wanna fall on your face.”

Dante set the bell back on the desk. Silas wiped his palm on his coat and waited for Dante to explain. His father never explained things in front of the people they were about. He explained them later, in pieces, or let Silas walk around believing he already knew what happened. At the very least, there was some kind of conversation.

Instead, Dante opened the map-room door. “Come here a minute.”

Melena’s head lifted. His father stopped and slid a plate of crackers from the bar within her reach. “Sit down. Eat something.”

“I don’t need—”

“Water too.”

Silas followed his father into the private room. Through the whisperglass, he watched Melena lower herself onto the sofa. Dante shut the door, and the sound seal turned blue. The office went quiet on the other side of the glass.

His father had left the route map uncovered. Three threads shifted beneath it, drawing tight near the north line before disappearing past the edge of the frame. Remy had counted two Shrouds on the north route last night. Dante’s map had apparently counted them too. Silas braced his good arm against the table and waited while Dante pulled a black pin from a closed passage.

“You know what just happened in there, don’t you?”

“Yes.”

He waited another second. Dante fitted the pin over Back Market.

“Care to share with the class?”

“They’re watching Rae’s routes more than usual. Quietcasters been walking the edges of where Rae had her.”

Quietcasters didn’t walk anywhere for the exercise. If they were circling Rae’s routes, the Synod had a reason, or believed it did. Silas thought about asking his father if it was connected to Jerrick’s token and the fifteen-year-old nobody had found yet, but Dante paid for information by the piece, and Silas didn’t have enough to make a fair trade.

Through the glass, Melena pushed the crackers around her plate.

“So now you’re offering Rae your kindness?”

“I’m moving her into one of your mama’s houses.” Dante pressed the pin into place. A line opened beneath the glass, running south from the Mercer building toward Gutterlight. Silas knew the turn behind the tailor row before his father traced it. “You’ll take her through there.”

Silas studied the narrow stretch beside the market. At this hour, the freight crews would be backed up at the public passage, which helped. The lane behind the sheds would be full of people buying funeral goods, which didn’t. Dante was playing with the dead. “Why me?”

“Rhea’s on a contract where she doesn’t want to be found. Jude’s too busy doing whatever that boy does when he doesn’t answer the phone. You’re here son because you know the thresholds.” Dante moved another pin, closing the public approach behind the house.

Silas kept his face expressionless. “You got handlers.”

“I got men who’d mistake her being scared for permission.” His father met his eyes across the table. “You won’t.”

It was Dante’s way of saying he trusted him, and trust always came with a job. Even if the people he trusted were his own children.

Silas had moved people who fought him every step of a route and people who wouldn’t lift their heads long enough to learn where they were going. None of them had done what Melena had just done in Dante’s office. Knowing the streets wouldn’t tell him what to do if it happened halfway across one.

“Bell going with her?” he asked.

“No.” Dante went back to the map, fiddling with the pin he’d moved. “Lorraine said there are two watchers near the front.”

“Who’s looking this deep into the lanes?” Silas glanced at the closed lane. “What is it that you’re not telling me?”

Dante checked the south line once more before sighing. “I need you to keep Shadowmere from eating her while she’s too busy grieving to see its mouth open.”

Through the glass, Melena finally put one of the crackers in her mouth. She chewed slowly, staring at the paper beside her plate.

Silas sighed. He watched her chew and looked away. “When she leaving?”

“Whenever Rae gets back to her.” Dante’s hand stopped over the final pin. “She ain’t regular cargo, and she ain’t yours. You understand me?”

Silas found himself laughing. “Not an issue. I prefer the ones who aren’t actively mourning.”

“At least that’s one less thing for me to worry about.” Dante opened the map-room door. He walked over to the chair and grabbed his coat.

Melena stood and looked at Silas. “Where are we going?”

*Smart girl.* She’d already worked out which one of them would be walking with her.

“Safehouse.” He walked over and flicked his gaze to her foot. “Your heel needs wrapping first. I can do it, or you can.”

Melena looked down at the torn slipper. She held still long enough that he thought she might refuse, then lifted the hem clear of her ankle. “You may.”

He brought the medical tin from Dante’s cabinet and crouched on his good side. The slipper had rubbed the skin open above her heel, and the silk had dried into the wound. He eased it off, careful not to tear the scab away, and laid a strip of cloth under her ankle before he started wrapping. Her hand tightened in her skirt when the damp cloth touched raw skin. He stopped and waited for her foot to settle before he finished, then closed the tin and put it in his coat.

Dante set a pair of boots beside them. They were Rhea’s, from back when she still came by this office. He must have kept them in a cabinet all this time, waiting for a day she might walk in and need them. Silas said nothing. Neither did his father.

Melena watched Silas check each boot for anything that might catch on the bandage, then took them and put them on herself. They had room at the toes, but the soles held when she stood.

Dante held the coat open, and Melena slipped into it. Brown ward-thread lined the inside. It covered the silk to her calves, but her hair still showed above the collar. In this light it was the brightest thing in the room. Silas pointed. She gathered it up, and when he touched his own head, she pulled the hood over and fastened the front. She tucked the envelope inside the coat after one long look at its ruined corner.

“Walk on my left,” he said. He needed his right hand free, and his left wasn’t good for much today anyway. “If I stop, you stop. Somebody calls you, keep moving.”

“How will I know if they’re calling me?”

He glanced at the black silk showing beneath the coat. “That silk marks you. Keep the coat closed, or we don’t move.”

Melena pulled the coat lower and said nothing else.

Dante opened the south passage and put a brass token in Silas’s hand. The route mark matched the line he’d set under the glass. Silas pocketed it, then looked back at him. “Who’s got first shift?”

“You.” Dante’s eyes went to Silas’s shoulder, which he hadn’t lifted since he walked in. “Let Camille look at that when she comes.”

Dante had noticed but Silas wasn’t about to volunteer another injury for his mother to get into. He stepped into the passage and waited for Melena. She passed his father without offering her hand, and Dante closed the door behind them before she could look back.

Rain tapped against the grate over the southern exit. Silas stopped under it and checked the alley through the bars. A freight cart blocked half the street beyond. Two workers tried to turn it while a runner argued his package couldn’t wait; no one was looking their way. He brought Melena out while the cart was still moving.

She stopped under the awning and tipped her head back. He followed her gaze past the hanging walkways to the cavern roof, where water fell through the violet mist. The only light up there came from a shop sign with two dead letters. She was looking for sky. Silas had seen people from the upper tiers do it for the first time this far down, searching the rock like it was a ceiling someone would eventually open. Nobody born under stone bothered. There wasn’t anything up there worth finding anyway.

He stepped into her line of sight. “This way.”

She came with him, keeping close to the wall as the freight cart scraped past. He shortened his stride for the boots. She kept lifting the dress beneath the coat instead of letting the hem drag, and each time, black silk flashed at her ankles. At the next turn, he slowed enough for her to gather a fistful of it and hold it there.

Past the market, freight workers waited at the public passage with their route permissions out. A clerk scanned the first set and waved the cart through. Silas took Melena behind the sheds before anybody at the checkpoint could ask for her permissions.

Behind the sheds, the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods. The lane narrowed until the stall counters took up half of it. Silas knew these stalls as well as he knew his own route permissions. Half of them had sold Camille thread at some point, and the other half wished they had. Mourning thread hung in dark skeins above a basin catching water off an awning. Farther up, near the bend, a shopkeeper snapped a case of Hush shut as a boy drifted too close to the counter. Silas raised an eyebrow. Kids like that were always getting into something in Shadowmere.

The lanes were so tight with lunchtime traffic. There was no room to pass anyone without brushing coats. He kept Melena between himself and the freight wall and moved her along at a steady pace until they reached Pell’s. This wasn’t the time for niceties.

Pell sat under his heat lamp with his three beard braids resting on his belly, rings catching the amber light every time he lifted a finger to point at somebody’s purse.

“Move!”

The boy from the Hush counter came down the lane at a dead run, elbows out, route coat flapping open. Silas caught Melena by the arm and turned her into the freight wall. The step back put him straight into somebody standing at Pell’s counter.

A girl. Shorter than him, solid on her feet. She didn’t fall, but a paper packet left her hand and burst against the wet stone. Grey salt scattered across the puddles.

“Damn, my bad,” the boy said over his shoulder, already gone past the bend.

The girl looked down at the salt, then up at Silas. Two Ironcross rings sat on her fingers. She’d shoved her other hand deep into the pocket of her skirt and left it there.

“You’ve got to be fucking kidding me,” the girl said, staring at the puddle where the salt started going dark. “That’s eighteen Veils dissolving in that puddle.”

“Eighteen?” Silas glanced at Pell. “You robbing folks in daylight now?”

Pell lifted both hands, rings flashing. “Price is the price, Graves. Ashvein orders come with a fee.”

Behind Silas, the heat came back.

Not much. Just enough to warm the back of his neck through. He didn’t turn around, but he felt Melena’s breath change, the same broken rhythm she’d had in Dante’s office before the bell.

“Pell,” he said. “Bag her another one. Put it on Camille.”

Pell’s eyebrows climbed. “Camille know she paying for this?”

The lane hadn’t stopped moving around them. A woman with a basket of thread shouldered past. Two men by the basin had quit talking. One of them was looking at the hood.

Silas dug into his coat with his good hand and came out with four Veilmarks. He set them on Pell’s counter one at a time, so the quicksilver caught the lamp. “Bag her another one.”

Pell looked at the coins. Then at Silas’s face, long enough to place it. Something shifted under his beard. “Don’t got another morning cut.”

“Then give her the evening cut early.”

Pell sighed like Silas had asked him to carry the whole tier on his back, and reached beneath the counter.

Silas watched as the girl stood up. This close, he could see that her locs ran dark at the roots and silver down the lengths. A small ring sat in her nose. More silver studs climbed one ear. Her coat was Ashvein-cut, heavier at the shoulders than anything sewn down here, and red-brown dust still clung to the hem.

*Ashvein. Long way to come for salt.*

She looked at him again, and this time she took her time doing it. The swelling under his eye. The split lip. The bandaged hand. Then past him, to the freight wall.

Silas shifted half a step left and put his shoulder in her view of Melena.

“I ain’t ask you to pay for it. I ain’t trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere. Everyone knows how y'all get down.”

Silas almost respected that. “I knocked it out your hand. Debt’s mine.”

Pell slapped a fresh packet on the counter, wrapped twice in waxed paper. She took it with her free hand. The other one never came out of that skirt. “Next time, watch where you backing up.”

“Next time, stand somewhere else.”

Her mouth twitched like she wanted to curse him out but decided he wasn’t worth the breath. She tucked the packet under her arm, stepped around the dark patch on the stone, and went up the lane without looking back. Silas turned. Melena had both hands pressed flat against the freight wall, and the wet brick under her palms had started to steam.

“Look at me,” he said. “Breathe out longer than you breathe. Count it."

She took her hands off the wall and pushed them into the coat. Two dark prints stayed on the brick where the rain couldn’t reach.

The two men by the basin had gone back to talking. One of them was still looking.

Silas took her elbow with his good hand and moved.

He didn’t take the bend. He cut through the gap behind the dye stall, where the drainage ran ankle-deep and the smell kept most people out, then doubled up the stairs behind the tailor row and down the other side. Melena didn’t complain about the water. She just held the dress higher and kept her eyes on his back. At the bottom of the stairs, he stopped long enough to listen. Gutterlight was already warming up for the night. Tram brakes. Somebody laughing too loud. No sound of boots behind them.

The house sat two doors past a shuttered pawn counter, no lantern at all above the frame. Dark house. Anybody walking by would take it for empty, which was the point.

Silas pressed the brass token into the lock plate. Funeral thread ran along the threshold seam in Camille’s stitching and he felt the ward check him before the door gave. Inside smelled like eucalyptus and cold stone. Three rooms, a sofa, a bathroom that Jude loved as a kid, a stove that hadn’t been lit in months. Camille’s thread crossed every corner and ran a double line under the window. Somebody had left a folded blanket on the cot. Probably her.

Melena stopped in the doorway and turned. Up close, under the hood, her face looked younger than it had in Dante’s office. Or just tired enough to quit pretending like grief wasn’t tearing her a new one.

“Bedrooms yours.” He nodded at the back room. “Lock it from your side. Ward’ll hold.”

“This is it?” she asked.

“This is it.”

“For how long?”

“Till Rae says.” He checked the window seal, then the back wall. “Door locks from the inside. Thread on the frame keeps sound in and people out. Don’t touch it.”

“And you?”

“Outside.”

She looked at the sofa, then at him. Her hand had gone inside the coat again, to where the envelope sat. “You’re going to stand in the rain all night.”

“Sit,” he said. “I’m going to sit.”

Something moved across her face that he couldn’t name and didn’t try to. She nodded once.

He stepped out. Behind him, the lock turned, then the thread drew tight against the frame. He waited until it went still.

Silas lowered himself onto the bottom step with his back to the door and his right hand loose near his knife. Rain dripped off the awning in a steady line. He kept his bad arm close to his ribs and watched the street.

At least last night, he’d known what he was fighting.
