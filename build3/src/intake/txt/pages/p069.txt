In the same period as Judges, deliberately, and reading like its rebuttal. Four
quiet chapters about two widows and a harvest, placed immediately after the
most violent book in the collection, arguing that survival is arranged by
ordinary loyalty rather than by warlords.
W H AT    H APPE NS


A famine drives a family abroad. The men all die. A foreign daughter-in-law
refuses to leave her mother-in-law and returns with her to a town with no
income and no standing. She gleans in a eld, is noticed by its owner, and
then — on her mother-in-law's coaching — goes to him at night on a
threshing oor and asks him directly to marry her. He does. Their great-
grandson is David.
