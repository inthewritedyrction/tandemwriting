HOW TO READ A LETTER
Twenty-one of the twenty-seven books in this half are letters, and almost every doctrine any empire has built
on them depends on forgetting that. These are not treatises. They are one side of a correspondence, written
to a named group in a speci c mess, and read as if you had found a single reply in someone's desk drawer
without the original complaint.
So the discipline is simple. Before extracting any rule, ask what problem the writer is answering, whether the
instruction is addressed to a whole community or one household or one troublemaker, and whether the same
author says something dierent elsewhere. He very often does — the man who tells one congregation that
women should be silent greets women running congregations in another letter, names one an apostle, and
calls another a deacon by title.

THE   BIG   FOUR
                                      Romans — the long argument about who belongs and on what basis; carried and
                                      probably explained by a woman named as a deacon. 1 Corinthians — a congregation
                                      tearing itself apart over status, food and sex; holds the love chapter nobody reads in
                                      context. Galatians — furious, about whether outsiders must assimilate to belong.
                                      Ephesians — where the household code sits, directly under the mutual-submission line.
THE   SHORT     ONES
                                      Philippians — written from prison, unreasonably joyful, and holding a hymn about
                                      power surrendered. Colossians, 1–2 Thessalonians — early, apocalyptic, practical.
                                      Philemon — a private note about an enslaved man, and the single most argued-over
                                      page in the abolition debate.
THE   PA S TO R A L S
                                      1–2 Timothy, Titus — church management: who may teach, who may be appointed,
                                      how widows are enrolled. The most institutional documents here, the most disputed in
                                      authorship, and the source of Dominion's clergy handbook. Note the pattern: the
                                      letters scholars most doubt are the ones most concerned with hierarchy.
