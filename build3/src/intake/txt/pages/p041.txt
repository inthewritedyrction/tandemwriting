FOUR WAYS TO READ IT
All four are held by serious readers. The companion's own instinct is the rst one, and it is still an interpretive framework
rather than a fact about the text — keep it visibly contested, because in TKC the reading a character holds is a political
position.
I    ·       R E S I S TA N C E   L I T E R AT U R E      AGAINST     ROME


Written for congregations under imperial pressure, using the conventions of apocalyptic to say what could not be said plainly.
Strong internal support — seven cities, imperial cult, a city on seven hills, an economy of luxury goods described with
contempt. Most historical-critical scholarship works here.
II       ·       A   SEQUENCE          OF   FUTURE        EVENTS


Read as a program of things still to come. Widely held in popular and some confessional traditions, with a long history and
enormous cultural inuence — and a long record of speci c predictions failing, which is itself part of the material.
III          ·   A   CYC L E ,   NOT    A   TIMELINE


The visions as recurring patterns of empire, judgment, and endurance rather than a chronology. Explains the repetition —
seals, trumpets, bowls covering the same ground three times — better than a sequential reading does.
IV           ·   LITURGY         AND    VISION


The book as worship — hymns, thrones, incense, a heavenly assembly — meant to reorder what the hearer thinks is
ultimately in charge. The Eastern churches' hesitation about reading it aloud sits oddly beside this, and both facts are true.

         The beast as an imperial power and the woman on the beast as a capital city are the readings the historical evidence most
