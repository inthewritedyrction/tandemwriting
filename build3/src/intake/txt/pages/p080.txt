EZEKIEL · DANIEL
Books xxvi–xxvii · both written from inside somebody else's empire


EZEKIEL                            A deported priest with no temple to serve in, receiving visions of extravagant
forty-eight chapters               strangeness: wheels of eyes, a glory that leaves the temple and moves east with the
                                   refugees, a valley of dry bones reassembling into an army. He performs his messages
                                   instead of speaking them — lying on one side for months, cooking over dung, shaving
                                   his head — and when his wife dies he is told not to mourn publicly, as a sign.
                                   His indictment of the shepherds of Israel is one of the sharpest attacks on a ruling class
                                   in the collection: you fed yourselves, you clothed yourselves in the wool, you did not
                                   seek the lost. And he insists on individual responsibility — a child does not inherit a
                                   parent's guilt, which cuts directly against collective punishment.
                                   For TKC: the mobile God. If the glory leaves the temple and travels with the deported,
                                   then no state can hold it by holding the building.

DANIEL                             Half court stories, half apocalypse, and the whole book is about how to keep your
twelve chapters                    integrity inside a regime that owns your career. Young captives are trained for imperial
                                   service and renamed; they negotiate over food, refuse to bow to a statue, survive a
                                   furnace, and interpret the dreams the empire's own experts cannot. A king goes mad;
                                   another sees writing appear on his banquet wall and is dead by morning.
                                   The visions in the second half depict successive empires as beasts and are almost
                                   certainly written about a persecution much later than the story's setting — which is
                                   exactly how coded resistance literature works.
                                   For TKC: the manual for a character inside the Ministry. Serve competently, refuse at
                                   one precise point, and let the refusal be public.
