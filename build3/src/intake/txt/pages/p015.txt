Every translation is an argument. Somebody had to decide whether a Greek
word describing a woman in a congregation means servant or deacon, whether
an instruction is addressed to wives or to all believers reciprocally, whether a
term for a household head implies ownership, and whether an ambiguous
phrase gets resolved toward hierarchy or toward mutuality. These are not
neutral calls, and the committees that make them have theological
commitments, denominational funders, and intended markets.
For your purposes the practical method is simple: read any passage that matters
in at least two translations from dierent traditions, and when they diverge,
stop, because the divergence is the interesting part. That gap is exactly where
Dominion lives. An empire that controls the ocial edition does not need to
lie; it only needs to keep choosing the harder-edged rendering, generation after
generation, until the softer reading sounds like modern invention.
It is worth knowing that translations sit on a spectrum. Some aim at word-for-
word delity and read stiy. Some aim at thought-for-thought clarity and
smooth over diculty. Some are frank paraphrases with a strong voice. None
of these is dishonest by de nition, but the smoother the English, the more
interpretive decisions have already been made for you and hidden.
One more thing, and it belongs in the novel. Chapters and verses are not
original. They were added centuries later for citation, and they have quietly
made the whole book quotable in fragments — which is precisely the format a
state needs for slogans, wall inscriptions, and school recitation. The verse is the
unit of propaganda; the paragraph is the unit of meaning.
