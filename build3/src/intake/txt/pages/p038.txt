TWENTY-SEVEN
DOCUMENTS
Written across roughly a century by a movement inside the Roman Empire that had no state power and no established
imperial standing — though it was not powerless in every sense, and its social position varied considerably by city, decade,
and class.
THE   FIVE   KINDS      OF   DOCUMENT         I N F O R M AT I O N




 Gospels · 4                 Narrative accounts of Jesus, written decades after the events, each for a particular community with
                             particular concerns.
 Acts · 1                    The movement's spread from Jerusalem outward, written by the author of Luke as the second volume of
                             one work.
 Pauline letters · 13        Occasional correspondence to speci c congregations about speci c problems — which does not prevent
                             several of them from containing sustained theological argument. Romans is a worked case, not a memo.
 General letters · 8         Hebrews and the letters attributed to James, Peter, John, and Jude — wider audiences, more varied
                             purposes.
 Apocalypse · 1              Revelation. A genre with its own conventions, not a coded newspaper.

COMPOSITION         ORDER     IS   NOT     CANONICAL                 ORDER


Paul's letters come rst chronologically — written in the 50s, before any Gospel existed. The collection opens with the
Gospels because it is arranged as a story, not as an archive. Reading it in composition order shows a movement working out
what it believed before anyone had written down the life it was believing about.
THE   ROMAN      SET TING,       WITHOUT      F L AT T E N I N G       IT


Occupation, client kings, taxation, cruci xion as public policy, imperial cult, and a legal status for the movement that
changed by place and period — tolerated in some cities, prosecuted in others, and not systematically illegal across the empire
for most of this century. Acts shows shared property in Jerusalem speci cally; it is not a description of every congregation
everywhere, and Paul's letters show congregations with property, patrons, and households.




   For TKC the useful fact is not that the early movement was poor or illegal. It is that a religion born under an empire was later
   administered by one — and that the same texts served both.
