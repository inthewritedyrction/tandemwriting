HOUSE
Seven commitments that keep this companion honest. They are not stylistic
preferences; they are the dierence between a novel that takes religion
seriously and a novel that uses it as set dressing.

I       The text is not the Empire. Every entry keeps them on separate rules,
        because the whole engine of this novel is the gap between what a passage
        says and what an institution can make it do.
II      Contested stays contested. Where scholars and traditions genuinely
        disagree, the disagreement is printed and left standing. Dominion may
        settle a question; the companion does not.
III     No character is an allegory. Mirrors illuminate; they do not map. Every
        mirror entry ends with where the parallel breaks, and that break is
        usually where the character becomes interesting.
IV      Belief has a cost and a teacher. No character believes anything in this
        book for free. Someone taught it to them, and something is at stake in
        keeping it, and both go in the dossier.
V       Discovery outranks plan. If the research contradicts the outline, the
        research gets a hearing. The outline is a hypothesis; the companion is
        where it gets tested.
VI      Nothing enters the novel untested. Every borrowed symbol, doctrine
        and parallel has to answer one question before it earns a scene, and that
        question has its own page at the back.
VII     Possibility is not canon. Freewriting, inference, thematic association
        and research notes stay visibly marked until you deliberately approve
        them. A compelling idea is still only a possibility until it survives the
        manuscript, the worldbuilding sources, and your own decision — and
        nothing becomes canon merely because it was surprising, interesting, or
        written in ink.

That variety is not a aw and it is not an embarrassment. It is the single most

righteous prosper; Job says that is a lie told by comfortable men. Ezra says send
the foreign wives away; Ruth says a foreign woman is the great-grandmother of

to choose which side of those arguments it wanted to be the ocial one.
That choosing is your novel. Dominion does not have a Bible problem, it has a
Bible strategy, and it is the same one every scriptural empire has used: elevate
the passages about order, hierarchy and submission into the plain and
permanent word of God, and reclassify the passages about jubilee, debt release,
foreign widows, prophets who insult kings, and a messiah executed by the state
as poetry or context-speci c instruction that no longer applies. Nothing has to
be deleted. The canon stays intact and the pulpit still cites chapter and verse.
All that is required is a consistent policy about which parts are read aloud, and
to whom.
So read it as a library with a ght in it. When you meet a passage, the useful
question is never only what it says, but which side of the internal argument it is
on, who bene ts from that side winning, and what it would cost a character to
have been raised hearing only one half.

No angel delivered a table of contents. The collection you can buy in an airport
was assembled slowly, by committees and customs and arguments, over                                   MARGINALIA



centuries, and the process left visible seams that are worth knowing about
because your Empire will be running the same process on purpose.
On the Hebrew side, the Torah settled rst and earliest, then the prophetic
books, then a looser third category of writings that stayed unsettled for a long
while. Some books nearly did not make it. Esther was disputed partly because
God is never mentioned in it. Song of Songs was disputed because it is frankly
erotic and required a great deal of allegorising to become respectable.
Ecclesiastes was disputed because it is bleak enough to sound faithless. The
pattern is not random: the books that struggled were the ones hardest to use as
instruction.
On the Christian side the process was messier and more political. For the rst
few centuries there was no xed New Testament, only a widening pool of
gospels, letters and apocalypses circulating unevenly between cities. Lists were
proposed, contested, revised. The four gospels that won were not the only four
written, and Revelation barely survived the cut in some quarters while other
apocalypses were dropped entirely. By the time an emperor was sponsoring
bishops the shortlist was largely xed, but the xing had human ngerprints all
over it.
Two things follow that matter enormously for TKC. First, canonisation is an
act of power, and it is always performed by whoever currently has the authority
to convene the meeting. Second, once a canon is closed, the arguing does not
stop, it simply moves — out of the question of which books and into the
question of which verses matter. Dominion inherits a closed canon and
therefore does all of its work at the level of emphasis, translation, lectionary
and commentary. That is a quieter kind of editing, and a much harder one to
catch.

phrase gets resolved toward hierarchy or toward mutuality. These are not
neutral calls, and the committees that make them have theological
commitments, denominational funders, and intended markets.
For your purposes the practical method is simple: read any passage that matters
in at least two translations from dierent traditions, and when they diverge,
stop, because the divergence is the interesting part. That gap is exactly where
Dominion lives. An empire that controls the ocial edition does not need to
lie; it only needs to keep choosing the harder-edged rendering, generation after
generation, until the softer reading sounds like modern invention.
It is worth knowing that translations sit on a spectrum. Some aim at word-for-
word delity and read stiy. Some aim at thought-for-thought clarity and
smooth over diculty. Some are frank paraphrases with a strong voice. None
of these is dishonest by de nition, but the smoother the English, the more
interpretive decisions have already been made for you and hidden.
One more thing, and it belongs in the novel. Chapters and verses are not
original. They were added centuries later for citation, and they have quietly
made the whole book quotable in fragments — which is precisely the format a
state needs for slogans, wall inscriptions, and school recitation. The verse is the
unit of propaganda; the paragraph is the unit of meaning.

WHAT THE BIBLE
ACTUALLY IS
A library, not a book. Sixty-six documents in the Protestant collection this companion starts from, written across
roughly a thousand years, in three languages, by people who did not know they were contributing to the same
volume — and who in several cases were arguing with each other.
WHICH       BIBLE    THIS    C O M PA N I O N        USES,       AND      WHY        T H AT   SENTENCE        IS     NECESSARY       I N F O R M AT I O N



We begin with the sixty-six-book Protestant canon, because that is the Bible in the room where you were raised and the Bible
Dominion would have inherited. It is not the only Bible. Jewish tradition holds twenty-four books in three divisions and does
not arrange them as an Old Testament. Catholic Bibles include the deuterocanonical books. Orthodox collections include
those and more, and dier among themselves. The Ethiopian Orthodox canon is larger than any of them. None of this is a
technicality — the shape of the collection is itself an argument about what scripture is for.

IT   DOES    NOT     SPEAK        IN   ONE     HUMAN         VOICE


This is the correction that matters most for a novelist. The Bible contains many voices — priestly and prophetic, royal and
exilic, ecstatic and administrative — in dierent genres, from dierent centuries, addressing dierent crises. When a character
quotes scripture as though it has a single settled opinion, that character is making a choice, and the choice is worth writing
about.

HOW IT WAS
ASSEMBLED
There was never a room where the Bible was decided. That is the single most useful thing to know about it, and
the hardest thing to hold onto — because a room would be easier to write about, and every empire in the book
would prefer you believed in one.

USE   CAME    F I R S T,   RECOGNITION            CAME     SECOND           I N F O R M AT I O N



Communities read, copied, argued over, and prayed with these texts for generations before anyone published a list. Lists,
when they came, mostly rati ed habits already formed — and the habits were local. A congregation in Alexandria and one in
Carthage could disagree about a book for a century without either considering itself in schism.

FOUR   GOSPELS,            AND   W H AT   T H AT   A C T U A L LY   MEANS


Other gospels existed and circulated. The four in the canon are the four that were most widely read across the most
communities, earliest, and were treated as apostolic in origin — and Irenaeus was arguing for exactly four by the late second
century, which tells you the number was contested enough to need an argument. Calling them the gospels that "won" makes
it sound like a vote. It was closer to consensus hardening around what was already in use, with the theological arguments
running alongside rather than deciding it in one stroke.

WHO    A C T U A L LY    HELD    THE   AUTHORITY          CONTESTED



Bishops wrote lists. Councils con rmed regional practice more often than they created it. Emperors funded copies, convened
meetings, and made certain outcomes safer to hold than others — Constantine's involvement at Nicaea was real, and Nicaea
was not about the canon. Local congregational use was the slow current under all of it. The honest sentence is that canon
formation was a long negotiation among all four, and the balance shifted by century and region.

THE   CLAIM    WORTH         KEEPING,        S TAT E D   C A R E F U L LY


Not canonization is always performed by whoever can convene the meeting — too absolute, and it credits meetings with more
than they did. What holds: the people who could convene, fund, copy, and distribute had disproportionate inuence over
which texts stayed in circulation, and circulation is most of what canon means in practice.

TRANSLATION AND
HOW TO COMPARE IT
The old title asked which translation to trust. Wrong question — it implies one exists that never has to be checked. The
goal is to understand how translation decisions get made, so you can see one being made and say what it did.
THREE    L ANGUAGES      I N F O R M AT I O N




 Hebrew                Most of the Old Testament. Written originally without vowels; the vowel pointing was added centuries later
                       by scribes preserving how the text was read aloud. A consonantal text can sometimes be vocalized more than
                       one way, and where it can, translators choose.
 Aramaic               Portions of Daniel and Ezra, and the common speech of Judea in the rst century. Jesus taught in it; the
                       Gospels record him in Greek.
 Greek                 The New Testament, in the common Greek of the eastern Mediterranean — not literary Greek, and not
                       uniform. Mark's is rough; Hebrews' is polished. Translations that atten every book to the same register lose
                       that.

MANUSCRIPTS       AND   VA R I A N T S


No originals survive for any biblical book. What exists is thousands of copies, and they dier — overwhelmingly in spelling,
word order, and obvious copying slips, occasionally in something substantive. Textual criticism is the discipline of
reconstructing the earliest recoverable text from that evidence, and modern translations rest on far better manuscript evidence
than any earlier century had. A handful of famous passages — the longer ending of Mark, the woman accused of adultery in
John, a clause in 1 John — are absent from the earliest manuscripts, and reputable Bibles say so in a footnote.
WHERE    DIFFERENCES        A C T U A L LY      COME     FROM


Most variation between translations is not ideological. It comes from a genuinely ambiguous word, a construction with no
clean English equivalent, a decision about register, or a choice between manuscript readings. Some dierences are
theologically motivated, and those are worth nding — but assuming that of every dierence will make you unable to
recognize the real cases when they appear.




   You do not need the languages to do this work. You need to know where the decisions live, and to look at more than one Bible
   when a sentence is doing something to somebody.

EIGHT QUESTIONS
Run all eight, in order, before deciding what a passage means. Not because it makes you an expert — it does not — but
because most con dent misuse of scripture happens by skipping one of them, and skipping is easy to catch in yourself if
you have a list.
1   ·    W H AT   KIND    OF   TEXT   IS   THIS?


Genre rst, always. It decides what the passage is capable of establishing before any other question is worth asking.
2   ·    WHO      IS   SPEAKING?


The narrator, a character, God as quoted, a rival, a friend who turns out to be wrong. Job's comforters are in the Bible and they are
mistaken, and the book says so.
3   ·    TO   WHOM?


A congregation with a speci c problem, a king, a foreign nation, an individual. Applying an oracle addressed to Babylon to
yourself is a category error with a long history.
4   ·    W H AT   COMES       BEFORE       AND    AFTER?


Read the paragraph, then the chapter. Most weaponized verses are load-bearing only when the sentence on either side has been
removed.
5   ·    W H AT   WORLD        PRODUCED      IT?


Empire, occupation, exile, monarchy, household economy, legal status of women and slaves. The setting is not background; it is
frequently the subject.
6   ·    W H AT   DO    THE    WORDS       DO?


Command, describe, lament, promise, threaten, concede, argue. Compare two translations and see whether they agree about
which.
7   ·    WHO      GAINS   FROM    THIS      READING?


Ask it of every reading, including the one you prefer. A reading that bene ts you is not thereby wrong — it is thereby worth
checking twice.
8   ·    W H AT   DOES    THIS   READING          COST?


And who pays it. This is the question Dominion has never once asked out loud, and the one Eden will eventually be unable to stop
asking.

        Not authority over people who quote this book for a living. Many of them run these questions better than you will. What
        it buys is the ability to tell the dierence between a reading that has done the work and one that has skipped a step —
        including your own — and to name which step.

    The most important distinction in this section. Treating all four as equally open is how a companion becomes useless —

The worked example, because Dominion's household law is built on this passage and because everything dicult about
reading scripture is present in it at once — a disputed author, a supplied verb, a contested word, a Roman legal

WHERE     THE    SENTENCE           A C T U A L LY   S TA R T S      I N F O R M AT I O N



Verse 21 instructs the whole congregation to submit to one another. Verse 22 addresses wives. Several of the earliest and most

is modern editorial apparatus, not scripture.              M S S : P 4 6 · S I N A I T I C U S · VAT I C A N U S

scholars, and the grammar alone does not decide between them — anyone who tells you the Greek settles it is overselling, in
either direction.

THE HOUSEHOLD CODE
Wives, children, slaves, addressed in that order, is not an invention of this letter. It is a recognizable Greco-Roman form
— moral instruction on managing a household, which was a political unit as much as a domestic one, because the
ordered household was understood to be the foundation of the ordered state.
W H AT   THE   FORM      ASSUMES


A male head with legal authority over wife, children, and enslaved people; the household as an economic enterprise; and the
state's interest in its order. A rst-century reader would recognize the shape immediately and would notice the departures.
WHERE     THIS   VERSION        D E PA R T S   FROM        THE   FORM    CONTESTED



It addresses the subordinate parties directly as moral agents rather than as property to be managed. It gives the husband the
longer and more demanding instruction. It grounds the whole arrangement in self-giving rather than in the head's
prerogative. Whether these departures amount to subversion of the code, a Christianized version of it, or an accommodation
that made the movement socially survivable is a live scholarly argument — and all three readings are held by people who have
done the work.
THE   INSTRUCTIONS         TO   HUSBANDS           —   READ      THEM,       THEY   ARE    THE   MAJORITY   OF   THE     PA S S A G E


Love as Christ loved and gave himself up. Nourish and cherish as one's own body. The passage issues the husband no
command to rule, no instruction to enforce, and no license to correct. A doctrine of headship that produces enforcement is
therefore drawing on the noun in verse 23 rather than on anything the passage tells husbands to do \u2014 and what it tells
them to do occupies most of its length.        EPH 5.25\u201333

HOW PROPHECY WORKS
B E F O R E   Y O U   R E A D   A N Y      O F    T H E M




A prophet is not a fortune teller. A prophet is someone who stands in public and tells a government what it is
doing, in the name of a God the government also claims. That is the whole job, and it explains why so many
of them are run out of town, imprisoned, or killed. Almost nothing in these seventeen books is about the
distant future; nearly all of it is about the harvest, the courts, the treasury, the treaty, and who is being
crushed this season.
The form is consistent. An accusation naming a speci c injustice, a verdict, and then very often a reversal —
the promise that after collapse something is rebuilt. Empires like the third part and are nervous about the
  rst, so a state religion teaches the prophets as comfort literature and hopes nobody reads the accusations
closely enough to notice they are itemised: unjust weights, seized land, bribed judges, unpaid workers,
widows without recourse, refugees turned away.
Two other things. The prophets attack worship itself, viciously, when it coexists with exploitation — the
festivals are hated, the oerings refused, the singing called noise. There is no more anti-clerical literature in
existence and it is in the canon. And false prophecy is a live theme here, with a test that is not accuracy but
courage: the false prophet is the one who tells power what it wants to hear.
