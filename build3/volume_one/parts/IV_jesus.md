### Part IV. Jesus, the Kingdom and the Lord's Prayer

The novel takes its title from one line of a prayer Jesus taught, and the Empire takes its founding sermon's name from the same line (TL). This part covers what "kingdom" meant in the gospels, what Jesus kept doing that institutions have found hard to absorb, the prayer itself clause by clause in its two versions, and a grid for holding the four gospels side by side without flattening them. The old Companion's prayer page had real errors, and they're corrected below. Scholarly positions on the historical Jesus are named where they belong to someone. E. P. Sanders (The Historical Figure of Jesus, 1993) and Bart Ehrman (The New Testament: A Historical Introduction) stand behind much of the background.

#### The kingdom

**The word.** Basileia means kingdom, kingship or reign, and it's the word for the empire that ruled Judea. "The kingdom of God" is the center of Jesus' preaching in the first three gospels. Mark's summary of his message is "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God has come near" (Mark 1:15). Matthew usually says "kingdom of heaven," a reverent way of avoiding the word God, and means the same thing.

**What it's like.** The parables describe it as small, hidden and growing without permission: a mustard seed, yeast worked into flour, treasure buried in a field, a merchant selling everything for one pearl (Matt 13:31–46). It belongs to the poor (Luke 6:20). Children, tax collectors and prostitutes go into it ahead of the respectable (Mark 10:14–15, Matt 21:31). It's very hard for the rich to enter, harder than a camel going through the eye of a needle (Mark 10:25).

**Rank turned over.** When his disciples argue about who's greatest, he tells them the rulers of the nations lord it over their subjects, "but it shall not be so among you." Whoever wants to be first must be slave of all (Mark 10:42–44, Matt 20:25–27). In John he washes their feet, a slave's work, and tells them to do the same (John 13:1–17). When a crowd wants to make him king by force, he withdraws (John 6:15). He enters Jerusalem on a donkey, which Matthew links to Zechariah 9:9, a king "humble and mounted on a donkey" (Matt 21:1–11). INTERPRETATION: Marcus Borg and John Dominic Crossan (The Last Week, 2006) read the entry as a deliberate counter-procession to the Roman governor's arrival with troops at Passover. The charge on the cross, in all four gospels, is "King of the Jews."

**Now or later.** CONTESTED: Whether the kingdom is present, future or both. Albert Schweitzer (The Quest of the Historical Jesus, 1906) argued that Jesus expected an imminent end of the world. C. H. Dodd (1935) argued the kingdom was already present in Jesus' ministry. Most scholars now hold some version of "already and not yet." Luke 17:21 says the kingdom is entos hymōn, which can mean "within you" or "among you," and the translation changes the meaning from an inner state to a present reality in the community.

**Two sayings the Empire would need.** "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36). CONTESTED: The Greek ek tou kosmou toutou means "from this world," and many scholars read it as a statement about the kingdom's source and not its location: it doesn't come from this world's kind of power, which is why his servants don't fight. "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Mark 12:13–17) answers a trap about paying tax to Rome. He asks whose image (eikōn) is on the coin. CONTESTED: Readers have taken the answer as endorsing two separate spheres, as evading the trap, and as a challenge, since the hearers know whose image human beings bear (Gen 1:27).

#### Four things he kept doing

**Authority.** Crowds say he teaches "as one who had authority, and not as the scribes" (Mark 1:22), and officials keep asking "By what authority are you doing these things?" (Mark 11:28). He argues sabbath practice with the Pharisees (Mark 2:23–3:6), denounces scribes and Pharisees as "blind guides" and "whitewashed tombs" (Matt 23:16, 27), and drives the money changers out of the temple court (Mark 11:15–17).

**Bodies.** He touches a man with a skin disease (Mark 1:41) and takes a dead girl by the hand (Mark 5:41). The woman with the hemorrhage touches him first, without asking, and he commends her faith (Mark 5:25–34, Part III). INTERPRETATION: In these stories the contagion runs the other way. He doesn't become unclean, and they become well. CONTESTED: Whether that amounts to opposing the purity system. Paula Fredriksen and Amy-Jill Levine (The Misunderstood Jew, 2006) warn that reading Jesus as against Jewish law is a Christian habit with an ugly history. The woman broke no law by being in a crowd, and Jesus sends the healed man to the priest with the offering Moses commanded (Mark 1:44).

**Women.** Mary of Bethany sits at his feet in the place of a student and he defends her (Luke 10:38–42). Named women fund the movement (Luke 8:1–3). A Syrophoenician woman argues with him over healing her daughter, answers his refusal with "even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs," and he grants it "for this saying" (Mark 7:24–30). She wins the exchange. He has a long theological conversation with a Samaritan woman (John 4) and is anointed by a woman in a room of men who call it waste (Mark 14:3–9). No gospel records him telling a woman to be silent.

**Money.** He tells a rich man to sell everything and give to the poor, and the man goes away grieving (Mark 10:17–22). Luke pairs blessings on the poor with woes on the rich (6:20–26) and adds the rich fool (12:13–21) and the rich man and Lazarus (16:19–31). In his first sermon at Nazareth he reads Isaiah 61, good news to the poor, release to the captives, "the year of the Lord's favor," which echoes the Jubilee release (Lev 25, II.3), and says it's fulfilled today (Luke 4:16–21). Debt forgiveness is written into the prayer (below).

Correction to the old Companion: it says the room at Nazareth "turns on him" over the year of release. The congregation first speaks well of him (4:22). They try to throw him off a cliff after he says Elijah and Elisha were sent to foreigners, a widow of Sidon and a Syrian general, and not to Israel (4:24–30).

#### Thy kingdom come: the Lord's Prayer

The prayer appears twice, in different settings and different forms. In Matthew it's part of the Sermon on the Mount, taught as the right way to pray instead of heaping up empty phrases (6:7–13). In Luke the disciples ask "Lord, teach us to pray, as John taught his disciples," and he gives a shorter version (11:1–4). Every pronoun for the people praying is plural: us, our. There's no "I" in it.

| Clause | Matthew 6:9–13 | Luke 11:2–4 | Common liturgical form |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Address | Our Father in heaven | Father | Our Father, who art in heaven |
| Name | Hallowed be your name | Hallowed be your name | Hallowed be thy name |
| Kingdom | Your kingdom come | Your kingdom come | Thy kingdom come |
| Will | Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven | Absent in the earliest manuscripts | Thy will be done, on earth as it is in heaven |
| Bread | Give us this day our daily bread | Give us each day our daily bread | Give us this day our daily bread |
| Forgiveness | Forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors | Forgive us our sins, for we ourselves forgive everyone indebted to us | Forgive us our trespasses, as we forgive those who trespass against us |
| Testing | Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil (or "the evil one") | Lead us not into temptation | Lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil |
| Doxology | Absent in the earliest manuscripts | Absent | For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen. |

**Clause by clause.**

"Our Father in heaven." A shared parent makes the people praying siblings. INTERPRETATION: Authority is placed where no earthly office can occupy it.

"Hallowed be your name." A request that God's name be treated as holy. INTERPRETATION: It implies that no other name is, imperial or dynastic.

"Your kingdom come." The title line, in both gospels. It asks for the kingdom's arrival, which assumes it isn't fully here. The Jewish Kaddish, an Aramaic prayer whose core many scholars date early, also pairs the hallowing of God's name with a prayer that God's kingdom be established soon, and the two prayers are often compared.

"Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven." This clause is Matthew's. It's absent from Luke in the earliest manuscripts (P75, Codex Vaticanus) and was added to Luke by later copyists. INTERPRETATION: In Matthew it ties the kingdom to the earth, to the place where taxes are collected and soldiers are stationed.

"Our daily bread." The Greek word translated "daily," epiousios, appears nowhere in Greek literature before the gospels, and its meaning is uncertain. Proposals include "for today," "for tomorrow," and "necessary for existence." Jerome translated it two different ways in the Vulgate: "supersubstantial" in Matthew and "daily" in Luke. It asks for enough, and for "our" bread, not "my" bread.

"Forgive us our debts." Matthew uses opheilēmata, debts, and Luke uses hamartias, sins, though Luke's second half keeps the debt language ("everyone indebted to us"). Both versions tie the request to the one praying it: forgive us as we forgive. "Trespasses" isn't in either gospel's version of the prayer. It comes from William Tyndale's English translation of Matthew (1526) and from the Book of Common Prayer (1549), and it passed from there into Anglican and Catholic English liturgy. Matthew's very next verses use a word that does mean trespasses, paraptōmata (6:14–15).

Correction to the old Companion: it says institutions render the clause "trespasses" to blunt its financial sense. The difference between "debts" (Matthew), "sins" (Luke) and "trespasses" (the liturgy) tracks which text and which translation tradition a church inherited. It isn't evidence of anyone's motive. Churches that say "debts," Presbyterian and Reformed ones among them, follow the King James Matthew.

"Lead us not into temptation." Peirasmos means testing or trial as well as temptation. INTERPRETATION: It's a request to be spared the test, not to pass it heroically. In 2017 Pope Francis criticized the traditional wording, and the French Catholic liturgy (adopted 2017) and the Italian (approved 2018, confirmed by Rome in 2019) changed it to "do not let us enter into temptation" and "do not abandon us to temptation." "Deliver us from evil" can also be read "from the evil one," since the Greek tou ponērou is ambiguous.

"For thine is the kingdom." The doxology is absent from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew (Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, Codex Bezae) and from every early manuscript of Luke. A shorter form, "for yours is the power and the glory forever," appears in the Didache (8:2), an early Christian manual from around 100 CE. Later manuscripts of Matthew added it, the King James Version printed it, and most modern translations put it in a footnote. Protestant churches generally say it. The Catholic Mass has said it since 1970, but separately, after a prayer the priest inserts.

Correction to the old Companion: it presents a single prayer and treats "on earth as in heaven" as part of the line the novel is named for. "Thy kingdom come" is in both gospels. "On earth as in heaven" is Matthew's alone, the doxology is a later addition, and the prayer most people recite is a composite of Matthew's text with a liturgical ending.

#### The Gospel Grid

There are four gospels, written for different communities over several decades, and they differ on the order of events, the wording of sayings, the day Jesus died and what happened at the tomb. All four are formally anonymous. The names were attached by tradition, generally in the second century. Every date below is a mainstream range and every one is argued.

| | Mark | Matthew | Luke | John |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Usual date | About 65–75 | About 80–90 | About 80–95 | About 90–110 |
| Length | 16 chapters, the shortest | 28 chapters | 24 chapters, continued in Acts | 21 chapters |
| Opens with | John the Baptist in the wilderness | A genealogy from Abraham | A dedication to Theophilus claiming orderly research | "In the beginning was the Word," before creation |
| Birth story | None | Magi, Herod's massacre of the children, flight to Egypt | Shepherds, Mary's song, presentation at the temple | None |
| Genealogy | None | Abraham to Joseph, naming Tamar, Rahab, Ruth and "the wife of Uriah" | Back to Adam, through a different line after David | None |
| Teaching | Short, often private explanations | Five large blocks, the Sermon on the Mount first | The Sermon on the Plain, and the most parables | Long first-person discourses, the "I am" sayings |
| Parables | Several | Many | The good Samaritan and the prodigal son are only here | None of the familiar kind |
| Exorcisms | Central | Frequent | Frequent | None |
| Lord's Prayer | Absent | 6:9–13 | 11:2–4 | Absent |

| Temple action | Final week, the trigger for the arrest | Final week | Final week | At the start of the ministry (ch. 2) |

Jesus appears to her first. |

| Leans toward | Disciples who fail to understand. His identity is kept quiet until a Roman soldier names it at the cross. | Fulfillment of scripture, the law, community discipline, judgment | The poor, reversals of status, women, foreigners | Who Jesus is, and belief |

Correction to the old Companion: it says "no two of the four give the same last words." Mark and Matthew give the same cry, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?", in slightly different forms (Eloi in Mark, Eli in Matthew). Luke's "Father, forgive them" (23:34) is missing from some of the earliest manuscripts, including P75 and Codex Vaticanus, and its originality is CONTESTED.

**Who used whom.** Matthew, Mark and Luke run parallel closely enough to print in columns, sometimes with identical Greek for long stretches. Some literary relationship is certain. Which one is CONTESTED.

| Proposal | What it claims | Its weak point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Two-source (the majority view) | Matthew and Luke each used Mark, plus a lost collection of sayings called Q, plus material of their own | Q has never been found and is reconstructed from the texts it explains |
| Farrer (Mark Goodacre, The Case Against Q, 2002) | Luke used both Mark and Matthew, so no lost source is needed | It has to explain why Luke broke up Matthew's sermons and moved the pieces |
| Matthew first (Griesbach and later forms) | Matthew is earliest, and Mark condensed the others | It has to explain why a condenser would add rough Greek and unflattering details |

Whether John knew the other three, knew a tradition behind them or wrote independently is a separate dispute.

**What only one gospel has.** Mark: a healing that takes two tries (8:22–26), and a young man who runs away naked at the arrest (14:51–52). Matthew: the magi, the massacre of the children, the guard at the tomb, and the sheep and the goats (25:31–46). Luke: the shepherds, the Magnificat, the good Samaritan, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus, the road to Emmaus. John: the wedding at Cana, Nicodemus at night, the Samaritan woman, the raising of Lazarus, the foot washing and the long farewell discourse.

**What all four keep.** Women are at the tomb first in every gospel, in a culture where women's legal testimony carried little weight. INTERPRETATION: Some scholars argue that's a detail a community inventing the story wouldn't have chosen. It's an argument from plausibility, not a proof.
