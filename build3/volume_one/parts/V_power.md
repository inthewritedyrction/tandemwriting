### Part V. Christianity and Power

Almost the whole Bible was written by people living under someone else's government, and most of its later history has been read aloud by people holding one. This part covers the empires inside the text, the separate powers that decide what a text means, the passages states and churches have leaned on, and American history from the Puritans to the present, which is the history the Covenant Coalition grew out of (TL). The American sections are new research, and they describe what movements said and did. Where a characterization of a living movement is disputed, it's marked CONTESTED.

#### Empires of the Bible

| Empire | What it wanted | How it got it | Where to read it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Egypt | Labor | Forced labor, rising quotas, and a decree controlling births (Exod 1, 5). Unrest is answered with more work. | Exodus 1–14 |
| Assyria | Submission, advertised | Terror and deportation. Conquered peoples were broken up and resettled among strangers, and the northern kingdom disappeared this way (2 Kgs 17). Its envoy shouts terms at Jerusalem's wall in the local language so the people on the wall can hear (2 Kgs 18:26–28). | 2 Kings 17–19, Nahum |
| Babylon | The top of a society | It deported the educated and skilled, trained young men in its own language and literature, gave them new names and careers (Dan 1:3–7), and left the poor to work the land (2 Kgs 25:12). | 2 Kings 24–25, Daniel 1–6, Lamentations |
| Persia | Order at low cost | Licensed and funded local religion. It sent the exiles home, paid for the temple and authorized its law (Ezra 1, 6–7). Its decrees can't be revoked (Esth 8:8, Dan 6:8). Its court gathers young women by search and removes a queen by legislation (Esth 1–2). | Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther, Daniel 6 |
| Rome | Taxes and quiet | Governed through local clients: a client king, a temple aristocracy, a governor. Crucifixion was public and slow, by roads. The emperor was honored as divine and his rule called peace. | The gospels, Acts, Revelation |

Correction to the old Companion: it calls Babylon's method elite capture "far cheaper than terror." Babylon also besieged Jerusalem, burned the temple and blinded the king (2 Kgs 25:7–9). It used both methods.

#### Who gets to say what it means

Authority over a text is a set of separate powers. They're often held by different people, and a religious state becomes total when one institution holds all of them.

| Power | What it decides | Historical case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canon | Which books count | Settled for most churches long ago, and so invisible (I.2) |
| Translation | Which words | William Tyndale was executed in 1536, in part for translating the Bible into English without license. |
| Lectionary | Which passages are read aloud, on which days | Most worshippers hear only what the calendar of readings allows. |
| Commentary | What a passage is taken to mean | The Geneva Bible's margin notes (1560) were one reason James I wanted a new translation without them (I.4). |
| Pulpit | Who may stand up and speak | Licensing of preachers, and the withdrawal of a license |
| The copy | Who owns a book, and which edition | Abridged editions like the 1807 Slave Bible (below) |
| Literacy | Who can read it unaided | South Carolina's Negro Act of 1740 banned teaching enslaved people to write, and several Southern states tightened anti-literacy laws after Nat Turner's revolt in 1831. |

INTERPRETATION: Movements that broke a religious monopoly usually did it by attacking translation, literacy or the copy. Reform tends to show up as a printing problem before it's a theological one.

#### Passages put to work

These are passages that states and churches have leaned on to justify their own authority. Each is in the text, and each has been read more narrowly or more widely than the text requires.

| Passage | How it's been used | What the text does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Genesis 3:16 | As a charter for male rule in the household and in law | CONTESTED: prescription or description (II.3). The woman isn't cursed in the chapter. |
| Romans 13:1–7 | As a demand for obedience to whatever government holds power. Loyalist clergy preached it against the American Revolution, and in June 2018 the U.S. attorney general cited it to defend a border policy that separated children from their parents. | Written by a man repeatedly jailed by the authorities he describes, in a canon that includes prophets defying kings (I.6) |
| Ephesians 5:22 | As household law | Grows out of 5:21, "submit to one another," and is followed by a longer section demanding self-giving from husbands (I.7) |
| 1 Timothy 2:11–12 | As a permanent ban on women teaching | CONTESTED authorship (II.1). Named women teach elsewhere in the New Testament (Acts 18:26, Rom 16). |
| Deuteronomy 28 | As a national scoreboard: prosperity proves virtue and disaster proves sin | One side of an argument the Bible has with itself. Job and Ecclesiastes refuse it (II.2). |
| Joshua 7 | As a case for collective punishment, since one household's theft endangers the nation | Set against Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18 (II.2, II.3) |
| Numbers 16 | As the standing sermon against challenging ordained leaders | Paired in the same book with the daughters of Zelophehad, whose petition succeeds (II.2) |
| Judges 21:25 | As an argument for strong rule: without a king, everyone does as he likes | The book's own closing frame, after its worst chapters for women |
| 2 Samuel 7 | As a promise of an eternal dynasty | Followed in the same book by Nathan's "You are the man" (Part III) |

Correction to the old Companion: it calls Genesis 3:16 "a curse, not a command." The woman isn't cursed in Genesis 3 (II.3), and whether the line is a command is the disputed point. It also calls 1 Timothy "the most disputed letter in the collection." 2 Peter was the most doubted book in antiquity (II.1). 1 Timothy's authorship is disputed along with the other Pastoral letters.

#### American history

**Puritan covenant theology and the city on a hill.** The Puritans who settled Massachusetts Bay in 1630 understood their colony as a community bound to God by covenant, on the model of Israel in Deuteronomy. Blessing would follow faithfulness, and judgment would follow breach. The sermon "A Model of Christian Charity" is traditionally attributed to John Winthrop and dated to 1630, given aboard or beside the Arbella. CONTESTED: Some scholars suggest another minister wrote it, and it wasn't printed until 1838. It says the colony "shall be as a city upon a hill," borrowing Matthew 5:14, and its point is that the whole world will watch, so failure will be public. It closes by paraphrasing Deuteronomy 30: "Therefore let us choose life." Daniel Rodgers (As a City on a Hill, 2018) argues the sermon was little known until the twentieth century. John F. Kennedy quoted it to the Massachusetts legislature as president-elect on January 9, 1961. Ronald Reagan made "a shining city upon a hill" a theme of his speeches and the center of his farewell address on January 11, 1989, adding the word "shining." In Winthrop's use the image is a warning about being watched. In later political use it became a description of national greatness.

**Proslavery and abolitionist readings of the same texts.** Both sides of the American slavery debate read the same Bible and claimed it. Proslavery ministers cited Noah's curse on Canaan (Gen 9:25, read as a curse on Africans, II.3), the permanent slavery of foreigners in Leviticus 25:44–46, Abraham's household slaves, the instructions to slaves in Ephesians 6:5, Colossians 3:22 and 1 Peter 2:18, and Paul's return of Onesimus in Philemon. They argued that Jesus never condemned slavery. After the Denmark Vesey plot in Charleston in 1822, the Baptist minister Richard Furman wrote an exposition, dated that December, for the South Carolina Baptist Convention defending slavery from scripture, and its arguments were used by Southern defenders of slavery until the Civil War. Thornton Stringfellow, a Virginia Baptist pastor, published A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery (Richmond, 1841).

Abolitionists answered with the Golden Rule (Matt 7:12), "God made of one blood all nations" (Acts 17:26, KJV), "there is neither bond nor free" (Gal 3:28), the capital law against kidnapping a person to sell (Exod 21:16), the refusal to return runaways (Deut 23:15–16) and the exodus itself. Angelina Grimké, from a South Carolina slaveholding family, published An Appeal to the Christian Women of the South (1836) through the American Anti-Slavery Society, and made the Bible her test. Frederick Douglass added an appendix to his Narrative (1845) to make clear that his attacks were on "the slaveholding religion of this land," which he set against "the Christianity of Christ." Mark Noll's The Civil War as a Theological Crisis (2006) argues that the conflict exposed a crisis in how Protestants read the Bible: both sides held scripture to be authoritative and plain, and neither could persuade the other from it.

The churches split along the same line. Methodists divided in 1844. The Southern Baptist Convention was founded in Augusta, Georgia, in 1845, after the national Baptist mission board refused to appoint slaveholders as missionaries. Presbyterians divided in stages, finally in 1861. In June 1995, meeting in Atlanta, the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution repenting of its past racism and apologizing to African Americans.

**The 1807 "Slave Bible."** In 1807 a London missionary society published Select Parts of the Holy Bible, for the Use of the Negro Slaves, in the British West-India Islands. According to Anthony Schmidt, a curator at the Museum of the Bible in Washington, it kept 232 of the 1,189 chapters in a standard Protestant Bible, leaving out about ninety percent of the Old Testament and about half of the New. The exodus story is gone. Passages that reinforce slavery, including Ephesians 6:5, are kept. A copy on loan from Fisk University was exhibited at the museum in 2018–2019. The name "Slave Bible" is modern. INTERPRETATION: It's the clearest case on record of controlling scripture without changing a word of it, by deciding which words are in the copy (I.7).

**The Black church tradition.** Under slavery, much Black Christian worship was what the sociologist E. Franklin Frazier called the "invisible institution," held in secret, often in woods and gullies known as hush harbors, beyond the reach of white supervision and of laws against Black gatherings. Albert Raboteau's Slave Religion: The "Invisible Institution" in the Antebellum South (1978) is the standard history. In Philadelphia, Richard Allen and other Black worshippers walked out of St. George's Methodist Church over segregated seating (Allen dated it to 1787, and some historians argue for 1792). Allen founded Mother Bethel in 1794, and in 1816 the African Methodist Episcopal Church became an independent denomination with Allen as its first bishop. In Charleston, the African church that became Emanuel AME was burned by white authorities in 1822 after Denmark Vesey, one of its founders, was accused of planning a revolt. Its members worshipped in secret until 1865. On June 17, 2015, a white supremacist killed nine people at a Bible study in the same church. C. Eric Lincoln and Lawrence Mamiya's The Black Church in the African American Experience (1990) describes the seven historically Black denominations that grew from this history. The spirituals, the exodus as the central story (II.3), the preaching tradition, and the civil rights movement's base in churches, from the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (1957) to Martin Luther King Jr.'s last sermon, all come out of it.

**Christian nationalism.** CONTESTED, as a term and in its boundaries. The sociologists Andrew Whitehead and Samuel Perry, in Taking America Back for God (2020), define it as "a cultural framework... that idealizes and advocates a fusion of Christianity with American civic life," and argue that the "Christian" in it is more an identity than a faith. A 2023 PRRI and Brookings survey built a scale from five statements about Christianity and American law and society. It classed about 10 percent of American adults as adherents and 19 percent as sympathizers, with 39 percent skeptics and 29 percent rejecters. Among white evangelical Protestants, 29 percent were adherents and 35 percent sympathizers. Critics, including the political scientist Mark David Hall, argue the definitions are too broad and sweep in ordinary religious participation in politics. Some on the right have taken up the term themselves. Stephen Wolfe's The Case for Christian Nationalism (2022) argues for it openly.

**Christian Reconstructionism.** R. J. Rushdoony founded the Chalcedon Foundation in 1965 and published The Institutes of Biblical Law in 1973, a commentary on the Ten Commandments arguing that God's law in scripture, including the case laws of the Torah, should be the standard for civil law. Rushdoony called the program Christian Reconstruction. Greg Bahnsen's Theonomy in Christian Ethics (1977) gave the position its name, and Gary North, Rushdoony's son-in-law, extended it into economics. The movement's most distinctive claim is that the Mosaic civil penalties remain valid, and its writers listed the death penalty for offenses including adultery, homosexual acts, blasphemy, idolatry, witchcraft and incorrigible children. CONTESTED: How consistently Rushdoony applied the penalties is argued, since he also wrote that nations not in covenant with God aren't bound to all of them. Most evangelical and Reformed theologians rejected theonomy. The faculty of Westminster Theological Seminary published Theonomy: A Reformed Critique (1990). Rushdoony's influence on the Christian school and homeschooling movements was large and is acknowledged by supporters and critics alike.

**Dominionism and the Seven Mountains.** CONTESTED, as a label. The researcher Sara Diamond described "dominion theology" in Spiritual Warfare (1989), and the journalist Frederick Clarkson and others used "dominionism" for the wider set of Christian Right groups that read Genesis 1:28, "have dominion," as a mandate for Christian control over public life. Critics, among them the evangelical writer Joe Carter, call the label a smear with conspiratorial overtones, and some who use it agree it's often used loosely. The Seven Mountain Mandate holds that Christians are called to influence or lead seven spheres of society, usually listed as family, religion, education, media, arts and entertainment, government and business. Its proponents trace it to a 1975 meeting at which Loren Cunningham of Youth With A Mission and Bill Bright of Campus Crusade for Christ each received the same list. Francis Schaeffer is sometimes added to the story, and his involvement is disputed. Cunningham and Bright framed it as evangelism. After Lance Wallnau heard the story from Cunningham in 2000 and was mentored by C. Peter Wagner, the founder of the New Apostolic Reformation, the mandate took a more political turn, set out in Wallnau and Bill Johnson's Invading Babylon (2013). Some trace the idea further back, to the Dutch theologian and prime minister Abraham Kuyper's doctrine of "sphere sovereignty." Matthew D. Taylor's The Violent Take It by Force (2024) traces the New Apostolic Reformation's networks and their role in the events of January 6, 2021, including the Appeal to Heaven flag.

**Headship, settled in 1987.** I.7 flagged two dates to check. Both hold. The Council on Biblical Manhood and Womanhood drafted the complementarian Danvers Statement at a meeting in Danvers, Massachusetts, in December 1987 and published it in final form in November 1988. Christians for Biblical Equality was founded in 1987 and published its egalitarian statement, "Men, Women and Biblical Equality," in 1989, reaching a wide audience in Christianity Today in 1990.
