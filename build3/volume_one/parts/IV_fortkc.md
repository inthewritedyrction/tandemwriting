#### For TKC

- **The title, twice.** Elias Vane's 2033 sermon is called Thy Kingdom Come, and it's been required curriculum in every Dominion school since 2061 (TL). The novel takes the same words. INTERPRETATION: Every character who prays the line is asking either for the Empire to last or for something to replace it, and may not know which. The same line can be said twice in one scene by two people meaning opposite things.
- **The Empire's text of the prayer.** INFERENCE: The Book of Covenant uses King James wording (DR3, I.7), so the Temple's base text is probably Matthew 6 as the KJV prints it: "debts," "Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven," and the doxology. OPEN QUESTION: Does the Temple say "debts" or "trespasses" in public worship, and does it print Luke's shorter version anywhere? (VIII, working question 3.)
- **Relocating the kingdom.** POSSIBLE: The Temple makes "My kingdom is not of this world" its favorite verse, teaching the kingdom as an inner state or a reward after death, and then names the Empire as the kingdom come. The old Companion called this move the engine of the novel (AM). It needs both halves: the kingdom emptied of territory, and the Empire claiming the territory.
- **"On earth as it is in heaven."** POSSIBLE: The clause the Temple can't spiritualize, because it names the earth. Someone who's read Luke knows the prayer works without it, and someone who's read Matthew knows it doesn't.
- **Debts and levies.** In Aurem, families of the top five Assessment finishers get the kind of review where unpaid levies disappear (DR3). INTERPRETATION: The Empire forgives a household's debts in exchange for its daughter. The prayer ties forgiveness to forgiving others. POSSIBLE: A character notices that the Temple says "trespasses" in the same building where the levy office is.
- **Lead us not into testing.** INTERPRETATION: Twelve Covenant Daughters pray every week to be spared the test while the Empire tests them for four months. POSSIBLE: The Temple teaches the clause as a prayer for the strength to pass.
- **Women at the tomb.** All four gospels put women at the tomb first, and in Luke the apostles call their report nonsense. In Mark the earliest text ends with the women saying nothing to anyone because they're afraid. Eden's Theological Interview report runs three pages longer than anyone else's, and the officer can't tell whether she's the most aligned woman in twenty years or the most clear-eyed (TR). POSSIBLE: The question of whose testimony counts is the Trials' question too.
- **The Syrophoenician woman.** She wins an argument with Jesus and gets what she came for "for this saying." POSSIBLE: The scriptural precedent for Eden's Public Testimony, where every sentence is defensible and the cumulative argument is incendiary (TR).
- **The donkey and the Boulevard.** The Opening Procession gives Aurem's Covenant Daughter forty-seven seconds on Sovereign Boulevard (DOM), and every candidate's walk is choreographed (CR). POSSIBLE: Someone in the cast knows the entry into Jerusalem was read as a parody of a governor's procession, and walks the Boulevard thinking about it.
- **Rank turned over.** "It shall not be so among you." POSSIBLE: Judah knows Mark 10:42–44 and it's part of why he doesn't perform (WR 36). In the Pairing Introduction he sends the officials out (TR), which is the closest thing in the Trials to washing feet.
- **Four gospels and one Broadcast.** The Broadcast edits the Civic Debates for Dominion release, cutting some exchanges and building narratives decided in advance (TR). INTERPRETATION: The gospels show four communities shaping one story, openly and in the canon. The Broadcast does it in secret and calls the result the record.
- **Keeping the anti-Jewish trope out.** The Temple of TKC is the Empire's institution. When Jesus argues with Pharisees and scribes in the gospels, the argument is inside first-century Judaism. If the novel uses those scenes against the Temple, the narration has to know the difference, or it repeats an old Christian slander (WR 38–39).
- **Luke 4.** The Nazareth congregation turns on Jesus when he says God sent prophets to foreigners. POSSIBLE: The Temple preaches the release of Luke 4:18 as spiritual and leaves out 4:25–27 entirely, because favor to outsiders is what the Empire can't afford in any Dominion.

#### Working questions

1. What's the Empire's official text of the Lord's Prayer? Which gospel, which translation, and is anything cut or added?
2. Who in the cast says "debts" and who says "trespasses," and does anyone notice?
3. Write the passage of Elias's 2033 sermon that explains what "Thy kingdom come" means. Which line does every schoolchild know?
4. What does Celeste say the line means, at the kitchen table, when Eden is small?
5. Which of the four gospels does the Temple read from most, and which is almost never read aloud?
6. Does the Temple teach that Mark ends at 16:8? Would a girl with Eden's scores have found out on her own?
7. Who in the Crownward has read the Magnificat as a political text, and what happened to them?
8. Which saying of Jesus would get a Covenant Daughter flagged if she quoted it in the Civic Debates?
9. When Eden prays "lead us not into temptation" during the Trials, what does she mean by it?
10. What does the Empire do with "the rulers of the nations lord it over them"? Is it carved anywhere, or kept out of the readings?
11. What is Judah's private reading of "my kingdom is not of this world"?
12. Whose testimony does the Empire refuse to count, and what did that person see?
