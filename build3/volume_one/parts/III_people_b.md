#### Women of the New Testament

**Mary of Nazareth** (Luke 1–2, Mark 3, John 2, 19, Acts 1:14). Her name is Mariam, Miriam's name. Told by an angel that she'll bear a son, she asks a practical question, "How will this be?", before she agrees: "Let it be to me according to your word" (Luke 1:34–38). She goes "with haste" to her relative Elizabeth's house in the hill country and stays about three months (1:39–56). The song she sings there, the Magnificat, takes Hannah's song as its model. God has brought down the mighty from their thrones, lifted up the humble, filled the hungry and sent the rich away empty (1:51–53), and the verbs are in the past tense, as if it's already done. Simeon tells her a sword will pierce her own soul (2:35), and she "treasured up all these things" (2:19, 2:51). At Cana she tells Jesus "they have no wine," then tells the servants "do whatever he tells you" (John 2:3–5). In Mark his family comes to take him home because people are saying he's out of his mind, and when his mother and brothers arrive he points to the crowd as his family (Mark 3:21, 31–35). She stands at the cross in John (19:25–27) and is with the disciples in the upper room after (Acts 1:14). CONTESTED: Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14 from the Greek, "a virgin (parthenos) shall conceive." The Hebrew word, ʿalmah, means a young woman of marriageable age, and the translation is one of the oldest disputes between Jewish and Christian readers. She's usually reduced to a single word of consent.

Correction to the old Companion: it says she "travels alone" to Elizabeth's house. The text doesn't say whether anyone went with her.

**Mary Magdalene** (Luke 8:1–3, Mark 15–16, Matthew 27–28, John 19–20). From Magdala in Galilee. Luke says seven demons had gone out of her, and that she and other women supported Jesus and the twelve "out of their own means" (8:2–3). She's at the cross and at the burial in all four gospels and is named first in most lists of the women. In John she's the first to see the risen Jesus and is sent to tell the disciples: "I have seen the Lord" (20:11–18). In Luke the apostles think the women's report is nonsense (24:10–11). The text never calls her a prostitute. Pope Gregory the Great merged her with the unnamed sinful woman of Luke 7 and with Mary of Bethany in a sermon in 591 (Homily 33), and the reputation stuck for fourteen centuries. The 1969 revision of the Catholic calendar dropped her identification with the sinful woman, and in 2016 her memorial was raised to a feast with the old title "apostle to the apostles." A second-century text outside the canon, the Gospel of Mary, makes her a teacher of the other disciples.

**The woman at the well** (John 4:1–42). A Samaritan woman drawing water at noon. Jesus asks her for a drink, and she asks why a Jewish man is asking a Samaritan woman (4:9). Their conversation is the longest Jesus has with any one person in the gospels. She raises the live political-religious dispute between their peoples, whether God is to be worshiped on Mount Gerizim or in Jerusalem (4:20), and gets a serious answer. Jesus tells her she's had five husbands and the man she has now isn't her husband (4:16–18), and the text doesn't explain why, condemn her or have him condemn her. She's the first person in John to whom Jesus says plainly that he's the Messiah (4:25–26). She leaves her water jar, goes back to town, and "many Samaritans believed in him because of the woman's testimony" (4:28–39). She's usually reduced to her five husbands.

Correction to the old Companion: it says "women in that world did not divorce men." Under Jewish law only a husband could initiate divorce, and Roman law allowed either spouse to. The text doesn't say whether her marriages ended in death, divorce or something else.

**The woman with the hemorrhage, and the woman who anointed him** (Mark 5:25–34, Mark 14:3–9). A woman who's been bleeding for twelve years, and has spent everything she had on doctors and only gotten worse, comes up behind Jesus in a crowd and touches his cloak. Under Leviticus 15:25–30 she'd count as impure (II.3). He stops and asks who touched him, and she comes forward trembling and tells him "the whole truth." He calls her "Daughter" and tells her that her faith has made her well. The story is set inside the raising of a twelve-year-old girl. In Mark 14 an unnamed woman breaks a jar of expensive ointment over Jesus' head two days before his arrest. The men present scold her for the waste, and Jesus says that wherever the gospel is preached, what she's done will be told in memory of her (14:9). Her name wasn't kept.

**Martha and Mary of Bethany** (Luke 10:38–42, John 11–12). In Luke, Mary sits at Jesus' feet listening, the posture of a disciple, while Martha is "distracted with much serving" and asks him to send her sister to help. He says Mary has chosen the good portion. In John, Martha meets him after her brother Lazarus's death and makes the confession Peter makes in the other gospels: "You are the Christ, the Son of God" (11:27). Mary anoints his feet with perfume and wipes them with her hair (12:1–8).

**Phoebe** (Romans 16:1–2). Paul commends her to the church in Rome as a diakonos of the church at Cenchreae, the same word translated "deacon" for men elsewhere, and a prostatis, a patron or benefactor, "of many and of myself as well." INFERENCE: She probably carried the letter to Rome, and couriers often read letters aloud and explained them. Translations divide. The KJV and ESV render her title "servant," and the NRSV and NIV (2011) render it "deacon."

**Junia** (Romans 16:7). Paul greets Andronicus and Junia, "my kinsmen and my fellow prisoners," who are "of note among the apostles" and were in Christ before him. Junia is a common Latin woman's name, and early writers including John Chrysostom read her as a woman apostle. From the Middle Ages on, many editions and translations printed a masculine "Junias," a name with no attestation in ancient sources. Eldon Epp's Junia: The First Woman Apostle (2005) traces the change. CONTESTED: Whether the phrase means "prominent among the apostles" or "well known to the apostles." Michael Burer and Daniel Wallace argued for the second (2001), and others have answered them.

Correction to the old Companion: it says she was "imprisoned for it," meaning for being an apostle. The text says she and Andronicus had been in prison with Paul. It doesn't say why.

**Priscilla** (Acts 18, Romans 16:3–5, 1 Corinthians 16:19, 2 Timothy 4:19). Also called Prisca. A tentmaker with her husband Aquila, and a church meets in their house. When the gifted preacher Apollos arrives in Ephesus knowing only John's baptism, the two of them take him aside and explain "the way of God more accurately" (Acts 18:24–26). She's named before her husband in four of the six mentions, which most commentators read as a sign of her prominence.

**Lydia** (Acts 16:11–15, 40). A dealer in purple cloth, a luxury trade, from Thyatira, living in Philippi with her own household and no husband mentioned. She's the first person in Philippi to be baptized, often called the first convert in Europe. She insists that Paul and his companions stay at her house, and after their release from prison they go back there to meet the believers.

**Tabitha** (Acts 9:36–43). Also called Dorcas. She's the only person in the New Testament called mathētria, the feminine form of "disciple." She's known for good works and charity. When she dies, the widows show Peter the tunics and garments she'd made, and he raises her.

**The widows** (Acts 6:1–6, 1 Timothy 5:3–16). One of the first disputes in the Jerusalem community is a complaint that the Greek-speaking widows are being neglected in the daily distribution of food. Seven men are appointed to manage it. 1 Timothy sets out an enrolled order of widows with an age requirement (sixty), conditions and support from the church.

The letters that contain the restriction passages (1 Cor 14:34–35, 1 Tim 2:11–15) belong to the same collection that greets about ten women in Romans 16 alone, several with titles. How those fit together is the fault line in I.6.

#### Men of the text

**Abraham** (Genesis 12–25). Held up as the model of faith. The text records him twice passing his wife off as his sister to protect himself (12, 20), sending Hagar and Ishmael into the wilderness at Sarah's demand (21), and taking his son up a mountain to sacrifice him (22). It also records him arguing with God over Sodom, bargaining down from fifty righteous people to ten: "Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?" (18:22–33). Paul reads him as justified by faith (Rom 4), James by works (Jas 2:21–23). Kierkegaard's Fear and Trembling (1843) is the classic reading of chapter 22 as a horror.

**Jacob** (Genesis 25–35, 49). Buys his brother's birthright for stew and takes his blessing by disguise. He's deceived in turn on his wedding night and works twenty years for his father-in-law under shifting terms (31:38–41). He wrestles all night with a stranger, is renamed Israel, "strives with God," and limps afterward (32:22–32). He loves Rachel over Leah and Joseph over his brothers, openly, and says nothing when Dinah is assaulted until his sons have acted (34:5, 30).

**Judah** (Genesis 37–38, 43–44, 49). The fourth son of Leah. He proposes selling Joseph instead of killing him (37:26–27). He wrongs Tamar and admits it (ch. 38). In Egypt he offers himself as a slave in place of his brother Benjamin, in the longest speech in Genesis (44:18–34). Jacob's blessing gives him the scepter (49:10). His name became the tribe's, the southern kingdom's and finally the word "Jew."

**Joseph** (Genesis 37–50). Favored, sold by his brothers, imprisoned on a false accusation, and raised to run Egypt's grain. His famine policy ends with the whole population selling its land and itself to the crown (47:13–26, II.3). He forgives his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (50:20).

**Moses** (Exodus–Deuteronomy). Raised in the palace of the regime killing his people. He kills an Egyptian overseer and runs to Midian (Exod 2:11–15). He argues with God five times before he goes (II.3), and later talks God out of destroying the people (32:11–14). He's eighty when he confronts Pharaoh (7:7). He's barred from the land after striking the rock at Meribah (Num 20:2–13) and dies in sight of it, buried by God in a grave no one knows (Deut 34).

Correction to the old Companion: it says he spent "forty years as a shepherd." Exodus doesn't give the length of his time in Midian. The forty years come from Stephen's speech in Acts 7:23, 30 and later tradition.

**Aaron** (Exodus–Numbers). Moses' brother and spokesman, and the first high priest. He makes the golden calf, announces "a feast to the LORD," and explains that the gold went into the fire "and out came this calf" (Exod 32:4–5, 24). He keeps his office. He joins Miriam's challenge in Numbers 12 and isn't struck. His sons Nadab and Abihu die offering "strange fire" (Lev 10). He dies on Mount Hor and the people mourn him thirty days (Num 20:22–29).

**Gideon** (Judges 6–9). Found threshing wheat in a winepress to hide it from raiders. He tests God twice with a fleece, and wins with three hundred men. Offered hereditary rule, he refuses: "I will not rule over you, and my son will not rule over you. The LORD will rule over you" (8:23). Afterward he makes a gold ephod from the plunder, and it becomes "a snare" to his family (8:27). His son Abimelech kills seventy brothers to make himself king. The one survivor, Jotham, answers with a fable in which the trees offer kingship to the olive, the fig and the vine, who all decline, and finally to the bramble, which accepts and threatens to burn them all (9:7–15). Abimelech dies when a woman drops a millstone on his head from a tower (9:53).

**Samuel** (1 Samuel 1–16, 25:1). Hannah's son, raised at the sanctuary. When the people ask for a king "like all the nations," he warns them in detail what a king will take: sons for his chariots, daughters for his kitchens, the best fields and a tenth of the flocks, "and you shall be his slaves" (8:11–18). He anoints Saul anyway, and later anoints David while Saul is still alive.

**David** (1 Samuel 16 – 1 Kings 2). The most fully drawn character in the Hebrew Bible, and the portrait isn't flattering. A musician, a soldier, an outlaw whose men ask Nabal for payment (1 Sam 25), a king who dances before the ark and quarrels with Michal over it (2 Sam 6). He takes Bathsheba and has Uriah killed by written order (2 Sam 11). He does nothing when Amnon rapes Tamar (13:21), and nearly loses the kingdom to Absalom. On his deathbed he gives Solomon a list of men to deal with (1 Kgs 2:5–9). God promises him a dynasty forever (2 Sam 7), which every Christian monarchy later claimed as precedent. Nathan confronts him and he lives (2 Sam 12). He's usually reduced to the boy with the sling, the harpist, and "a man after God's own heart" (1 Sam 13:14).

**Nathan** (2 Samuel 7, 12, 1 Kings 1). The court prophet who first delivers the dynastic promise and then walks in and tells the king a story about a rich man who stole a poor man's only lamb. David condemns the rich man, and Nathan says, "You are the man" (12:7). Later he works with Bathsheba to put Solomon on the throne over an older brother (1 Kgs 1).

**Solomon** (1 Kings 1–11). Bathsheba's son, who wins the succession over an older brother and has rivals killed (1 Kgs 2). His first judgment is between two women who are prostitutes, each claiming the same baby, and the king hears them both in person (3:16–28). He builds the temple with conscripted labor (5:13–18). 1 Kings 9:20–22 says the forced labor fell on non-Israelites, and 1 Kings 12 says the northern tribes revolted over the "heavy yoke" he'd laid on them, so the two passages pull against each other. He breaks all four prohibitions of Deuteronomy 17 (II.3). Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs are attached to his name.

**Elijah** (1 Kings 17–19, 21, 2 Kings 1–2, Malachi 4:5–6). A prophet from Tishbe who confronts Ahab and Jezebel. A foreign widow at Zarephath feeds him from her last flour and oil (1 Kgs 17:8–16). He wins a contest on Mount Carmel and has the prophets of Baal killed (18:20–40). Afterward he runs from Jezebel's threat, asks to die, and is fed and told to sleep. God comes to him not in wind, earthquake or fire but in "a sound of thin silence" (19:1–12). He's taken up in a whirlwind (2 Kgs 2:11). Malachi ends with the promise that Elijah will return before the day of the LORD (4:5–6). The King James New Testament spells his name Elias (Matt 11:14, 17:3).

**Jeremiah** (the book of Jeremiah). Put on trial for his life for preaching that the temple would be destroyed (ch. 26). The king cuts up his scroll column by column and burns it, and he dictates it again (ch. 36). He's put in the stocks, accused of deserting to Babylon, and dropped into a muddy cistern. A Cushite eunuch in the palace, Ebed-melech, gets the king's permission and pulls him out with ropes and rags (38:7–13). He tells the exiles to "seek the welfare of the city" where they've been sent (29:7).

**Peter** (the gospels, Acts, Galatians 2). Impulsive and often wrong. He rebukes Jesus for predicting his death and is called "Satan" (Mark 8:32–33), cuts off a servant's ear in the garden (John 18:10), and denies knowing Jesus three times, the first time to a servant girl (Mark 14:66–72). He's restored with a question asked three times (John 21:15–17). Paul rebukes him publicly at Antioch for withdrawing from table fellowship with gentiles (Gal 2:11–14).

**Paul** (Acts, the letters). A Pharisee who persecuted the movement and then joined it (Gal 1:13–16). He's imprisoned repeatedly and defends himself in Roman courts. He writes that in Christ "there is no longer slave or free, no longer male and female" (Gal 3:28) and greets women leaders by name (Rom 16).

Correction to the old Companion: it says the silencing passages and Galatians 3:28 are "both his." 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 appears at a different place in some manuscripts, and many scholars think it was added later. 1 Timothy is one of the Pastoral letters whose authorship is CONTESTED (II.1, I.6). Neither is certainly Paul's.

**Judas** (the gospels, Acts 1). One of the twelve, who keeps the common purse (John 12:6, 13:29). The gospels give different accounts of why he hands Jesus over. Matthew has him ask the chief priests what they'll pay (26:14–16). John calls him a thief. Luke and John say Satan entered him (Luke 22:3, John 13:27). Matthew says he returns the silver and hangs himself (27:3–5), and Acts gives a different death (1:18).

Correction to the old Companion: it says "one gospel implies disillusionment." None does. Disillusionment is a modern reading.

**Pilate** (the gospels). The Roman prefect of Judea from 26 to 36 CE. All four gospels have him find no case against Jesus and order the crucifixion anyway. Only Matthew has him wash his hands before the crowd (27:24). An inscription found at Caesarea in 1961 names him.

**John the Baptist** (the gospels, Josephus). The son of a priest (Luke 1:5), preaching in the wilderness. He calls the crowds, or in Matthew the Pharisees and Sadducees, a "brood of vipers" (Matt 3:7, Luke 3:7). Herod Antipas imprisons him for condemning his marriage to his brother's wife and has him beheaded at a banquet at his stepdaughter's request (Mark 6:17–29). From prison he sends to ask Jesus, "Are you the one who is to come, or shall we look for another?" (Matt 11:2–3). Josephus says Herod killed him because he feared John's influence over the crowds might lead to revolt (Antiquities 18.116–119), so the political reason is on the record too.
