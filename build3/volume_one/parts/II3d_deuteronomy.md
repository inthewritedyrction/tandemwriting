#### Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy is Moses' farewell on the plains of Moab, a series of speeches that restate the law for a generation about to enter the land without him. It ends with his death and a burial place no one knows (34:5–6). The Hebrew name, Devarim, means "words." Moshe Weinfeld's Anchor commentary and his Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School (1972) stand behind much of this entry.

**The name.** "Deuteronomy" comes from the Greek deuteronomion, "second law." It's a mistranslation. In 17:18 the king is told to write for himself "a copy of this law" (mishneh hattorah hazzot), and the Greek translators rendered the phrase as "this second law." The book presents itself as the same law restated, not a new one.

**Josiah's scroll.** In 2 Kings 22–23, during repairs to the temple under King Josiah (about 622 BCE), the high priest Hilkiah finds "the book of the law." The king tears his clothes when it's read to him and sends to the prophet Huldah, who authenticates it. A covenant renewal, a purge of outlying shrines and a centralized Passover follow. W. M. L. de Wette argued in 1805 that the scroll was a form of Deuteronomy, since the reform matches its demands. CONTESTED: Most critical scholars accept some version of the link. They divide over whether the core was written for the reform, came south from the northern kingdom after 722 BCE, or was largely shaped later, during and after the exile. Traditional readers attribute the book to Moses.

**Treaty form.**

| Treaty part | What it does | In Deuteronomy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Preamble | Names the sovereign and the setting | 1:1–5 |
| Historical prologue | Recounts what the sovereign has done | 1:6–4:43, recalled again through 5–11 |
| General stipulation | Demands exclusive loyalty, called "love" | 5–11, with the Shema at 6:4–5 |
| Specific stipulations | The laws | 12–26 |
| Deposit and public reading | Where the text is kept and when it's read | 10:1–5 and 31:9–13, 24–26. It's read every seventh year, at the feast of Booths, to men, women, children and foreigners. |
| Witnesses | Called to observe the oath | Heaven and earth (4:26, 30:19, 31:28, 32:1) |
| Blessings and curses | The consequences of keeping or breaking it | 27–28 |

William Moran (1963) showed that "love" in ancient treaties names political loyalty, which is the sense of "you shall love the LORD your God" (6:5). CONTESTED: Whether the closer model is the Hittite treaties of the second millennium BCE (Mendenhall), which would support an early date, or the Neo-Assyrian treaties of the seventh century (Weinfeld), which fit Josiah's time.

**Chapter 6 and the household.** The Shema, "Hear, O Israel" (6:4), is followed by its instructions: keep these words on your heart, teach them to your children, talk about them sitting in your house and walking on the road, lying down and rising, bind them on your hand and write them on your doorposts and gates (6:6–9). Teaching happens in the household, and no priest is present. When a child asks what the laws mean, the answer is a story: "We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt" (6:20–21).

**Two Decalogues.** Deuteronomy 5 repeats the ten words with differences. Exodus says "remember" the sabbath and Deuteronomy says "observe" it. Exodus grounds it in creation, and Deuteronomy grounds it in the memory of slavery: "that your male and female slave may rest as well as you. You shall remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt" (5:14–15). Deuteronomy's coveting command puts the wife first, gives her her own verb, and adds the neighbor's field (5:21).

**The prosperity warning.** The people are told they'll inherit cities they didn't build, houses full of good things they didn't fill, cisterns they didn't dig, and vineyards and olive trees they didn't plant, and they're warned that this is exactly when they'll forget (6:10–12). Later the warning is sharper. Don't say "my power and the might of my hand have gotten me this wealth" (8:17).

**Chapter 12: one place.** Sacrifice is limited to "the place the LORD your God will choose" (12:5). Jerusalem is never named. Every other altar is to be destroyed. Because people far from the one altar can no longer slaughter there, ordinary slaughter at home is made secular: eat meat in your towns as you like, and pour out the blood (12:15, 20–25). The tithe can be converted to money if the place is too far, carried there, and spent on "whatever you desire, oxen or sheep or wine or strong drink," eaten in celebration with the household and the Levite (14:22–27). Every third year the tithe stays in the local towns for the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow (14:28–29, 26:12). The local Levites are left without altars and appear again and again as dependents "within your gates." A Levite who comes to the central place may serve there equally (18:6–8). In Josiah's reform, the priests of the outlying shrines didn't serve at the Jerusalem altar (2 Kgs 23:9), so the law and the history don't match.

**Chapter 17: the law of the king.** Israel may set a king over itself, one God chooses and one of their own people (17:14–15). He must not acquire many horses or send the people back to Egypt to get them. He must not acquire many wives, "lest his heart turn away." He must not acquire much silver and gold (17:16–17). When he takes the throne he's to write out a copy of the law for himself, keep it with him and read it all his life, "that his heart may not be lifted up above his brothers" (17:18–20). There's no enforcement clause. Nobody is named who can remove a king who breaks it. Solomon breaks all four: he imports horses from Egypt (1 Kgs 10:28–29), marries Pharaoh's daughter (3:1) and has seven hundred wives (11:3), and makes silver "as common as stone" in Jerusalem (10:27).

**Chapter 15: release.** Every seventh year debts between Israelites are released. A foreigner's debt can still be collected (15:1–3). The chapter says both "there will be no poor among you" (15:4) and "the poor will never cease out of the land" (15:11). The first is conditional on obedience, and the second is the reason for the command to open your hand. Jesus quotes the second (Mark 14:7). A Hebrew man or a Hebrew woman sold into service goes free in the seventh year (15:12). They're not to be sent away empty-handed. The master must supply them liberally from the flock, the threshing floor and the winepress, "as the LORD your God has blessed you" (15:13–14). A slave who chooses to stay has the ear pierced with an awl against the door, and "to your female slave you shall do the same" (15:16–17). CONTESTED: Whether Deuteronomy revises Exodus 21:7, which kept a sold daughter from going free, or addresses a different case. An escaped slave who takes refuge with you isn't to be returned to his master and may live wherever he chooses (23:15–16). Neighboring law ran the other way. The Code of Hammurabi makes harboring a runaway slave a capital offense (§§15–20). In Jeremiah 34 the king and the people of Jerusalem release their slaves under siege and then take them back, and Jeremiah answers with a "release" to the sword. Nehemiah 5 is the debt crisis of II.2.

Content note: the next two entries concern war law, the killing of noncombatants and captive women.

**Chapters 7 and 20: war.** The seven nations of the land are to be "devoted to destruction," ḥerem, with no covenant and no mercy (7:1–2). The stated reason is contamination: "they would turn away your sons from following me" (7:4), and later, "so that they may not teach you to do according to all their abominable practices" (20:18). The same chapter that commands destruction also forbids intermarriage with these peoples (7:3), which assumes they'll still be there. War law has two tiers. A distant city is offered peace first, and if it accepts, its people become forced labor. If it refuses, its men are killed and its women, children and livestock taken (20:10–15). The cities of the land itself are to leave nothing alive that breathes (20:16–17). Fruit trees may not be cut down in a siege: "Are the trees in the field human, that they should be besieged by you?" (20:19). Some men are sent home before battle: those with a new house, an unharvested vineyard, a fiancée, or fear (20:5–8).

A woman taken captive may be married. She shaves her head, cuts her nails, puts off her captive's clothing and mourns her father and mother a full month first. If her husband later doesn't want her, he must let her go where she wants and may not sell her, "since you have humbled her" (21:10–14).

The ḥerem idea isn't unique to Israel. The Moabite king Mesha's inscription (about 840 BCE) boasts of devoting an Israelite town to his god and killing seven thousand people. CONTESTED: Archaeology doesn't support a sweeping conquest. Excavations at Jericho (Kathleen Kenyon, 1950s) and Ai found no city of the right period matching the account, and many archaeologists see Israel emerging gradually in the central highlands. Judges 1 itself lists peoples not driven out. Kenneth Kitchen, James Hoffmeier and others defend more of the narrative's historical basis.

**Chapters 22 and 24: marriage law.** A husband who claims his bride wasn't a virgin goes before the elders, and her parents bring out the cloth from the wedding night. If his charge is false, he's fined a hundred shekels, paid to her father, and can never divorce her. If it's true, she's stoned (22:13–21). If a betrothed woman lies with another man in the city, both are put to death, on the reasoning that she didn't cry out. If it happens in the field, only the man dies, because "she cried out and there was no one to rescue her" (22:23–27). The field gives her the benefit of the doubt, and the city doesn't. A man who seizes an unbetrothed virgin pays her father fifty shekels of silver, must marry her, and can never divorce her (22:28–29). Exodus 22:16–17 lets the father refuse the marriage. Deuteronomy doesn't mention refusal.

A husband who finds "some indecency" in his wife may write her a certificate of divorce and send her away (24:1). She may remarry, but she can't later return to the first husband (24:2–4). The certificate lets her prove she's free. Only the husband can initiate. The phrase ʿervat davar, "some indecency," divided the rabbis: the house of Shammai allowed divorce only for unchastity, and the house of Hillel for anything, even a spoiled dish (Mishnah Gittin 9:10). Jesus is asked about this verse (Matt 19:3–9, Mark 10:2–12).

Comparison with neighboring law cuts in more than one direction. The Middle Assyrian Laws (A 55) let a raped virgin's father hand the rapist's own wife over to be raped and give his daughter to the rapist in marriage. The Code of Hammurabi (§130) puts to death a man who rapes a betrothed virgin and declares the woman free of blame. "Better than its neighbors" and "good" are different claims, and some neighbors weren't worse.

**Chapters 27–28: blessings and curses.** Twelve curses are pronounced from Mount Ebal with the people answering "Amen" (27:11–26). Almost every one names an act done where no one can see it: an idol made in secret, a boundary stone moved, a blind man sent the wrong way, justice perverted for the foreigner, the orphan and the widow. In chapter 28 the blessings take fourteen verses (28:1–14) and the curses fifty-four (28:15–68), a little under four times as many. The curses end with siege, famine so severe that parents eat their children (Content note: 28:53–57), and the people shipped back to Egypt to be sold, with no buyer (28:68). Several curses closely match the Succession Treaty of Esarhaddon of Assyria (672 BCE), copies of which were found at Nimrud in 1955 and at Tell Tayinat in 2009. The sky as bronze and the earth as iron (28:23) is one match, and the order of several curses is another. Chapter 13, which requires reporting and executing even a brother, son, daughter, wife or friend who secretly urges the worship of other gods (13:6–11), has close parallels in the treaty's demand that subjects report anyone, even family, who speaks against the king. The book closes its argument with a choice: "I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life" (30:19). Just before, it says the commandment "is not too hard for you, neither is it far off. It is not in heaven" (30:11–12).

Two smaller laws matter for TKC. "Fathers shall not be put to death for their children, nor children for their fathers" (24:16). Wages are owed to a poor worker, Israelite or foreigner, the same day, before sunset (24:14–15).

Correction to the old Companion: it says the curses run "more than four times as many" verses as the blessings. Fifty-four to fourteen is just under four times.

**Reception.** Martin Noth argued in 1943 that Joshua through Kings form a "Deuteronomistic History," written to judge Israel's kings by this book's standard, especially chapter 12. Jesus answers all three temptations with Deuteronomy (Matt 4:1–11, quoting 8:3, 6:16 and 6:13) and names the Shema as the first commandment (Mark 12:29–30). John Winthrop closed "A Model of Christian Charity" (1630) by paraphrasing 30:15–20, "Therefore let us choose life," and Puritan covenant theology read New England through the book's terms (Part V). Abolitionists cited 23:15–16 against the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, and 24:7 with Exodus 21:16 against kidnapping and the slave trade.
