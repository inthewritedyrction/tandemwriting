### II.3 Deep dives: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus and Deuteronomy

Four books of the Torah get full treatment because the Empire's founding logic draws on all four, and because the Trials name two of their three phases after Genesis and Exodus (TR). Numbers is covered in II.2. Each entry follows its book in order, marks where readers divide, and notes where the text has been put to uses it doesn't support. Corrections to the old Companion are stated where they fall. Content notes come before the hardest material, which is reported and not quoted at length.

#### Genesis

Genesis runs from creation to Joseph's coffin in Egypt (50:26). Chapters 1–11, the primeval history, are about humanity as a whole. Chapters 12–50 follow one family across four generations. There's no king in the book, no priesthood and no temple. Everything happens in households, and households decide who inherits. CONTESTED: Most critical scholars read Genesis as woven from more than one source, and the two creation accounts are the clearest seam. The classic documentary hypothesis has been heavily revised since the 1970s, and traditional readers hold to a single author.

**Two creation accounts.** The first runs from 1:1 to 2:3 (many scholars end it at 2:4a). The second starts at 2:4b and runs through chapter 2. They're placed side by side and never harmonized.

| | Genesis 1:1–2:3 | Genesis 2:4b–25 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name for God | Elohim, "God" | YHWH Elohim, "the LORD God" |
| Starting point | Dark water, the deep | Dry ground with no plants, no rain and no one to work it |
| Order | Light, sky, land and plants, lights, sea creatures and birds, land animals, then humans last | The human first, then a garden, trees, animals, then the woman |
| How God makes | By speaking and separating | By shaping dust, breathing, planting and building |
| Humans | Male and female created together, both in God's image (1:27) | The human (ha'adam) shaped from the ground (ha'adamah), the woman built from his side |
| Their task | Be fruitful, fill the earth, subdue it, have dominion (1:28) | Work the garden and keep it (2:15) |
| Food | Plants only, for humans and animals (1:29–30) | Every tree but one |
| Ends with | God resting on the seventh day | Two people naked and unashamed |

**The helper.** In 2:18 God says it isn't good for the human to be alone and makes "a helper fit for him," ʿezer kenegdo. The noun ʿezer appears about twenty times in the Hebrew Bible, and most of those uses describe God helping Israel (Exod 18:4, Deut 33:29, Ps 121:1–2). It names the one who supplies what the other can't, so "helper" doesn't mean assistant. Kenegdo means "corresponding to him" or "opposite him." The word usually translated "rib" (tsela) means "side" nearly everywhere else, including the sides of the tabernacle (Exod 26:20). The man's line in 2:23 is the first human speech the Bible quotes, and it's a poem of recognition.

**The primeval history in four movements.**

| Movement | Chapters | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Creation | 1–2 | Two accounts of the making of the world and the first humans |
| The garden and after | 3–4 | The fruit, the pronouncements, the expulsion. Cain kills Abel. Lamech boasts of killing a man for wounding him (4:23–24). |
| The flood | 5–9 | A genealogy to Noah, the "sons of God" and human women (6:1–4), the flood, a covenant with every living creature, Noah's drunkenness and the curse on Canaan |
| Nations and Babel | 10–11 | The table of nations, the tower, and a genealogy that narrows to Terah, Abram and Sarai, who is barren (11:30) |

**Genesis 3.** The serpent asks a question, and the woman answers it, adding that they mustn't even touch the tree (3:3). She eats and gives fruit to her husband, "who was with her" (3:6). God questions the man first. He blames "the woman whom you gave to be with me" (3:12), and she blames the serpent. The pronouncements follow, and only two things are cursed: the serpent (3:14) and the ground, "because of you" (3:17). The woman isn't cursed, and the man isn't either. Her pain in childbearing and his toil on the land are the same Hebrew noun, ʿitstsavon (3:16, 3:17). The man names her Eve, Ḥawwah, "mother of all living," only after the judgment (3:20). The chapter never uses the word "sin." Its first appearance is 4:7, spoken to Cain. The fruit is never called an apple. The apple came from a Latin pun on malum, which means both "evil" and "apple."

CONTESTED: Genesis 3:16 ends "your desire shall be for your husband, and he shall rule over you." One reading takes it as prescription: God sets male rule in place as the order of things. The other takes it as description: the verse names what the break has done to the pair and what will now go wrong between them. The word for "desire" (teshuqah) appears only three times in the Hebrew Bible: here, in 4:7, where sin's desire is for Cain and he must rule over it, and in Song of Songs 7:10, where it's the man's desire for the woman. Readers who line up 3:16 with 4:7 hear a struggle for control. Readers who start from the Song hear longing. 1 Timothy 2:13–14 reads the garden story as grounds for restricting women's teaching, and how far that argument reaches is its own dispute (I.6).

**Four generations and seven promises.** The ancestral story runs through Abraham and Sarah, Isaac and Rebekah, Jacob with Leah, Rachel, Bilhah and Zilpah, and finally Joseph and his brothers, with Judah rising beside him. A promise is made or renewed at each turn.

| To whom | Where | What's promised | Sign or terms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Noah and every living creature | 9:8–17 | No flood will destroy the earth again | The bow in the clouds. No conditions. |
| Abram at the call | 12:1–3 | A land he'll be shown, a great nation, a great name, blessing for "all the families of the earth" | He has to leave |
| Abram, between the pieces | 15 | Descendants like the stars, the land, and four hundred years of oppression in a foreign land first | A smoking firepot and a torch pass between the halves. God alone passes through, and Abram sleeps. |
| Abraham, circumcision | 17 | Many nations, kings, an everlasting covenant, a son by Sarah | Circumcision of every male, slaves included. Abram and Sarai are renamed. |
| Hagar | 16:10–12, 21:17–18 | Descendants too many to count, and a great nation from Ishmael | None |
| Jacob at Bethel | 28:13–15 | The land, descendants like dust, "I am with you" | Jacob answers with a conditional vow of his own (28:20–22) |
| Judah | 49:8–12 | His brothers will bow, and the scepter won't leave him | Spoken by the dying Jacob, not by God. The phrase "until Shiloh comes" (49:10) is one of the most disputed in the book. |

Hagar's is the only one of the seven spoken to a woman. She's also the only person in scripture who gives God a name: El-roi, "the God who sees me" (16:13).

**The family line.** In every generation the promise passes over the firstborn.

| Firstborn | Passed over for | Where |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cain | Seth, after Abel's death | 4:25 |
| Ishmael | Isaac | 21:10–12 |
| Esau | Jacob | 25:23, 27 |
| Reuben | Judah for rule and Joseph for the double portion | 49:3–4, 8–12, 22–26 (and 1 Chr 5:1–2) |
| Manasseh | Ephraim, by Jacob's crossed hands | 48:13–20 |
| Zerah | Perez, who breaks out first at birth | 38:27–30 |

Women decide several of these. Sarah demands Hagar's expulsion, and God tells Abraham to do as she says (21:12). Rebekah engineers Jacob's blessing and takes the risk on herself: "Upon me be your curse, my son" (27:13). Tamar forces Judah to acknowledge her son. INTERPRETATION: Genesis treats inheritance as election and never as law. The legal heir is the one person the story reliably doesn't choose.

**Six women.**

Hagar. An Egyptian slave woman belonging to Sarai, who gives her to Abram as a wife (16:3). When Hagar conceives, Sarai "dealt harshly with her," the same verb used for Egypt's treatment of Israel (Gen 15:13, Exod 1:11–12), and Hagar runs. A messenger finds her at a spring, sends her back, and promises her descendants. She names God there. Years later, after Isaac is born, Sarah demands that Hagar and Ishmael be sent away, and Abraham sends them into the wilderness with bread and a skin of water (21:9–14). When the water runs out she puts the boy under a bush and sits a bowshot away so she won't have to watch him die. God hears the boy and opens her eyes to a well. She later takes a wife for Ishmael from Egypt (21:21), acting as the head of her own household. Paul reads her as an allegory of slavery (Gal 4:21–31). Phyllis Trible counted her story among the "texts of terror" (1984), and Delores Williams built a womanist theology on her survival in Sisters in the Wilderness (1993).

Correction to the old Companion: Hagar isn't "driven out twice." She flees once (ch. 16) and is expelled once (ch. 21).

Sarah. Barren from her first mention (11:30). Twice Abraham presents her as his sister and she's taken into a king's household, in Egypt (12:10–20) and in Gerar (ch. 20). Isaac later does the same with Rebekah (ch. 26). She laughs at the promise of a son and then denies laughing (18:12–15). The name Isaac, "he laughs," is given by God (17:19) and by Abraham (21:3), and Sarah plays on it: "God has made laughter for me" (21:6). She's absent from the binding of Isaac (ch. 22), and her death follows in the next chapter. A later rabbinic tradition connects the two. Her grave is the first land Abraham owns in Canaan, the cave of Machpelah, bought for four hundred shekels (ch. 23).

Rebekah. Chosen at a well because she offers water to a stranger's ten camels (24:14–20). Her family asks whether she'll leave at once, and she answers "I will go" (24:58). INTERPRETATION: The question put to her is about timing, and the marriage itself had already been agreed. She's barren for twenty years (25:20–26). When the pregnancy becomes painful she goes "to inquire of the LORD" herself and receives the oracle that the older will serve the younger (25:22–23). She acts on it in chapter 27. Her death is never narrated. Only her nurse's is (35:8).

Leah. Her eyes are described with a word, rakkot, that means either "weak" or "tender" (29:17). Her father substitutes her for Rachel on the wedding night. The text says she was "hated" (29:31, often softened to "unloved"), and the LORD opens her womb. She names her first four sons as running commentary on her marriage: seen, heard, joined, and finally Judah, "this time I will praise the LORD" (29:32–35). She buys a night with Jacob from Rachel for mandrakes (30:14–16). Levi and Judah are her sons, so the priesthood and the kings both come through her. She's buried beside Jacob at Machpelah (49:31). Rachel is buried by the road (35:19).

Content note: the next entry concerns sexual violence and a massacre.

Dinah. Leah's daughter, the only daughter of Jacob the text names. She goes out "to see the daughters of the land," and Shechem "took her and lay with her and humbled her" (34:1–2). CONTESTED: Most translators and readers take this as rape. Lyn Bechtel (1994) argued the verb ʿinnah marks social shaming and not necessarily force, and others answer that the sequence of verbs signals force. Shechem then wants to marry her. Her brothers demand that the city's men be circumcised, and while they're recovering Simeon and Levi kill them and take the women and children (34:25–29). Jacob complains about his reputation (34:30). The brothers get the last word: "Should he treat our sister like a prostitute?" Dinah never speaks, and no one asks what she wants. Anita Diamant's novel The Red Tent (1997) gives her a voice.

Tamar. Judah's daughter-in-law (ch. 38). Her husband Er dies. His brother Onan is obliged to give her a child in Er's name and won't, and he dies. Judah keeps his third son from her and sends her back to her father's house as a widow. She veils herself and waits on the road, Judah takes her for a prostitute, and she keeps his seal, cord and staff as a pledge. When she's found pregnant Judah orders her burned. She sends the pledge with a message: "Identify whose these are." Judah answers, "She is more righteous than I" (38:26). Her son Perez is an ancestor of David (Ruth 4:18–22), and Matthew names her in Jesus' genealogy (1:3). The custom she invokes, a brother's duty to a dead man's widow, becomes law in Deuteronomy 25:5–10. Onan's offense was refusing his brother an heir. Using his name for masturbation is a later misreading.

Correction to the old Companion: Three matriarchs are barren, Sarah (11:30), Rebekah (25:21) and Rachel (29:31). The old text said "four of the central women." Leah is the fertile one.

Correction to the old Companion: it says Sarah "names her son for laughter." God gives the name and Abraham confers it. Sarah's line about laughter comes after.

**Joseph's famine administration.** Joseph reads Pharaoh's dreams and is put over the land, and in the seven good years he stores a fifth of the harvest (41:34–36). When the famine comes, the people buy grain with all their money (47:14). When the money's gone they hand over their livestock (47:16–17). In the second year they offer the last thing they have: "Buy us and our land for food, and we with our land will be slaves to Pharaoh" (47:19). Joseph buys every field in Egypt for the crown. The Hebrew text says he moved the people into the cities (47:21), and the Samaritan Pentateuch and the Greek say he made them slaves. The priests keep their land and their allowance (47:22). A fifth of every harvest goes to Pharaoh permanently, "to this day" (47:26), and the people thank him: "You have saved our lives" (47:25). Meanwhile Jacob's family is given land in Goshen (47:11, 27).

Exodus opens on the result. A new king who didn't know Joseph sets Israel to forced labor building store cities (Exod 1:8–11). CONTESTED: Whether Genesis presents Joseph's policy as wise government or as the start of a trap. The text praises his wisdom and records the cost without comment.

**Fault lines and documented misuses.** Some disputes over Genesis are real scholarly arguments, and they're marked CONTESTED above. Four uses of the book are a different kind of thing (I.6, category 4). The text doesn't say what they claim, and the history of the claim can be traced.

| The claim | What Genesis says | How it was used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The curse of Ham | Ham sees his drunken father naked and tells his brothers. Noah wakes and curses Canaan, Ham's son: "a servant of servants shall he be" (9:25). Ham himself isn't cursed. Nothing in the passage mentions skin, color or Africa. In Genesis, Canaanites are the people of the land Israel will occupy. | David Goldenberg's The Curse of Ham (Princeton, 2003) traces how later interpreters attached blackness to Ham and slavery to blackness, and shows that the supposed derivation of "Ham" from a word for "black" doesn't hold. The link was retrofitted onto the text. In the Atlantic world it became a standard justification for enslaving Africans, and Stephen Haynes documents its central place in American proslavery argument in Noah's Curse (Oxford, 2002). |
| The order of creation as permanent rank | In chapter 1 humans are made last and together. In chapter 2 the man is formed first and the woman last, with the animals in between. | Read as a decree that women are subordinate by nature, in law as well as in church. INTERPRETATION: Order alone settles rank in neither account, since in chapter 1 the animals come before both humans. The separate New Testament argument from creation order (1 Tim 2:13) is CONTESTED and belongs to I.6. |
| Dominion as license | "Have dominion" (1:26, 28) sits beside "work it and keep it" (2:15). The humans of chapter 1 are given plants to eat, and meat isn't permitted until after the flood (9:3). | Read as permission to use the earth without limit. Lynn White Jr. made the charge famous in "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis" (Science, 1967). The stewardship reading is the main answer to it. |
| Childbearing as a sentence | Pain is announced in 3:16, with the same noun as the man's toil in 3:17. | Read as a punishment women are meant to bear. When James Young Simpson began using chloroform in labor in 1847, he published an answer to religious objections to it. How widespread clerical opposition really was is debated by historians. |

**Other fault lines.** CONTESTED: What "the image of God" (1:27) means. Proposals include a capacity like reason, a royal status, a function as God's representative, and a relationship. The text doesn't define it, and each definition has been used to decide who fully qualifies. CONTESTED: What Sodom is condemned for. The narrative centers on a mob's violent demand against guests under a host's protection (19:4–11), and Ezekiel names Sodom's guilt as "pride, excess of food, and prosperous ease," and not helping the poor and needy (Ezek 16:49). CONTESTED: The binding of Isaac (ch. 22). Readers have taken it as a test passed, a polemic against child sacrifice, or a horror the text leaves unexplained. Jon Levenson's The Death and Resurrection of the Beloved Son (1993) is the standard study. Sarah is never told, and in the narrative Abraham and Sarah never speak to each other again. CONTESTED: The genre of chapters 1–11. Communities differ on whether they're historical report, theological narrative or a kind of writing modern categories don't divide well. Two other points belong here. Cain is protected with a mark instead of executed (4:15), and the first city in the book is founded by him (4:17). The flood story counts in two ways at once, pairs of animals and sevens (6:19–20, 7:2–3), forty days and a hundred and fifty (7:17, 7:24), which source critics read as two accounts woven together. INTERPRETATION: Babel (11:1–9) is a single people with a single language building so they won't be scattered. Read beside an empire that standardizes speech and centralizes construction, it's the most politically pointed story in the first eleven chapters.

The Dominions are named after a verse about stewardship of the earth (AM). INFERENCE: The verse is Genesis 1:28 or its preface in 1:26, the only place in Genesis where humans are told to "have dominion." OPEN QUESTION: Who named the Dominions, and does the Temple teach the verse as rule or as care?
