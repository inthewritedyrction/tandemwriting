#### Exodus

Exodus moves from a labor population inside an empire (1–15) to the wilderness (15–18) and to a covenant at Sinai, followed by the plans and building of the tabernacle (19–40). The golden calf (32–34) sits between the instructions for the tabernacle and its construction. The Hebrew name, Shemot, means "names," from its first line, and the Greek exodos means "a going out." There's no external record of the departure as the book describes it, and proposals for its date, scale and route vary widely. This entry treats the narrative as scripture and literature and prints no reconstruction as history.

**Six women before Moses.** The deliverance begins with women, and most of them act against a royal order.

| Who | Where | What she does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shiphrah and Puah | 1:15–21 | Ordered to kill Hebrew boys at birth, they "feared God" and didn't. Questioned by the king, they tell him Hebrew women give birth before the midwife arrives. God gives them households. |
| Moses' mother | 2:1–10 | Hides him three months, then puts him in a basket in the reeds. She's paid wages by Pharaoh's daughter to nurse her own son. She's named Jochebed only later (6:20, Num 26:59). |
| His sister | 2:4–8 | Watches from a distance and offers Pharaoh's daughter a Hebrew nurse |
| Pharaoh's daughter | 2:5–10 | Sees the child crying, pities him, knowingly keeps a Hebrew boy alive against her father's order, and names him |
| Zipporah | 2:16–22, 4:24–26, 18:2–6 | A Midianite priest's daughter, one of seven sisters Moses defends at a well. She saves his life in the night scene below. |

The midwives are named, and the king isn't. CONTESTED: "Hebrew midwives" (1:15) can also be read as "midwives to the Hebrews," which would make them Egyptian. The Greek translation reads it that way. Whether the sister of chapter 2 is Miriam is also a reading. She's unnamed there, and Miriam is first named in 15:20, as "the sister of Aaron."

Pharaoh's stated fear is numbers: the Israelites are "too many and too mighty" and might join an enemy (1:9–10). The policy escalates from forced labor to harsher labor to killing newborn boys.

**The name.** Moses is working his father-in-law's flock in Midian when the call comes. What God says first is a report of having heard: "I have seen the affliction of my people... I have heard their cry because of their taskmasters" (3:7). At the bush Moses asks what he should say when Israel asks God's name. The answer is ʾehyeh ʾasher ʾehyeh (3:14). "I am who I am" is the familiar rendering. The Hebrew verb form can also mean "I will be what I will be," so the tense is genuinely open. The next verse gives the name itself, four consonants, YHWH (3:15). Jewish readers traditionally don't pronounce it and say Adonai, "my Lord," or HaShem, "the Name," in its place. Most English Bibles print LORD in small capitals wherever it stands. "Jehovah" is a hybrid of the four consonants with the vowels of Adonai, and "Yahweh" is a scholarly reconstruction. CONTESTED: 6:3 says God wasn't known to the ancestors by this name, though Genesis uses it throughout. Source critics take that as a seam between traditions, and others read it as a claim about the name's meaning being revealed.

**Five objections.** Moses argues back five times: who am I (3:11), what's your name (3:13), they won't believe me (4:1), I'm "heavy of mouth" (4:10), and please send someone else (4:13). Only the last makes God angry, and the answer is Aaron.

**The bridegroom of blood.** On the road back to Egypt, at a lodging place, "the LORD met him and sought to put him to death" (4:24). Zipporah takes a flint, cuts off her son's foreskin, touches it to "his feet," and says, "Surely you are a bridegroom of blood to me." After that the LORD lets him alone (4:24–26). The text doesn't say whom God was attacking (Moses or the son), whose feet she touched, why, or what her words mean. Every translation that makes it clear has supplied the answers. It's left unsmoothed here.

**The signs.** Ten plagues run from 7:14 to 12:30. Pharaoh's magicians match the first signs, the staff turned to a serpent, water turned to blood, and the frogs. They fail at the gnats and tell Pharaoh, "This is the finger of God" (8:19). From the flies on, the plagues distinguish Goshen from the rest of Egypt. Before the hail, some of Pharaoh's officials heed the warning and bring their workers and livestock inside (9:20–21), and before the locusts his courtiers beg him to let the people go (10:7). God says the last plague is judgment "on all the gods of Egypt" (12:12). INTERPRETATION: The plague cycle works as a contest over legitimacy. Two powers claim the right to command the same people, and the narrative keeps score.

**The hardening.** Who hardens Pharaoh's heart is the oldest problem in the book, and it isn't resolved here. There are three patterns. God announces in advance that he'll harden it (4:21, 7:3). Pharaoh hardens his own heart (8:15, 8:32, 9:34). And in other places his heart simply "was hardened" (7:13, 7:22, 9:7). From the sixth plague on, the narrator mostly says God hardened it (9:12, 10:20, 10:27, 11:10). Three verbs share the work: one means to strengthen (ḥazaq), one to make heavy (kaved) and one to make hard (qashah), and translations flatten them into one word.

**Passover.** Chapter 12 sets up the rite before the escape happens. It belongs to the household. Each family takes a lamb, small households share one, the blood goes on the doorposts and lintel, and the meal is eaten standing, dressed for the road. There's no priesthood yet (Aaron and his sons aren't ordained until Lev 8). The rite builds in the child's question, "What do you mean by this service?" (12:26, 13:8, 13:14), and the answer is a story. A "mixed multitude" leaves with Israel (12:38), and a resident foreigner who's circumcised may keep the Passover under "one law" for native and stranger alike (12:48–49).

**The Egyptians' silver.** Israel "asks" (shaʾal) the Egyptians for silver, gold and clothing and so "plunders" them (3:21–22, 11:2, 12:35–36). The King James "borrow" made it read like a trick. CONTESTED: Ancient readers, Philo among them (Life of Moses 1.141) and a story in the Talmud (Sanhedrin 91a), took the goods as back wages for generations of unpaid labor. Others read the verb as asking for a parting gift, and still others take the plunder language at face value.

**The firstborn.** Content note: the death of children. The last plague kills every firstborn in Egypt, "from the firstborn of Pharaoh who sits on his throne to the firstborn of the slave girl who is behind the handmill," along with the firstborn of the livestock (11:5, 12:29). God had already called Israel "my firstborn son" (4:22–23). Afterward every firstborn male in Israel belongs to God and has to be redeemed (13:1–2, 11–16). The text doesn't soften the deaths, and neither does this entry.

**The sea, twice.** The crossing is told in prose (13:17–14:31) and then sung (15:1–18), with Miriam's short song after it (15:20–21).

| The Egyptians | Chariot wheels clog, the army turns to flee, the water returns at morning | Horse and rider are thrown into the sea, they sink like a stone, the earth swallows them |

Miriam is called "the prophet" (15:20), the first woman in the Torah given that title. She leads the women with a hand drum and dancing, and her two lines match the song's opening. CONTESTED: Frank Moore Cross and David Noel Freedman (1955) argued that the song was once credited to Miriam, and feminist scholars including Phyllis Trible and Rita Burns developed the case. One Qumran manuscript (4Q365) has extra lines of a song for Miriam after 15:21. It's a proposal and not a result. Many scholars count the song among the oldest poetry in the Bible, and that dating is also disputed. Miriam's challenge to Moses and her exclusion from the camp are in Numbers 12 (II.2). Micah 6:4 names her with Moses and Aaron as the three God sent.

**After the sea.** Three days later there's no water (15:22–25). By chapter 16 the people say they ate their fill of bread in Egypt (16:3). Manna comes with a rule that nobody can hoard it, since what's kept overnight rots, except on the day before the sabbath (16:16–30). Moses' father-in-law Jethro watches him judging the people alone from morning to evening and tells him to appoint officers over thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens and hear only the hard cases himself (18:13–26). The first administrative structure in Israel comes on the advice of a Midianite priest.

**Sinai in treaty form.** George Mendenhall (1954–1955) compared the Sinai covenant to Hittite treaties between an overlord and a vassal: a preamble naming the overlord, a history of what he's done for the vassal, stipulations, provisions for keeping and reading the text, witnesses, and blessings and curses. The Decalogue opens with the first two parts in one sentence: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (20:2). The law is presented as a response to something already done.

The text calls them the "ten words" (34:28) and doesn't number them. Traditions divide them differently.

| Tradition | First | Where the tenth falls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Jewish | "I am the LORD your God who brought you out" (20:2) | All coveting is one commandment |
| Catholic and Lutheran | No other gods, with the ban on images inside it | Coveting is split in two, the neighbor's wife and then his goods |
| Reformed, Orthodox and Anglican | No other gods, with images as the second | All coveting is one commandment |

The sabbath (20:8–11) is labor law as much as worship. Rest is owed to the son and daughter, the male and female slave, the livestock and the resident foreigner. Exodus grounds it in creation (20:11), and 23:12 adds a second reason, "that the son of your female slave and the foreigner may be refreshed." The coveting command lists the neighbor's house first and then his wife, his male and female slaves, his ox and his donkey (20:17), so the wife appears inside an inventory of what a man owns. Deuteronomy puts her first and gives her a separate verb (5:21). After the ten words, the people stand at a distance and ask Moses to speak to them instead of God, "lest we die" (20:18–19). The mediator is their request. The covenant is then read aloud, the people answer "All that the LORD has spoken we will do," and Moses throws blood on the altar and on the people (24:3–8).

**The covenant code and its slavery laws.** Chapters 21–23 begin with slavery. A Hebrew man who's bought serves six years and goes free in the seventh with nothing owed (21:2). If he came married, his wife leaves with him, and if the master gave him a wife, she and the children stay with the master (21:3–4). If he chooses to stay, his ear is pierced at the door with an awl and he serves for life (21:5–6). A daughter sold by her father "shall not go out as the male slaves do" (21:7). She has protections: she can't be sold to foreigners, and if the man takes another wife he can't reduce her food, clothing or her third due, ʿonah (traditionally "marital rights," and the meaning is disputed). If he does, she goes free without payment (21:8–11).

Penalties follow status. Kidnapping a person to sell is a capital crime (21:16). A master who beats a slave to death is punished, but if the slave survives a day or two there's no penalty, "for he is his money" (21:20–21). A slave who loses an eye or a tooth goes free (21:26–27). An ox that kills a free person is stoned and its negligent owner may die, and an ox that kills a slave costs its owner thirty shekels paid to the master (21:28–32).

The same code protects the vulnerable. A poor man's cloak taken in pledge must be returned by sunset, "for that is his only covering," and God says he'll hear the man's cry (22:26–27). Foreigners aren't to be wronged or oppressed, "for you know the heart of a foreigner, since you were foreigners in Egypt" (22:21, 23:9). Widows and orphans aren't to be mistreated (22:22–24). Impartiality runs both ways: don't favor the poor man in his lawsuit (23:3), and don't pervert the justice due to the poor (23:6).

All three of these things are true at once. The code doesn't abolish slavery. It also isn't a charter for the plantation system, since it limits Hebrew debt servitude, makes kidnapping a capital crime and frees a slave for a lost tooth, and American slave codes did none of those things. And it gives women fewer exits than men. Permanent slavery of foreigners is authorized elsewhere (Lev 25:44–46), and proslavery writers cited it.

**The calf.** While Moses is on the mountain, the people ask Aaron to make them gods. He collects their gold earrings and makes a calf, they say "These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of Egypt," and Aaron announces "a feast to the LORD" (32:1–5). God tells Moses to stand aside so he can destroy them and make a great nation from Moses instead (32:10). Moses argues God down, appealing to what the Egyptians will say and to the promise to Abraham, and "the LORD relented" (32:11–14). He breaks the tablets, burns the calf, grinds it to powder and makes the people drink it. Aaron's explanation is that he threw the gold in the fire "and out came this calf" (32:24). The Levites rally to Moses and kill about three thousand people, and Moses tells them "today you have been ordained for the LORD," each at the cost of a son or brother (32:28–29). The priesthood's ordination is narrated as a purge. Moses intercedes a second time and offers to be blotted out of God's book with the people (32:31–32). Aaron isn't punished in Exodus. Deuteronomy later says God was angry enough with him to destroy him and Moses prayed for him (Deut 9:20). Afterward Moses asks to see God's glory and is told no one can see God's face and live. He's put in a cleft of the rock and sees God's back (33:18–23). What he receives instead of the face is a spoken self-description, "merciful and gracious, slow to anger," forgiving, and by no means clearing the guilty (34:6–7), which later books quote and argue with (Num 14:18, Jonah 4:2, Ps 103:8). The tablets are rewritten, and the book ends with the tabernacle built from offerings so generous that the people have to be told to stop bringing them (36:5–7).

**Reception.** Enslaved Christians in America made Exodus their book. "Go Down, Moses," with its refrain "Let my people go," was among the first spirituals published, in 1861, and Harriet Tubman was called Moses (Albert Raboteau, Slave Religion, 1978). Slaveholders cited the covenant code and Leviticus 25 to show that scripture regulates slavery and therefore allows it. In 1776 Benjamin Franklin proposed a Great Seal showing Pharaoh drowning in the sea under the motto "Rebellion to tyrants is obedience to God." James Cone made the God of the exodus the center of A Black Theology of Liberation (1970) and God of the Oppressed (1975). Robert Allen Warrior's "Canaanites, Cowboys, and Indians" (Christianity and Crisis, 1989) read the story from the side of the Canaanites, since the exodus ends in a conquest, and for Native Americans the Puritans had played Israel. The civil rights movement preached it constantly. Martin Luther King Jr.'s last sermon, in Memphis on April 3, 1968, ended at Moses' view of the promised land: "I may not get there with you."
