#### Leviticus

Leviticus is priestly instruction delivered at Sinai. The English name comes from the Greek for "about the Levites," though Levites as a group appear only once (25:32–33). The Hebrew name, Wayyiqra, means "and he called." The book falls into offerings (1–7), the ordination of the priests (8–10), purity (11–15), the Day of Atonement (16), the Holiness Code (17–26, named by August Klostermann in 1877) and vows (27). Jacob Milgrom's three-volume Anchor commentary (1991–2001) is the standard reference behind this entry.

INTERPRETATION: In the reparation offering, a wrong against a person can't be cleared at the altar until the person has been repaid with interest.

**Purity.** Ritual impurity in Leviticus is a status, not a moral judgment. It comes from childbirth, bodily discharges, contact with a corpse and certain skin conditions. It's temporary, it spreads by contact, and it ends with washing, waiting and sometimes an offering. Priests incur it too, and they live under stricter rules about corpses (21:1–4, 10–12). Mary Douglas (Purity and Danger, 1966) read the system as a map of categories, with the unclean as whatever doesn't fit its place. She revised her reading of Leviticus in Leviticus as Literature (1999). Jonathan Klawans (Impurity and Sin in Ancient Judaism, 2000) distinguishes ritual impurity, which is unavoidable and not sinful, from moral impurity, which comes from grave sins like sexual transgression, idolatry and bloodshed, defiles the land, and can't be washed off. The later Christian division of the law into moral and ceremonial parts doesn't map neatly onto either.

**Dietary law.** Chapter 11 sorts animals by stated criteria (split hooves and chewing the cud, fins and scales) and never explains them. Proposals include hygiene, separation from neighbors and Douglas's anomaly reading. None is settled, and the only reason the text gives is holiness (11:44–45).

**Skin inspection.** Chapters 13–14 concern tsaraʿat, a surface condition that can appear on skin, cloth and the walls of houses (13:47, 14:33). It isn't leprosy in the modern sense (Hansen's disease), though the Greek lepra carried that name into English. A priest inspects, isolates the person for seven days, inspects again and declares. Someone declared unclean tears their clothes, leaves their hair loose, covers their upper lip, calls out "Unclean, unclean," and lives alone outside the camp (13:45–46). Restoration involves two birds, one killed and one released into the open field (14:4–7). Miriam's skin disease in Numbers 12 belongs here (II.2).

**Chapters 12 and 15 together.** A woman who gives birth to a son is impure for seven days, and then for thirty-three more days she can't touch holy things or enter the sanctuary. For a daughter both numbers double, to fourteen and sixty-six (12:1–5). The text gives no reason, and the explanations offered over the centuries are guesses. At the end she brings a lamb and a bird. If she can't afford a lamb, two birds will do (12:8). Luke records Mary bringing the poorer offering after Jesus' birth (Luke 2:22–24).

Chapter 15 sets out discharges for men and women in matching sections: an abnormal male discharge, a seminal emission, intercourse, menstruation and abnormal female bleeding. A man with an abnormal discharge brings the same two birds as a woman with abnormal bleeding (15:14, 15:29). After intercourse both partners are impure until evening (15:18). Seven days of impurity follow menstruation (15:19). The chapter states its own purpose: to keep the people from defiling the tabernacle among them (15:31). The woman with bleeding who touches Jesus' clothes (Mark 5:25–34) lives under 15:25–30 (Part IV).

**The Day of Atonement.** Chapter 16 opens "after the death of the two sons of Aaron." In chapter 10 Nadab and Abihu had offered "strange fire," and fire came out from the LORD and consumed them. Aaron was silent (10:1–3). He's forbidden to mourn in public, and the next instruction bans wine for priests on duty (10:6–9). On the Day of Atonement the high priest enters the inner sanctuary once a year. The blood of the purification offerings is sprinkled to cleanse the sanctuary itself "because of the uncleannesses of the people of Israel and their transgressions" (16:16). The sanctuary is purged first, and the people's atonement follows from that. Two goats are chosen by lot. One is for the LORD. Over the other, "for Azazel," the high priest confesses all the people's iniquities with both hands on its head, and it's sent into the wilderness (16:8–10, 21–22). The day applies to the resident foreigner as well as the native (16:29).

CONTESTED: What Azazel means. William Tyndale coined "scapegoat" in 1530, reading the word as "the goat that departs." Other proposals are a place name for a rugged cliff, a wilderness demon (Azazel is a fallen angel in 1 Enoch 8–10), and an abstract noun meaning complete removal.

**Chapters 18 and 20.** Chapter 18 is addressed to Israelite men. It opens with a refusal of the practices of Egypt and Canaan (18:3) and closes with the land vomiting out peoples who did them (18:24–30). The list is mostly forbidden unions with kin, set out from the man's side: his mother, his father's wife, his sister, his granddaughter, his aunt, his daughter-in-law, his brother's wife. A man's own daughter isn't named, which scholars have long noticed and explained in different ways. The list also forbids marrying a wife's sister as a rival while the wife lives (18:18), sex during menstruation (18:19), a neighbor's wife (18:20), giving children to Molech (18:21), lying with a male "as with a woman" (18:22), and sex with animals (18:23), the only verse in the chapter that makes a woman the one acting. Chapter 20 assigns penalties to many of the same acts, often death.

The word toʿevah, usually "abomination," covers the whole list (18:26–30). It's used elsewhere for what Egyptians found offensive about eating with Hebrews (Gen 43:32), for unclean food (Deut 14:3), for dishonest weights (Deut 25:16, Prov 11:1) and for haughty eyes and a lying tongue (Prov 6:16–19).

**Chapter 19 in full.** "You shall be holy, for I the LORD your God am holy" (19:2) opens a chapter that puts ritual and ethics side by side without ranking them. Revere your mother and father (mother first) and keep the sabbaths (19:3). Leave the edges of the field unharvested, and leave the gleanings and the fallen grapes, for the poor and the foreigner (19:9–10). Don't steal, lie or swear falsely (19:11–12). Don't hold a hired worker's wages overnight (19:13). Don't curse the deaf or put a stumbling block before the blind (19:14). Don't favor the poor or defer to the great in judgment (19:15). Don't slander, and don't stand by while your neighbor's blood is shed (19:16). Don't hate your brother in your heart, reprove him openly, take no vengeance and bear no grudge, and "love your neighbor as yourself" (19:17–18). Don't mix kinds of cattle, seed or cloth (19:19). Don't make your daughter a prostitute (19:29). Rise before the aged (19:32). The foreigner living with you is to be treated as a native, "and you shall love him as yourself, for you were foreigners in Egypt" (19:33–34). Keep honest scales, weights and measures (19:35–36). Jesus quotes 19:18 as the second great commandment (Mark 12:31), and Ruth gleans under 19:9 (Ruth 2).

Content note: 19:20–22 concerns an enslaved woman. A man who lies with an enslaved woman promised to another man isn't put to death, "because she was not free." There's an inquiry or a penalty (the word is disputed), and he brings a reparation offering. The text says nothing about her.

**Chapter 25: the sabbath year and the Jubilee.** Every seventh year the land rests (25:1–7). After seven times seven years, a horn sounds on the Day of Atonement and the fiftieth year is proclaimed: "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" (25:10). Land returns to the families it came from, "for the land is mine, and you are foreigners and sojourners with me" (25:23). Houses in walled cities are an exception (25:29–30). An impoverished Israelite isn't charged interest (25:35–37), and one who sells himself isn't to be worked as a slave, but as a hired worker until the Jubilee (25:39–43).

The boundary of that protection is printed in the same chapter. Male and female slaves may be bought from the surrounding nations and from foreigners living in the land. They're property, they can be passed to children as an inheritance, and "you may make them slaves forever" (25:44–46). Whether the Jubilee was ever practiced is unknown. Sabbath years are attested in the Second Temple period (Neh 10:31, 1 Macc 6:49, 53), and there's no clear record of a Jubilee being kept.

**Chapter 21: bodies at the altar.** A descendant of Aaron with a "blemish" (mum) may not offer at the altar: a man who's blind or lame, has a mutilated face or a limb too long, a broken foot or hand, a hunched back, dwarfism, a defect of the eye, scabs or crushed testicles (21:16–23). He may still eat the priests' food. The same word marks animals unfit for sacrifice (22:20–25). Saul Olyan (Disability in the Hebrew Bible, 2008) reads the passage as one place where the Hebrew Bible ranks bodies. The same chapter restricts whom a priest may marry, more tightly for the high priest, limits which dead he may mourn, and orders that a priest's daughter who becomes a prostitute be burned (21:7–15). Priests and their households live on set portions of the offerings (22:10–16). The blemish list sits two chapters after 19:14, which protects the deaf and the blind from abuse. Isaiah 56:3–5 later promises eunuchs a name in God's house better than sons and daughters.

**Salt.** Every grain offering is seasoned with salt: "you shall not let the salt of the covenant with your God be missing" (2:13).

**Reception.** Inside the Bible, the release language of chapter 25 returns in Isaiah 61:1–2, and Luke has Jesus read that passage in the synagogue at Nazareth and say it's fulfilled today (Luke 4:16–21). John Bergsma's The Jubilee from Leviticus to Qumran (2007) traces its later uses. Rabbinic Judaism developed the purity, dietary and agricultural laws into living practice, sabbatical-year observance included. In Acts 15 the early church decided which of these laws applied to non-Jewish believers. The Pennsylvania State House bell, cast in 1752 and recast in 1753, carries 25:10. Abolitionists adopted it in the 1830s. The Anti-Slavery Record called it the Liberty Bell in 1835, and a poem William Lloyd Garrison's Liberator printed in 1839 spread the name.

Correction to the old Companion: it says the word "jubilee" is on the bell. The bell reads "Proclaim LIBERTY Throughout all the Land unto all the Inhabitants Thereof" with the reference Lev. XXV. v X. The word jubilee isn't on it.

 The Jubilee 2000 campaign for cancelling the debts of the poorest countries took its name and its argument from chapter 25. Thomas Aquinas divided the Old Testament law into moral, ceremonial and judicial precepts (Summa Theologiae I–II, q. 99), and the Westminster Confession (1646, ch. 19) made the same division Reformed doctrine. Christians who keep some of Leviticus and set aside the rest usually rely on that division, and the text doesn't contain it. The book's purity vocabulary, "unclean" and "leper" above all, has long been used outside its system as an insult for people a community wants to keep out.
