## Chapter 3: Silas

Silas had soaked his shoulder before leaving the apartment. It bought him enough movement to get dressed without help, which was about all the good it did. By the time he reached the Mercer building, rain had worked through his coat, and the muscle under it had started tightening again one slow notch at a time.

Nothing on the outside said Mercer. No sign hung over the door, and nobody had ever painted the name on the glass. Three route marks were cut into the stone beside the frame instead: Access, Passage, and Return, their edges worn smooth by years of hands. People who needed the Mercers already knew the address. Everybody else could keep walking.

He stepped inside one minute before noon and pulled the wet collar away from his neck. One minute early was the only kind of early that didn't look like nerves, and the only kind of late Dante couldn't call late.

A man stood at Lorraine's desk with both palms flat on a route permission she hadn't accepted. Behind him, a courier held a wardcloth package against her hip, keeping it clear of the water dripping off everybody's coats.

“He said I could come through,” the man told Lorraine.

“Then he can put his name on it.” Lorraine slid the permission back under his hands and held out her palm for the courier's package. Her grey locs were wrapped high, leaving the old route marks along her neck bare. Silas had never asked about those marks. In this building, that counted as manners. She checked the number tied to the cloth against her ledger before setting the package out of the man's reach. “Move over. You blocking somebody who finished their business before they got here.”

The man stepped aside just far enough for Silas to pass. His eyes dropped to the bandage across Silas's knuckles, climbed to the swelling under his eye, and went back to Lorraine. Whatever else he'd been about to say could apparently wait. Silas couldn't tell whether the man knew his face or only knew what a face like that usually meant. In Shadowmere, it came to the same thing.

“Mother Rae just left,” Lorraine said.

Silas stopped wiping the rain off his face. Last night Mother Rae had been sitting on a platform at Gutterlight, telling him with two fingers to make it clean. This morning she'd been here before him and gone before he could ask her why. He didn't like being the second stop on anybody's route, least of all hers. “She say what she wanted?”

“Now you know that woman don't tell none of us a damn thing.” Lorraine turned a page in the ledger, already finished giving him whatever she had. She pointed the courier toward a broker waiting beside the inner hall, then caught the man reaching across her desk again without lifting her head. “That permission ain't changed since I handed it back.”

Silas left her to it. A scanner chirped behind him as the broker checked the package. Farther down the hall, somebody was arguing through a half-open office door about a passage that had closed after payment, and the words cut off when the door shut. Silence-thread crossed the ceiling over his head, patched near the third office where the new stitching didn't quite match the old. He'd learned years ago that pausing under it wouldn't get him anything but Lorraine calling his name from the desk.

He passed the brokers' offices and knocked once on Dante's door.

“Come in.”

Silas shut the door behind him and reached for the lock. Halfway there, he felt somebody watching his hand, and he stopped.

A woman sat on his father's sofa with a crushed envelope held against her stomach. Both of her hands were on it. Silas checked that first, before anything else about her, and only then let himself take in the rest. She'd pulled her feet up under the heavy skirt of a mourning dress, though one ruined slipper showed at the hem. Black silk covered her to the wrists and throat. The stones sewn into her collar were finer than anything sold near Gutterlight, finer than anything Silas had seen sold anywhere, and the pins left in her crimson hair had mostly given up holding it out of her face.

“You missed my call,” Dante said.

His father stood behind the desk, tipping a bottle over a short glass. He'd kept his shoes on. By noon they were usually under the desk, and his route coat would be thrown over whatever chair Silas normally took. The coat was there now, but the shoes were still on his feet and the Mercer keys still hung from his belt. A man who planned to sit all afternoon took his keys off. Dante had either just come in or expected to go back out, and he hadn't decided which one he wanted Silas to believe.

“You know how your wife gets,” Silas said, glancing back at the woman.

Her eyes were green enough to stay green under Dante's dim-ass office lamps. Warm brown skin, full mouth. She was fine, but it wasn't Shadowmere fine, and Silas noted it the way he'd note anything else people would remember about her. A woman who looked like that didn't pass through a lane. She got described afterward. Even with her face swollen from crying, nothing about her belonged to the nearby lanes. Shadowmere girls learned early how to sit so they could get up fast. She sat like nobody had ever needed her to. She caught him looking and lifted her chin a little, and he let his gaze move down to the torn slipper.

“Silas, this is Melena,” Dante said.

Melena didn't offer her hand. Silas wasn't about to reach for it anyway.

“What happened to her shoe?”

Her mouth tightened.

Dante set the bottle down. “She walked.”

“From where?”

“Halflight.”

Silas looked at the slipper again. The sole had split near the toe, and a dark stain climbed the silk where her foot had bled into it. Between Halflight and here was nothing but stairs, freight paths, and people who could pick a stranger out by her shoes. Nobody unfamiliar with the lower tiers made that walk and arrived with only one ruined slipper. Either somebody had walked it with her, or she'd been lucky, and Silas had stopped believing in lucky around the same time he'd stopped throwing up after fights.

“By herself?” Silas raised an eyebrow.

Melena stood before Dante could answer. The dress pulled against her knees, and she set her bad foot down carefully, as if she'd spent the last few hours learning exactly how much it would take. A dark red seal had broken across the fold of the envelope in her hands. He couldn't make out the mark from where he stood, and he noticed she'd turned it so he couldn't. “You can direct your questions to me. I am standing in front of you.”

Nobody down here said *I am* when *I'm* would do. She talked like somebody who'd been corrected for less.

Silas looked up from her foot. “Did you come from Halflight by yourself?”

“Yes.”

“You get followed?”

“No.”

“You check?”

Her fingers tightened around the envelope.

Silas turned to Dante. “She got followed.”

“I did not.”

Dante took a drink before either of them could go another round. His silver ring clicked against the glass. “Rae opened the final routes, if you must know.”

*If you must know* was what Dante said when he was handing over the least he could get away with. Rae had either walked the girl down herself or opened the doors ahead of her, and either way she'd made sure she was gone before Silas could ask her anything about it. Melena's mouth tightened again. She didn't correct him, but her grip changed on the envelope, and the incense smoke above Dante's desk wavered as if somebody had opened a door.

Nobody had.

Heat spread through the office, too fast to have come from the lamp or the radiator under the window. Melena pulled one hand inside her sleeve. The silk darkened where her fingertips caught it, and a thin curl of smoke rose beside the mourning stones.

Silas drew his knife and stepped between her and the desk. His shoulder caught halfway through the movement, a hot, bright stop that nearly took the blade out of his grip, so he kept it in his good hand and held it low, where she could see it without having to look down. Held high, a knife started arguments. Held low, it only told somebody where the line was.

Silver lit the doorframe. Cold pushed out from the seals in the walls and lifted the hair on the back of his neck, but the heat stayed on his face. He'd felt those seals answer before, and the room usually settled once they did. This time Melena stumbled, and the smoke kept coming.

Everything Silas knew that could do this to a room came out of the space between living and dead. Thanomancers. Root-Listeners. Lament workers. People who could pull memories up out of the dark. He'd been raised around that kind of work, and all of it had a pull to it, a cold underneath, a mark somewhere you could find if you knew how to look. This had none of that. It was only heat, coming off a crying girl like something that had been shut in too long.

“Take your hand out your sleeve,” Silas said.

She dragged it free. Black had spread beneath her nails and over the ends of her fingers like charcoal. She stared at them, then shut her hand around the envelope again. “I'm not going to hurt anyone.”

Silas believed that she meant it. He'd also watched a lot of people mean things right up until they couldn't help it.

“Put the knife away,” Dante told him.

Silas didn't turn. The heat had worked its way under his collar. His father was close behind him, close enough that stepping aside would put Dante straight in front of it, and Dante would never thank him for standing there. Silas stayed anyway. “You see her hands?”

“I see her.” Dante crossed to the wall and laid his palm flat against a cold seal. The silver in the doorframe steadied. “She crossed seven tiers without burning through one. Give her room.”

Seven tiers. Silas counted them up from Shadowmere without meaning to and didn't like where he ended up. Whoever she was, she'd started a long way from any street he knew.

He eased back a step. Not far. Melena's eyes followed the knife, then went to the locked door, and another breath of heat crossed the office. Whatever she'd done to get through seven tiers, it wasn't helping her much now.

“Who saw you come down?” he asked.

“I wore a veil until Shadowmere.” Her voice stayed level. The envelope trembled between her hands.

Silas watched the smoke gather at her cuff. There wasn't a mark he could follow or a working he knew how to break. He couldn't see what she was holding on to. He could only see where it was getting away from her. Even if he'd been fool enough to open in his father's office, he wouldn't have known where to put his hand.

Dante picked up Rae's bell and rang it once.

Silas knew the sound before it finished. Small and silver, the same voice as the bells Rae wore around her wrist. She'd left it here on purpose, which meant she'd known this might happen, and nobody had seen fit to mention it to the man who'd be walking the girl through the street. The low note reached him through the pressure in his ears. Melena gasped. The heat pulled back so sharply that he felt it leave his collar, and her knees folded before she could catch the sofa.

He put the knife away and caught her wrist. Her weight dragged him forward, and his shoulder told him exactly what it thought about that, but he held her up long enough for her other hand to find the leather. Her skin was still hot. Under his thumb, her pulse struck fast, missed, then came hard again. He counted three of them before she found her footing.

“Let me go.” Melena planted both feet and pulled against his grip.

Silas opened his hand as soon as she had her balance. She stayed braced against the sofa, breathing through her mouth. The envelope had crumpled where she'd caught herself.

“Do not touch me without asking,” she said.

He looked at the hand she'd pressed flat against the leather. It was holding her well enough now. “All right.”

He'd have caught her again. Next time he'd ask first if there was time to ask, and if there wasn't, she could be mad at him upright.

Dante set the bell back on the desk. Silas wiped his palm on his coat and waited for the explanation his father made no move to give. Dante never explained things in front of the people they were about. He explained them later, in pieces, or he let Silas walk around believing he already had.

Instead, Dante opened the map-room door. “Come here a minute.”

Melena's head lifted. His father stopped with one hand on the frame and slid a plate of bread within her reach. “Sit down, baby. Eat something.”

“I don't need—”

“Water too,” Dante said, without raising his voice.

Silas had been on the other end of that voice his whole life. It sounded like a suggestion right up until somebody tried to treat it like one.

He followed his father into the private room. Through the whisperglass, he watched Melena lower herself onto the sofa with the envelope still in her hand. Dante shut the door, and the sound seal turned blue. The office went quiet on the other side of the glass.

His father had left the route map uncovered. Three threads shifted under it, drawing tight near the north line before they disappeared past the edge of the frame. Remy had counted two Shrouds on the north route last night. Dante's map had apparently counted them too. Silas leaned his good hand against the table and waited while Dante pulled a black pin from a closed passage.

“You know what just happened in there,” Silas said.

“Yes.”

He waited another second. Dante fitted the pin over Back Market.

“You gon' tell me?”

“She needs somewhere she can stay without being seen. Rae's routes are being watched. Quietcasters been walking the edges of where Rae had her.”

Quietcasters didn't walk anywhere for the exercise. If they were circling Rae's routes, the Synod had a reason, or believed it did. Silas thought about Jerrick's token sitting under Jude's lamp with a Dawnveil stamp on it, about Shrouds standing still on the north line, about a fifteen-year-old nobody had found yet. He thought about saying some of it. Dante paid for information by the piece, though, and Silas didn't have the whole piece yet. He kept it.

Through the glass, Melena had set the envelope beside the water. She tore a piece off the bread and held it without eating.

“So you bringing her through ours.”

“I'm moving her into one of your mama's houses.” Dante pressed the pin into place. A line opened beneath the glass, running south from the Mercer building toward Gutterlight. Silas knew the turn behind the tailor row before his father traced it. “You'll take her.”

Silas studied the narrow stretch beside the market. This time of day the freight crews would be backed up at the public passage, which helped, and the lane behind the sheds would be full of people buying funeral goods, which helped and didn't. A crowd hid somebody. A crowd also bumped into them. “Why me?”

“Rhea's on a contract that touches the Synod. Jude's too busy doing whatever that boy does when he doesn't answer the phone. You know the routes, and you know Camille's thresholds.” Dante moved another pin, closing the public approach behind the house.

Silas knew exactly what Jude was doing. He kept his face where it was. “You got handlers.”

“I got men who'd mistake her being scared for permission.” His father met his eyes across the table. “You won't.”

It sounded like trust. With Dante, trust usually came with a job attached, and you didn't find out how heavy the job was until you'd already picked it up.

Silas had moved people who fought him every step of a route and people who wouldn't lift their heads long enough to learn where they were going. He'd moved the sick and the hunted. None of them had done what Melena had just done in Dante's office. Knowing the streets wouldn't tell him what to do if it happened halfway across one.

“Bell going with her?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then what am I supposed to do if that happens again?”

“Get her inside. Keep people away from her.”

“You giving me a job and leaving out the part that can get us caught.”

Dante turned his ring under his thumb. He did that when he'd already decided how much he was giving and was waiting for you to quit asking for more. “I'm giving you what you need to move her. Keep the coat closed. Stay off the public approaches. Don't let anybody stop you to ask questions.”

“Who's looking?”

“Lorraine counted two watchers near the front. You're taking the south passage.” Dante went back to the map without filling in the rest.

Silas had walked through that front door ten minutes ago and hadn't seen anybody watching it. He didn't like that Lorraine had. He liked even less what that said about how much last night was still sitting in his body.

Through the glass, Melena finally put the bread in her mouth. She chewed slowly, staring at the paper beside her plate. Eighteen, maybe. Young enough that somebody should have been sitting with her while she grieved, instead of a sofa and a plate of bread. She held the bread the way people did when they'd forgotten what eating was for.

“Who decides when she leaves?” Silas asked.

“Mother Rae. Camille's going over this evening to check on her. Your mama built the place. She can get in without waiting on you.” Dante's hand stopped over the final pin. “She ain't regular cargo, and she ain't yours. You understand me?”

Silas understood him fine. Not his to keep, not his to decide for, and not his to answer for once Rae came to collect her. Dante had put all three in one sentence so he wouldn't have to say any of them twice.

Silas glanced at the closed public approach. “You want her kept inside.”

Dante checked the south line once more before sighing. “I need you to keep Shadowmere from eating her while she's too busy grieving to see its mouth open.”

So somebody had died, and Dante knew who. Silas didn't ask. He'd get the same nothing he'd gotten on everything else, and Dante would charge him for the question.

His father left the pin where it was. Silas knew that tone. Arguing wouldn't open the route back up. Dante opened the map-room door instead.

Melena had emptied the water. Half the bread was left, torn into pieces across the plate. She gathered the envelope before either man got close and smoothed one crushed edge against her knee.

Dante took his route coat off the chair. “You'll need this.”

“Where are we going?” Her attention stayed on Silas. She'd already worked out which one of them would be walking with her.

“Safehouse.” He stopped beside the sofa instead of crouching over her foot. “Your heel needs wrapping first. I can do it, or you can.”

Melena looked down at the torn slipper. She held still long enough that he thought she might refuse, then lifted the hem clear of her ankle. “You may.”

Nobody had ever told Silas he *may* do anything. He took it.

He brought the medical tin from Dante's cabinet and crouched on his good side. The slipper had rubbed the skin open above her heel, and the silk had dried into the wound. He eased it off slowly so it wouldn't tear the scab away with it, cleaned the skin, and laid a strip of cloth under her ankle before he started wrapping. Her hand tightened in her skirt when the damp cloth touched raw skin. He stopped and waited for her foot to settle before he finished, then closed the tin and put it in his coat.

Dante set a pair of boots beside them. Silas knew them by the cracked left heel. They were Rhea's, from back when Rhea still came by this office, and Dante had kept them in a cabinet all this time like she might walk in and need them. Silas didn't say anything about that. Neither did his father.

Melena watched Silas check inside each boot for anything that might catch on the bandage, then took them out of his hands and put them on herself. They had room at the toes, but the soles held when she stood.

“Rae's people checked your clothes?” Silas asked.

“Yes.”

“I need to look too. Anything somebody can follow.” He kept his hands where she could see them. “Seams and stones. You can stay sitting.”

She looked toward Dante. His father waited beside the desk with the coat over his arm and gave her nothing either way, which was its own kind of answer. Dante never made anybody's choice for them when he could make them stand in it instead. After a moment, Melena sat back down and held out one cuff.

Silas checked what she offered first. The stones carried marks he couldn't make out even this close, small and exact and in no hand anybody had taught him. He turned one far enough to see its back without pulling at the stitching. Nothing in the seam answered him. He worked around the collar and checked the pins she'd kept in her hair, stopping when she drew away so she could take out the last comb herself.

The damaged hem had picked up soot and old route residue. He checked it without asking her to stand again, then sat back on his heels. “Nothing I recognize.”

Which wasn't the same thing as nothing. He didn't say that part.

Dante held the coat open, and she slipped into it. Brown ward-thread lined the inside. It covered the silk to her calves, but her hair still showed above the collar, and in this light it was the brightest thing in the room. Silas pointed. She gathered it up, and when he touched his own head, she pulled the hood over all of it and fastened the front. She tucked the envelope inside the coat after one long look at its ruined corner.

“Walk on my left,” he told her. His right hand needed to stay free, and his left wasn't good for much today anyway. “If I stop, you stop. Somebody calls you, keep moving.”

“How will I know if they're calling me?”

He glanced at the black silk showing beneath the coat. “They'll find something to call you.”

She pulled the coat lower and said nothing else.

Dante opened the south passage and put a brass token in Silas's hand. The route mark matched the line he'd set under the glass. Silas pocketed it, then looked back at him. “Who's staying tonight?”

“You. Outside her door.” Dante's eyes went to the shoulder Silas hadn't lifted since he walked in. “Let Camille look at that when she comes.”

So Dante had noticed. Of course he had. Silas wasn't about to volunteer another injury for his mother to get into, and he wasn't about to tell Dante that either. He stepped into the passage and waited for Melena. She passed his father without offering her hand, and Dante closed the door behind them before she could look back. Silas couldn't tell whether that was mercy or habit. With Dante, he'd never found the line between the two.

Rain tapped against the grate over the southern exit. Silas stopped under it and checked the alley through the bars. A freight cart blocked half the street beyond. Two workers were trying to turn it while a runner argued that his package couldn't wait, and nobody had a reason to look toward the passage yet. He brought Melena out while the cart was still moving.

She stopped under the awning and tipped her head back. He followed her gaze past the hanging walkways to the cavern roof, where water fell through the violet mist. The only light up there came from a shop sign with two dead letters. She was looking for sky. Silas had seen people from the upper tiers do it their first time this far down, searching the rock like it was a ceiling somebody would eventually open. Nobody born under stone bothered. There wasn't anything up there for her to find.

He stepped into her line of sight. “This way.”

She came with him, keeping close to the wall as the freight cart scraped past. He had to shorten his stride for the boots. She kept lifting the dress beneath the coat instead of letting the hem drag, and every time she did, black silk flashed at her ankles. At the next turn, he slowed enough for her to gather a fistful of it and hold it there.

Past the market, freight workers waited at the public passage with their route permissions out. A clerk ran a scanner over the first set and waved the cart through. Silas took Melena behind the sheds before anybody at the checkpoint turned their way. She looked back once but kept walking.

Behind the sheds, the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods, and the lane narrowed until the stall counters took up half of it. Silas knew these stalls the way he knew his mother's handwriting. Half of them had sold Camille thread at some point, and the other half wished they had. Mourning thread hung in dark skeins above a basin catching water off an awning. Farther up, near the bend, a shopkeeper snapped a case of Hush shut as a boy drifted too close to the counter. The boy was too thin and too interested in everything at once. Silas marked him and kept moving.

There wasn't room to pass anybody without brushing coats. He kept Melena between himself and the freight wall and moved her along at a steady pace, because if she stopped to apologize to somebody, somebody would look at her face.

Pell's salt stall sat at the narrowest point, under a striped awning that leaked at one corner. A woman stood at the counter with a grey-paper bundle under her arm and her back to the lane, and as Silas came even with her she said, “You'll make it up now,” in a voice flat enough to set a cup on. Pell reached for his scoop without a word. Silas had never in his life seen Pell take an instruction without arguing it first. It snagged at the edge of his attention, the way a sound from the wrong direction would, and he let it go because the Hush boy had just quit drifting.

Up ahead, something crashed. Glass went across stone, and the shopkeeper yelled, and the boy came out of the Hush shop at a dead run, straight down the lane at them with both arms wrapped around whatever he'd taken.

“Wall,” Silas said.

Melena went to it. He stepped back to put himself between her and the boy and walked his shoulders straight into somebody behind him.

He knew the shape of what came next before he finished turning. A bump from behind and a runner from the front was the oldest lift in the Back Market, and he had a brass Mercer token in one pocket and Camille's medical tin in the other. He came around fast. His left shoulder screamed through the whole turn, and he caught the fist anyway, his bandaged right hand closing around a wrist that was already driving.

The punch stopped an inch from his chest.

Rings first. Three of them, heavy Ironcross rings across the knuckles, the kind a person wore because she'd planned on hitting somebody with them someday. Then her feet. She'd set them before she swung, weight on the back one, and somebody had taught her that, because people who'd never been taught swung from their shoulders and fell in after it. Red grit had dried on the toes of her boots. Ashvein dust, or somebody who'd walked through a lot of it. She was a foot shorter than him and hadn't seemed to notice.

Then her mouth, which was cussing him out.

She hadn't stopped since before her fist landed in his hand. It was steady and specific, and none of it repeated, and Silas found that he was still looking at her mouth after he'd already gotten everything off it he could use. He had no practical reason for that. He noticed himself doing it the way he'd notice a limp in his own leg, and he let go of her wrist and stepped back with both hands open in front of his chest, where she could see them.

The boy tore past behind him and was gone.

“You always swing first?” he said.

“You always walk backwards?”

“What's your name?”

He asked before he'd decided why. The reasons showed up a second later and lined themselves up like they'd been there first: she'd been standing three feet from Melena, she'd swung at him in a lane full of witnesses, and he liked knowing who people were before they knew who he was. All of them were true. None of them had been what made him ask.

Something moved at the corner of her mouth, like she'd almost laughed at him and decided he didn't deserve it. “You ain't earned it.”

It was a fair answer. Most people would have given him a name, a fake one, and he'd have known it was fake by the second syllable. She hadn't bothered lying. She just stood there in the drip from Pell's awning and let him not know, and Silas liked that more than he had time for.

He looked past her to check on Melena, and the liking went out of him.

Melena had pressed herself so hard into the freight wall that her hood had slid back. A length of red hair had come loose over the collar of Dante's coat, bright as a lamp in that grey lane, and anybody between here and the bend who'd turned around for the crash had gotten a good long look at it.

“Hood,” he said.

Melena pulled it up with both hands. Silas moved her off the wall and up the lane without touching her, keeping his body on the side of the stalls, and didn't let himself walk faster than the crowd. They passed the Hush shop, where the shopkeeper was down on one knee in broken glass, cussing the boy and the boy's mother and the boy's whole block.

Just past the shop, Silas stopped and turned around.

He told himself it was to see whether she'd follow, or whether the boy had partners, or whether anybody else in that lane had started paying the wrong kind of attention to a girl in a man's coat. She was still standing at Pell's counter with her bundle under her arm, and she was watching him. She didn't look away when he caught her at it. She looked at him like he owed her the explanation and she was prepared to wait for it.

“You made me lose my cousin,” she said across the lane.

Silas didn't know anything about a cousin. He knew what it sounded like when somebody kept a ledger, though, and she'd said it like one.

“Then I owe you,” he said.

He meant it, which he hadn't planned on either. Then he took Melena around the corner, and the lane closed up behind them.

Melena didn't speak until the noise of the market had thinned behind a wall of crates. “Did she take anything?”

He'd already checked. Token in the left pocket, tin in the right, knife where it belonged. “No.”

“She hit you.”

“She tried.”

Melena looked at him from under the hood for a moment longer than the answer needed, then looked away. Silas went back over the lane in his head to see who might have turned for the crash and who might have kept looking afterward. He got the Hush shopkeeper, two women at the thread stall, a man with a cart who hadn't looked up at all. Then he got Ironcross rings, Ashvein dust, a missing cousin, and *You ain't earned it*, and he realized he had more of her than he had of anybody else in that lane, and none of it was what he needed.

He didn't have her name.

A roar came through the stone ahead of them.

Melena stopped so suddenly that he had to stop with her. Her hand went inside Dante's coat, over the place where she'd put the envelope. “What is that?”

“Gutterlight.” Silas waited for the crowd to settle enough that she could hear him. A match had just let out. Somebody had won money, and somebody else had probably been carried through the service gate on a stretcher with two bowls of salt behind him.

“You're taking me near an arena?”

He checked the crossing beyond her shoulder. A pair of runners was hauling a box between them and arguing about which door they'd been paid to use. “Nobody hears much else when a match is on.”

She looked at the wall the sound had come through. He gave her time for one breath, then started for the closed tailor shop. She followed without needing the second instruction he'd been ready to give her.

The shop's shutter was down. Silas checked beneath it, then the narrow gap beside the false utility panel. He took the brass token from his pocket and pressed it into the center of the panel. Ward lines showed under his palm, and the panel released without pulling anything from him. He held it open for Melena and watched the alley until she had both feet on the stairs, then stepped through and closed it behind them.

Her borrowed boots sounded too loud in the narrow stairwell. She heard it too and placed the next step more carefully. Silas kept to the side so she could use the rail and took the climb slower than he normally would. His shoulder didn't object to that part.

Camille's seal waited at the top. He knew her work from the cuts around the frame, deep and even, every line finished all the way to the end the way she finished everything. The brass token wasn't going to be enough for this door. Silas drew his knife, nicked the side of his thumb, and pressed the fresh blood into the seal. He held it against the stone until the line closed, then took his hand away and put the small cut to his mouth. Behind him, Melena had stopped on the last stair.

“Was that necessary?” she asked.

Silas wiped the blade and put it away. “For this door? Absolutely.”

He pushed it open before she could ask what made it different from the one downstairs.

Warm amber light reached the landing. Silas let Melena pass him, then checked the inner edge of the threshold before he stepped across. She stopped near the dining table. She looked past the flowers and the bowl of fruit to the back wall, then turned to the wall beside her, and then the next one. She was looking for a window. There wasn't one, and she'd figure that out on her own without him saying it.

He left her in sight of the open door while he checked the bathroom and the bedroom. Fresh clothes filled the wardrobe. The bed was made, and the flowers on the table hadn't started to drop. Somebody had readied this place in the last day. Either Dante had sent someone this morning, or his mother had known a lot more over that crossword than *Don't know*. Silas would have bet on the second and hoped for the first. He checked the hidden emergency route out of habit. Only Mercer blood opened it, which made it the one door in the place nobody else could come through.

Back in the main room, he lifted the corner of the rug nearest the kitchen. The ward line under it hadn't been disturbed. He laid the rug flat, checked the seals on the food, then took a bottle of water from the cabinet and set it within Melena's reach.

She'd taken off Dante's coat and laid it over a chair. Without it, the burned cuff showed against the table. She was smoothing the envelope again, working carefully around the place her hand had crushed it in the office.

“How long am I staying here?” she asked.

“Until Mother Rae decides where you go.”

“And if I decide first?”

Silas set the medical tin where she could reach it. “You need to stay inside the ward line.”

Melena lifted her face. “I did not come here to be imprisoned.”

“Right now they use the same door.”

He didn't like saying it. He'd said it anyway, because it was true, and because she'd hear it from the lock in an hour whether he said it or not.

She looked past him to the threshold. “You don't know what I came here for.”

His attention dropped to the envelope. She slid it under her palm before he could read so much as the outside. He looked back at her face. “No. I don't.”

Heat gathered near the table. A flower at the edge of the vase curled, its lowest petal browning against the glass. Melena pulled her hand off the paper. Black was creeping back beneath her nails.

He stopped on the other side of the table. “Your heart still running like it was in the office?”

She pressed her lips together, then gave him one small nod.

“Let me check?” He held out his hand and waited.

Melena set the envelope down. She offered her wrist and watched his fingers settle over the pulse. It was fast, though steadier than after the bell, and her skin was still hot. Another petal browned while he counted. He let go before she had to ask.

“You feel it coming?”

“Sometimes.” She drew her wrist back against herself.

“What stops it?”

Melena stared at the blackened edge of the flower. “Nothing for long.”

Silas looked toward the door. Camille's seal hadn't changed. He could check every line his mother had put in this house and still not know whether any of it was built for what Melena had carried inside. Camille built for the dead and for the people who came after them. As far as Silas could tell, this was neither.

“Camille's coming this evening,” he said. “She gon' want a look at you.”

“Who is Camille?”

“My mama.” He checked the kitchen latch on his way back to the door. “You got a problem with me, tell her. You want the placement changed, she can take it to Mother Rae.”

It was the most useful thing he had to offer her. Camille would listen to a complaint about him with real interest.

Melena watched him for a moment. “And until then?”

“I'll be outside.”

He stepped into the corridor and kept his good hand on the door until it closed. Two marks waited beside the frame. He pressed his palm across the entry seal first, then the containment mark, and silver spread over the lock and settled into the metal.

The handle turned from the inside.

Silas would have tried it too. He listened to it stop, then try once more, and then go still.

He dragged the chair from the opposite wall and set it where he could see the lock without turning his head. Gutterlight roared on the other side of the eastern wall. He lowered himself into the chair carefully, kept his bad arm close against his ribs, and watched the handle.

His mind went back to the lane while his eyes stayed on the lock. Ironcross rings. Ashvein dust. A cousin she'd lost because he'd stepped backward at the wrong time. He went over all of it twice before he caught himself, and the second time through, he hadn't been thinking about who had seen Melena's hair.

Last night, at least, he'd known what he was fighting.

## Chapter 4: Isola

The noon courier took fourteen Veilmarks, two more than Isola had meant to pay. The route inspectors were out. She'd known they might be, and she'd brought the fourteen, and it still felt like being robbed by someone polite.

He was a thin man with an Ironcross stamp on the back of his hand, and he checked the seal on Micah's packet twice before sliding it into the flat case at his hip. Isola counted the coins into his palm one at a time. She'd never listened to money before this morning. Now each one rang against the one before it on the way down, low and full, and she didn't let go of a single coin until she'd heard it.

Micah stood in the doorway behind her with his hands in his jacket, as if he could make the packet move faster by staring at it. When the courier stepped back into the lane, Micah let his shoulders drop for the first time since first bell.

“That's it?” he said.

“That's it.” Isola shut the door. “Now you just gotta not do nothing stupid until Danika write back.”

“I been not doing nothing stupid for thirty days.”

“Twenty-nine and a morning.” His eyes went to his phone on the table, and Isola picked it up before he could. “And you still ain't getting your phone.”

He sucked his teeth and dropped onto the couch, which meant he'd given up for now. With Micah, *for now* usually lasted until he got hungry. Isola looked up at the clock over the name wall. The minute hand had slipped past twelve while the courier counted.

Noon had come. Asha hadn't.

Her door was still open down the hall, the way Isola had left it. Isola went in and shut it behind her.

The bag stood where she'd left it against the wall, torn pocket turned toward the plaster. She'd folded the strap under it before she walked out this morning, and the strap was still folded. Nobody had touched it. Isola worked two fingers through the tear and felt along the lining until she found the coin and drew it out. She didn't look at it again. She'd already looked at it enough to know what it was.

She stood there with it closed in her fist and made herself think it through one more time. If she left it here, it stayed under Omari's roof, with every name on that wall in the front room standing right next to it. If she took it, the only person it could bind was the one who'd chosen to pick it up. And if Asha came home and went looking for it, she'd find the lining empty and know somebody had been in her things. Isola wanted her to know that.

She tucked it into the hidden pocket of her skirt, behind the seam where she kept the money, and stood the bag back up so the tear wouldn't show from the doorway.

In the kitchen, Selah was packing roots into a jar. The humming had started again sometime after breakfast and hadn't let up long enough for Isola to decide whether that was better or worse.

“I'm going for the salt,” Isola said.

Selah wiped her hands on her apron and drew the order slip from her pocket. She didn't hand it over right away. She held it a second and looked at Isola the same way she'd looked at her over the dishes, then put it in her palm.

“Pell,” Selah said. “Not nobody else.”

“I know.”

“Funeral grade.”

“Mama. I know.”

Selah went back to the jar. “Then you ain't got nothing to worry about.”

Selah didn't repeat herself. When she did, she wasn't talking about the thing she was saying. Isola put the slip in her front pocket, where she'd see it every time she reached for money, and set Micah's phone face down on the counter. Selah looked at it, then at her, and moved the jar so it sat on top of the phone. It was as good as a lock.

Tevin's door was shut. Isola didn't knock, because knocking would tell him she was leaving. She checked the front room on her way out instead. His route coat was still hanging over the chair where he'd dropped it. Good.

The token Mother Rae had given her for runs hung on a cord under her shirt. Rae had never taught her to read the marks on its face, and Isola had never asked. She pulled it out in the lane and kept it in her fist all the way down the cliff stairs, past the shrine at the second landing and past the women hauling water up from the vent cisterns. One of them asked after Selah without stopping. Isola told her Selah was fine without stopping either. The stairs cost nothing and saved her the lift fare. She'd pay for the lift coming back if the salt was heavy.

The chasm bridge was loud with people trying to get across before the mist came up. It had come up twice already today, according to the boy selling grounding charms at the head of the span, and the second time three people had stood frozen in the middle for ten minutes because they couldn't see the rail. Isola could see the rail. She could see the whole bridge, the black stone arching over the vent and the violet glow breathing up out of the dark beneath it, and at the far end the Shadowmere guard post with its lamp and its line.

The line moved slowly. The guard was a big man in a mercenary's coat with half the buttons gone. He had a route scanner on a chain and a metal plate set into the counter in front of him. The woman ahead of Isola paid at the plate. She pricked her thumb on the needle beside it without being told and pressed it down, and the guard watched the plate drink until it had enough. The boy behind her either didn't have blood to spare or didn't want to give it. He gave a name instead, a crew name Isola recognized from the east gate, and swore to run for them whenever they called. The guard wrote it down without looking up.

Isola knew that crew. She knew boys who'd made that exact promise at fourteen and were still running it off at twenty, and she watched this one walk onto the bridge like he'd paid with something he could earn back.

She'd bled on that plate herself before Mother Rae gave her the cord.

She stepped up and opened her fist. The guard looked at the token before passing the scanner over it. The scanner made the short flat sound she'd been waiting for, and he lifted his chin toward Shadowmere without asking where she was going or what she had in her skirt. She kept her free hand on her bag strap the whole time, nowhere near the pocket. She didn't know whether a scanner that read tokens could read anything else, and she wasn't about to find out by touching her own hip in front of it.

Isola didn't like Shadowmere.

She'd never had to explain that to anybody at home, because nobody there liked it either. In Ashvein, a crew wore its colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were on before you stood on it, and if you didn't, somebody's auntie would tell you from a window. Shadowmere didn't have aunties in windows. It had neon signs and violet mist and rain falling out of rock, and people who smiled at you while they figured out what you were worth. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

That was why she went to the assayer before she went to Pell.

The woman kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain didn't reach. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Mother Rae and watching the woman weigh it on a little brass scale with weights no bigger than teeth. She could weigh anything. People brought her scrapings, filings, and coins that had been through fire, and she put on her loupes and told them what it was worth. Isola had never once heard her ask where any of it came from. That was the whole reason to come here.

She was weighing something now for a man in a dock coat who was arguing with the result. Isola waited until he lost and left, then stepped up, set her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over.

The coin lay in the middle of her palm. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody passing could see more than a corner.

The assayer looked at it. Then she put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it. “I don't weigh High Heresy.”

The man at the next counter, selling charm wire, stopped talking to his customer, and the customer stopped listening to him.

“Get it off my counter,” the assayer said.

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

She walked away without hurrying, because hurrying in the Back Market was the same as asking to be followed. In Asha's room, she'd told herself the coin was good enough to pass in bad light. It was. The light under that arch was as bad as light got, and the woman hadn't needed anything better. She hadn't picked it up. She hadn't tilted it toward a lamp. Isola had watched that woman spend a full minute on a thimble of melted silver, and she'd known this coin from across her own counter in the time it took to look down.

Nobody learned to see something that fast by seeing it once.

Isola came out from under the arch into the rain and let it hit her face for a second. Two strangers now had her face sitting next to the words *High Heresy*. She couldn't take the coin home, and she couldn't take it to Mother Rae with nothing in her hands but a question. That left the salt, which was the reason she was down here in the first place and the only part of today she could still do right.

She went looking for Pell.

His stall stood in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods. The lane was so narrow that the counters took up half of it, and everybody walking had to share the other half. Pell had a striped awning that leaked at one corner, a scale he didn't let anybody else touch, and a mouth that never stopped running, which was usually the only annoying thing about him. Mother Rae bought her funeral grade from him because he kept it dry and didn't cut it. Selah had made that clear the first time she sent Isola down here alone. Usually, by the time Isola got to the counter, Pell had asked after Selah, complained about the drip in his awning, and tried to sell her something she hadn't come for.

“Rae's girl,” Pell said when she reached the counter. He was already reaching. “What she need?”

He hadn't asked about Selah. Isola set the slip down. He read it, nodded, and scooped from the open barrel at his elbow into a square of brown paper.

She watched the scoop go in and come out. The salt was grey and coarse, with dark grit caught between the grains, and it clumped where the damp had gotten to it. Isola had been packing salt into Selah's windows since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them. She'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name: fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it was trying to get away. Pell kept it under the counter in a locked drawer, wrapped in grey paper with his stamp on the fold, and every time Isola came for Rae, he'd unlocked that drawer without being asked.

He was folding the brown paper.

“That ain't funeral grade,” Isola said.

Pell didn't look up. “It's what I got today.”

“That's quarry salt.”

“It's salt.”

“It's quarry salt, and it's wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop.” She put her hand flat on the slip. “Rae's slip say funeral grade.”

“Rae can come get it herself.”

He'd never said anything like that in front of her. Pell talked too much, sure, but he talked the way people did when they wanted to keep you happy, not the way they did when they wanted you gone. Isola looked at his hands. They were folding the paper too carefully, the corners squared up over and over, the way people kept their hands busy so their faces didn't have to do anything. She pulled the cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he had to look at it.

“She sent me,” Isola said. “Same as last time. Same as the time before that. Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today.”

Pell stopped folding.

He looked past her. Isola knew better than to turn around. She'd grown up in a house where you learned what was behind you by watching the face in front of you, and Pell's face had just gone blank.

Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end of the counter, and the other stood right beside her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word to Pell or to her. In Ashvein, she'd have known whose men they were by their sleeves before they ever got to the counter. These two wore plain dark coats and the kind of patience a person only had if they'd never once been told no at a counter.

Pell reached under the counter, farther back than the drawer. He pulled out an envelope and set it down. The man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking at it. The man at the end of the counter looked at Isola's hand, the one still holding Rae's token out in the open air, and Isola felt that look all the way up her arm. She closed her fist over the token. He followed the first man out of the stall, and the two of them went up the lane toward the bend without hurrying.

Pell didn't unlock the drawer until they'd been gone several minutes.

Isola dropped the cord back under her shirt while she waited. *Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something.* Pell owed it something. She'd just watched him pay.

He took out a grey paper bundle, set it on the scale, and watched the needle shake. He opened the bundle to show her the salt inside, and when he went to close it again, a thin stream of it ran onto the counter and off the edge into the wet at her feet. Pell watched it go and didn't say a word about it.

Isola had never seen him not say anything about anything.

“Who was that?” she asked.

“Who?”

She waited.

“Rae account,” Pell said. His letters wandered across the line as he logged the purchase in his notebook. “Tell her I said hey.”

“You want me to tell her who was here when I came for her salt?”

He tore the receipt out and held it toward her. When she didn't take it, he laid it on top of the bundle and pushed both across the counter. His eyes went up the lane, to where the men had gone.

“I want you to take your salt,” he said.

Isola tucked the bundle under her arm and stood there a second longer than she needed to, looking at the grey grit still sitting in the brown paper he'd tried to hand her. She could see it now, all the way through. Pell hadn't been cheating Rae. He'd been trying to get Rae's girl gone before those two walked up and found her standing at his counter with Rae's token around her neck. *Rae can come get it herself* had been the closest thing to a warning he could afford to give her.

She also knew she couldn't prove a bit of it, and that Mother Rae would ask about the delay if she stood in this stall much longer.

“And you short,” she said, because it was true and because she wasn't about to let him think she hadn't noticed. “It's on the ground, not in my paper.”

“I'll make it up next time.”

“You'll make it up now.”

He reached for the scoop. Coats brushed behind her as somebody came up the lane, and Isola didn't look, because something farther up had caught her eye where the lane bent toward the crossing and Gutterlight's noise came through the stone.

Asha was standing at the bend.

Isola knew her from the way she stood before she could see her face, weight back, head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her. Isola had never seen Asha stand like that anywhere she was supposed to be. The coat was dark, fitted close at the waist, and fell to her knees. Isola had never seen it in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Money like that came from somewhere. Isola thought of the bags lined up against Asha's wall, and the coin sitting against her own hip, and she didn't like how easily the three things fit together.

She turned from the counter, still talking. “And don't put none of that grey mess on her slip again or I'm telling her myself—”

Up ahead, something crashed. Glass went across stone, a shopkeeper yelled, and a boy came out of the Hush shop running straight down the lane at them. Somewhere close, a man's voice said one flat word, and a hooded shape moved toward the freight wall.

Then the man stepped back into Isola.

One bumps. One runs. You find out what's gone when you get home. Every story Isola had ever heard about the Back Market went like that, and here was a man's whole back in her chest and a boy running at her from the front, and the coin was in her skirt.

Isola couldn't afford to lose anything, so she did the one thing her father would have told her never to do.

She swung.

He turned before she could land it. His shoulder came around, and then her fist stopped like it had hit a wall. His hand had closed around her wrist. Her ring was an inch from the middle of his chest, close enough to touch his shirt. There was a bandage across his knuckles, white cloth gone grey at the edges and wrapped tight over two of them. Somebody who knew what they were doing had wrapped that hand. Not him. Nobody could get a wrap that tight one-handed.

Somebody had hit him recently, too. One cheek was swollen high under the eye, and his bottom lip was split, the middle healing dark. Rain soaked his locs and still ran off them, down past his ear and into his beard, and he didn't wipe it away.

His eyes went to her hand first, to the rings, then down to her feet and the way she was standing, and then they traveled up to her mouth. Her mouth was cussing him out, she realized. It had been since before her fist stopped. He let go of her wrist and stepped back with both hands open in front of his chest, where she could see them, as if she were the one who might do something.

“You always swing first?” the man said.

“You always walk backwards?”

“What's your name?”

She almost laughed. The nerve of him, asking that with his hands still up. “You ain't earned it.”

Six seconds. It took her six whole seconds of standing there in the drip off Pell's awning to realize he was a full foot taller than her.

His gaze moved past her shoulder, and Isola followed it to a hooded girl pressed against the freight wall. The hood had slid back when she pressed into the stone, and a length of hair had come loose over the collar of her coat. It was red. Isola had never seen that color on anybody's head in her life. The coat was a man's coat. The boots were somebody else's too, wide at the toe, and under all of it was black silk with stones on the collar, catching what little light the lane had. Mourning clothes, and good ones. Isola had grown up around funeral thread, and she knew what it cost to dress somebody's grief like that. Somebody had dressed this girl in whatever was nearest and hadn't had time to make any of it match.

“Hood,” the man said, and the girl pulled it up with both hands.

He moved her off the wall and up the lane without touching her. He didn't have to. She went where he went, and Isola didn't know what that made them to each other, but she knew it wasn't nothing.

Isola looked past them for Asha.

The bend was empty. Two runners crossed it with a box between them, and behind them there was nothing but the lane curving away toward the noise. When she looked back, the man had stopped just past the Hush shop and turned around. She didn't look away. If he wanted to catch her looking, he could catch her.

“You made me lose my cousin,” she said across the lane.

“Then I owe you,” he said, and he took the girl around the corner, and then he was gone too.

Isola stood in the lane long enough for a woman with a cart full of thread to tell her to move.

She went to the bend. Nothing there told her where Asha had been standing or which way she'd gone. Isola checked the crossing and the doorways on either side and the mouth of the next alley, and she kept at it until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her teeth. The crowd at the bend had thickened by the time she turned back toward Pell's stall. She walked slowly. She told herself it was so she wouldn't miss anything Asha might have been dumb enough to leave behind.

The real reason was him. Isola didn't like that either.

She'd met men who looked at her that long before. Most of them wanted something, and the rest wanted her to think they didn't. This one had read her the way Omari read a lock, quick and in order, and then he'd stepped back as if he'd decided she wasn't worth the trouble of finding out. He'd stopped a full swing without leaving a mark on her, and she'd felt how easily he could have done something else with that hand. She didn't owe him one more thought, and she already had a whole list of him she hadn't asked for: the bandage somebody else had tied, the lip healing dark in the middle, the rain he hadn't bothered to wipe, his hands up like she was the danger.

Pell had a twist of grey paper waiting on the counter when she got back. He pushed it across with two fingers and went back to his notebook, though he wasn't writing anything.

Isola opened the twist. Funeral grade, fine and white, enough to cover what he'd spilled and maybe a pinch more. The pinch more was Pell's apology. It was the only kind he'd ever given her.

“Pell.”

“I made it up.”

“Who was the man in the coat? The tall one with his hand wrapped.”

Pell looked up for the first time since she'd come back. The color had come back into his face. Whatever those two men had taken out of him, her question had put some of it back. “You ain't know who that was?”

“Would I be asking?”

“If you don't know, then I ain't telling you.” He laughed and shook his head like she'd told him a joke he didn't have time for. “Take your salt home, Rae girl.”

In Shadowmere, a man everybody knew was either somebody you paid or somebody you stayed out of the way of. Pell hadn't looked like he meant to do either one. He'd looked at her like she'd walked up and asked who the rain belonged to.

She folded the twist into the bundle and left.

The line at the bridge was shorter than she'd expected. The salt wasn't heavy, but Isola paid for the lift anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might have decided to follow a girl with Rae's token and a coin nobody would weigh.

The lift groaned up the shaft with eleven people pressed into a cage built for eight. Somebody's elbow sat in her ribs the whole way up. Isola kept the bundle flat against her chest and her free hand over her skirt, right where the seam bunched around the coin. The cage rattled at every level marker, and the operator called them out without looking up from his lever. Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.

She spent the whole ride irritated by six seconds of him.

Six seconds. She'd taken longer than that to decide whether to trust a coin. She went back over *You ain't earned it* and still liked it. Then she went back over *Then I owe you* and liked that a lot less, because she didn't know what he thought he owed her or how he planned to pay it. Isola knew what owing meant. Owing came with a number and a day, and he hadn't given her either one.

By the time the gate opened, the rain on her coat had started drying into dust. Coal smoke hung low over the lane, and she'd missed at least one bell while she was gone. The boys from across the street were gone from under their lantern. Isola trusted the empty spot less than she'd trusted the boys standing in it. Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge, and Isola looked at that little pile longer than a pile deserved.

The lantern above the door still burned amber.

She stood under it a moment longer than she meant to. Then she knocked the dust off her boots against the frame and let herself in. Micah was asleep on the couch with one arm hanging off the cushion and his shoes still on. He'd given up on the day, but not on the shoes. Tevin's coat hadn't moved from the chair, and his door was still shut. She listened at it on her way past and heard him breathing on the other side, slow, the way he breathed when he was pretending not to be awake. Good. He could pretend all evening if he wanted.

Asha's door was open. Isola didn't go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she'd left it. If Asha had come home, she'd come and gone without touching it.

In the kitchen, Selah was at the stove. She turned when Isola came in and held out both hands for the bundle before Isola reached the counter. “Pell?”

Isola laid the receipt on the counter. “He tried me with quarry salt first.”

“And?”

“And I told him no.”

Her mother untied the grey paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. It ran the way it was supposed to. She nodded once and folded the paper closed again.

“You find your cousin?”

This morning, Selah had told her not to go looking. Now she hadn't asked whether Isola had looked. She'd asked whether she'd found her. Isola reached for a rag that didn't need reaching for. “Saw her. Lost her in the Market.”

“Lost her how?”

A tall man with his hand wrapped and probably somebody else's blood still in the creases of his knuckles. A coat on Asha's back that Isola couldn't have bought with three months of courier fees. A coin the assayer wouldn't touch. Two men who never said a word, and Pell, quiet.

“Crowd,” Isola said.

It was the second time today she'd handed one of her parents a word that was only mostly true.

Selah held her stare long enough that Isola wiped the same spot on the counter twice. Then her mother turned back to the stove.

“Wash up,” Selah said. “Food almost ready.”

Isola went to the sink and turned on the water. She held her hands under it and watched the Shadowmere rain go down the drain with the red dust from the stairs, and she prayed that whatever Asha had gotten herself into, it wouldn't end with her back in Shadowmere.
