# Part One: Voice Diagnosis

## The short version

Your voice is **dry, practical, socially exact close third**. The narrator always knows what something costs, who's allowed to say what, and what everybody in the room is pretending not to know. You build the world through institutions people put up with instead of explaining them: the Bookkeepers, the Quiet Staff, lantern codes, route tokens, name-walls, the Doctrine of Descent. Your humor is the humor of people who are tired and competent. Your strongest scenes put a domestic ritual on top of a plot turn (the crossword, the name-binding, the dishes), so the family business and the danger come through the same doorway.

What's holding the draft back isn't a lack of richness. It's **rhythm and placement**. Both chapters lean hard on one-sentence paragraphs. They tend to open on the POV character's name. Description and backstory sometimes show up as a block while a scene is running, when they could come through the character's attention. And at a few emotional turning points the camera keeps moving where the character should have stopped to register what happened.

## How your paragraphs work

- **Your best paragraphs** run three to six sentences. They start with something concrete, turn on a judgment, and end on a dry note: "Silas would've respected the faith if she wasn't about to lose money behind it." "Keeping anything that green alive in Shadowmere felt like showing off." "The thing lost eleven minutes every day but that ain't stop him from winding it up every morning." Those are your voice at full strength.
- **Your setting paragraphs are systems, not scenery.** "Ashvein didn't have a sky. / It had work bells." works because it describes the world by how it's run. The lantern codes, the bell sequence, the salt rite and the deadbolt are all setting that tells you who's in charge.
- **The single-sentence paragraph is your default emphasis button.** About 60% of the narrative paragraphs in Chapter 1 are one sentence long (94 of 158), and Chapter 2 is about the same (85 of 136). Many of them earn it: the kill-count opening, "Now his chair faced the Bookkeepers," "Their names answered," "Behind her, the humming stopped." But when everything stands alone, nothing does. Fight beats like "He raised his hands" and "He dropped his hand" get the same white space as the chapter's real turns.
- **Name-first openings.** About a quarter of the narrative paragraphs in Chapter 1 begin with "Silas," and Chapter 2 does the same with "Isola." In the fight that cadence is fine, even useful. During the walk home and in the kitchen scenes it turns into a drumbeat.
- **What you were worried about isn't really the problem.** "That was / That meant / That made" barely appear (about three times across both chapters). The machine-cadence risk in these chapters is the one-line paragraph and the name-first sentence, not that construction.

## How close third works for you

- **Silas's attention is tactical and numerical.** He counts kills, minutes, steps, runners by age, blades legal and illegal. He reads people the way he reads Jerrick's knee: as weight that gets carried wrong. His judgments come out as social law ("Shadowmere manners," "Folks heard rules and forgot to ask who paid for them"). His feelings show up as practical problems: morning is going to be rude, his mother is going to raise hell, Jude needs to hurry up.
- **Isola's attention is managerial and deadline-driven.** She counts days (thirty, thirty-one), coins (twelve Veilmarks, fourteen if inspectors are out) and minutes before Tevin moves. She thinks two moves ahead about the people she's responsible for: the aunties at the east gate, the borrowed clothes, the route coat, Micah's phone. Her first question about anything is who it's going to cost.
- **They mirror each other, and you built it that way.** Both POVs count. Both chapters end on a household ritual that turns into a summons (the pencil snapping on WRIT, the humming stopping). Both mothers speak in proverbs and puzzles and use silence like a tool. That's a strong spine for a dual-POV romantasy, and I'd protect it.
- **Free indirect voice.** The narration slides into the character's register at moments of judgment ("finna be rude as hell," "gon' raise hell," "that ain't stop him") and stays more standard during plain action. That's the right instinct. The slide is a signal, so it works best when it's rationed, and you mostly ration it.
- **Where the camera takes over:** a few passages report what happens without the POV character weighing it. Examples: the salt-rite explanation, Tevin's physical description, the Wraithmoor house paragraph, and the moment after the kill. At each of these the reader is told something the character would actually be *using*, but the character isn't using it yet.

## Dialogue and humor

- Dialogue is clipped, layered with subtext, and nearly always a contest over standing: who can tell whom what to do. "You look terrible." / "You say that every time." "Yes, Daddy." / "Fuck you." "Everybody in this house got legs but me." / "And yet yours work the best." Love shows up as bossiness and reports. Nobody says the soft thing out loud.
- The crossword is your best structural instinct in these chapters. Volition, Requiem, Burden, Broker/Mercer and Writ let Camille deliver the plot as a mother's sideways commentary. "I don't control the newspaper" defuses the on-the-nose risk before a reader can complain. The pencil snapping on the T is a real chapter ending.
- Your proverbs are strong and specific to these characters: "A lie don't care who tell it." "You and your daddy the only two people care about the ending. Don't need to bother y'all with the middle." These do more cultural and characterization work than any amount of dialect could.
- **Tag verbs are the main dialogue polish issue.** "Micah sighed," "Selah chuckled," "Camille chuckled," "Silas grunted," "Silas shrugged" are used as speech tags, and two lines aren't punctuated as dialogue ("Still a choice." Silas shrugged.). A beat of action (what the body does) or a plain "said" reads more like you than a sound-verb does.

## What makes it feel lived-in

None of it comes from slang. It comes from social logic the narration never stops to explain:

- **Family authority and its exceptions.** Dante asks Camille and Rhea for things, but gives his sons "times, places, and instructions." Omari "let people make choices first. He got involved after that." Camille's silence "had ended arguments between all three of her children." Jude gets the token first, and everybody knows Silas waits.
- **Practical care used as control.** "Shoes." "You eat?" "Protein bar." "That ain't food." "People carry all type of nasty shit in they mouth." Jude's ointment versus "Then let your hand rot." Taking Micah's phone. Holding the route coat.
- **Neighborhood intelligence networks.** The aunties at the east gate. Shadowmere manners about blood on a shirt. The mother covering her son's route patch while nobody reacts.
- **Record-keeping as survival.** The name-wall, kept because "trusting the Synod to remember somebody was an easy way to lose them," is the deepest cultural move in these two chapters. It's genealogy as defense, and nobody translates it for the reader.
- **Godparent and household obligation.** "You know how your godmother is." The salt run as a family errand that's also a reason to cross tiers.
- **Food and objects that carry history.** House shoes "that should've been thrown away before Jude learned to walk," the stew meat Camille "would lie" about, Omari's watch that loses eleven minutes a day.

Where to deepen it: **naming** (who calls Isola "Lo" and who uses her whole name, and when), **who's allowed to tease whom**, and **the moments where a character chooses not to react** as a kind of manners. Chapter 2 also promises breakfast and never quite serves it.

## Your strongest instincts

1. Hook lines built on social arithmetic ("six times … maybe four minutes").
2. World institutions that show up already compromised (the Floor Judge, the record charm, the report that will say "after the match").
3. Domestic ritual as the vehicle for plot (crossword, name-binding, dishes, salt).
4. Withholding: magic gets named and used without being explained ("You open?" "No." "Steel had been enough.").
5. Endings that close a ritual and open a threat at the same moment.

## Where you leave quality on the table

1. **Emotional hinges get a camera instead of a mind.** You open Chapter 1 with a countdown to twenty-four, then never let Silas register twenty-four. The opening asks a question and the chapter never answers it, even in one cold sentence. When Selah turns to Isola, the beat lands on a stock image ("chills went down Isola's spine") where it could land on something only this mother and daughter would notice.
2. **Description arrives as a block while the scene is running.** Tevin's hoodie-and-locs paragraph and the house-paneling paragraph are good material. Run them through what Isola is *using* them for (Tevin's fresh retwist is evidence he planned to be seen; the sealing walls are her father's answer to raids) and they become characterization instead of a pause.
3. **The runners' story is summarized when it could be felt.** "Fifteen, seventeen, and nineteen" is excellent. Silas thinking about the runners by age is very him. But the runner who goes still at the slur, and the arithmetic of who's alive, can carry more weight without adding facts. Silas can do the subtraction himself.
4. **Logic and continuity slips that a reader will notice.** For example: Isola "holds his stare" and then "refuses to give Tevin the eye contact." "Tevin frowned." / "Isola frowned." back to back. "The back door opened" when Omari comes out of the back room. "Longer than Isola had been alive" used twice in the same chapter. "Shrouds had closed the north line" when Remy only said they were posted. Micah's "this some bullshit" moment plays well, but the setup for him being Tevin's backup could be planted one beat earlier.
5. **Some sentences explain what the reader just understood.** "Jerrick was used to hurting and staying upright" repeats "Lowtorch men knew how to stand through pain." "One blade the Wardens had somehow missed" partly repeats the earlier setup, though it can be turned into a callback.
6. **Isola's discovery of the coin could pay off her own earlier line.** "Asha always said she liked being prepared. / Isola had stopped asking what for." is set up beautifully, and then the packed bags are never looked at again once she's holding a counterfeit.

## How I'll draft from here

- Order things the way the POV character takes them in: notice, read, judge, act. Let plain movement stay plain.
- Use one-line paragraphs only for real turns. Keep developed paragraphs where the thinking runs long.
- Vary sentence openings by starting with the thing noticed, the time, the object or the other person, not by stacking clauses.
- Use action beats and "said" instead of sound-verb tags.
- Run every description through what the character is using it for.
- No added slang. Narration slides into the character's register only at moments of judgment.
- Chapters 1–2 stay the source of fact. Additions are limited to interpretation, sensory detail, relationship knowledge and connective logic that the text already supports.
- Em dashes are for interrupted dialogue, not for rhythm in narration.

# Part Two: Calibration Rewrite

## Chapter 1: Silas

The first time Silas Graves killed somebody, he’d thrown up for an hour.

The seventh time, dinner stayed down.

By the nineteenth, he could drink water afterward without tasting blood.

The twenty-third time, he hadn’t blinked.

Tonight would make twenty-four.

Jerrick’s blood hit the dirt between them.

He wiped his mouth with the back of his hand and came forward again, dragging his right foot just enough to confirm what Silas had noticed four minutes ago. Something was wrong with that knee. Each time Silas shifted right, the man turned his whole body instead of trusting the leg to carry him, and each time he did it a little slower.

The crowd hadn’t noticed yet. They were too busy screaming.

“Graves!”

His name hit the Gutterlight Ring’s stone walls and came back louder. Boots rattled the metal stands. Veilmarks struck palms behind the chain-link. Somewhere near the eastern Bookkeeper box, a woman kept shouting for Jerrick to get his ass up, though he hadn’t fallen yet.

Silas would’ve respected the faith if she wasn’t about to lose money behind it.

Jerrick spat again. “You ain’t tired?”

Silas rolled his left shoulder. It had gone stiff around the eighth minute, right after Jerrick caught him with an elbow he had no business landing, and it hadn’t loosened since. His ribs ached on the same side. Nothing felt broken, but morning was still finna be rude as hell.

He raised his hands. Jerrick laughed through the blood on his teeth. “Ain’t got shit to say now?”

Talking wasted breath, and Silas had already given this man twelve minutes more than he deserved.

A red ward burned around the lamps above the Ring, marking the match steel-legal. Both fighters had been searched before entering. Two blades each. Nothing dipped in poison, dressed in Silencecraft, or carrying a death tether that could follow somebody home. The Cage Wardens checked for all three and still missed something every fight night.

The official Pit rules prohibited killing. The Floor Judge had repeated that twice before the gate opened, loud enough for the witnesses and the record charm hanging over his head.

Now his chair faced the Bookkeepers.

Jerrick lunged.

Silas stepped outside the punch and drove two knuckles into the soft space beneath his ribs. Jerrick folded around the hit. Silas caught the back of his neck, brought his knee up, and felt cartilage give.

The stands roared like they’d paid for it, and most of them had. Jerrick stumbled back with both hands over his face. Blood ran between his fingers, but he stayed on his feet. Lowtorch men knew how to stand through pain. They spent their lives beside furnaces hot enough to blister skin through a work coat, and burn scars climbed from Jerrick’s collar to his jaw, shiny and tight under the red light. A broken nose wasn’t going to put a man like that down. Silas had quit counting on pain to slow him.

The bad knee was still useful, though.

Jerrick wiped his nose and turned toward the raised platform across the Ring. Mother Rae sat between two Bookkeepers with one leg crossed over the other, black fabric covering her from throat to ankle. Silver bells circled her wrist, quiet despite the noise around her. She hadn’t cheered. She hadn’t touched the glass at her side. Her eyes followed Jerrick without giving him a damn thing to read.

He shouldn’t have looked at her.

Silas hit him before he turned back around. His shoulder drove into Jerrick’s chest and sent him back three steps. Jerrick swung wild. Silas ducked under it, caught the wrist, and twisted until the knife hidden beneath the sleeve dropped into the dirt.

So that was what the Wardens had missed tonight.

Silas kicked it under the chain-link. A runner snatched it up before it stopped sliding and disappeared into the crowd. Jerrick’s eyes jumped to the gate. Nobody there moved to help him.

“You know what this is,” Silas said.

Jerrick’s face tightened. “Wasn’t my fault them little niggas took a dirty route.”

The runner nearest the gate went still. He couldn’t have been much older than the boys Jerrick had sold out. His brown route coat hung too wide across his shoulders, and a burn mark crossed the front where somebody had tried to take off the block seal stitched there. He’d heard every word. So had every runner in the stands.

Silas turned back to Jerrick. “You sold it.”

“I sold information. What they did after ain’t got shit to do with me.”

Three runners had disappeared along that line. Fifteen, seventeen, and nineteen. Remy had spent two nights going through burial lists while Jude tracked every holding ward between Shadowmere and Stoneveil, and Jerrick had known all of it when he signed for this match. He’d taken it anyway. Maybe he thought the crowd would protect him. Maybe somebody had told him Silas wouldn’t kill under Gutterlight with this many witnesses.

Folks heard rules and forgot to ask who paid for them.

Across the Ring, Mother Rae tapped two fingers against her knee.

Make it clean.

Jerrick caught the signal too. His hand went for the blade at his waist.

Silas’s came out first.

He crossed the space between them and struck Jerrick’s wrist before the knife cleared its sheath. Bone snapped under his grip. Jerrick screamed and swung with his other hand, catching Silas across the mouth, and by the time the sting registered Silas was behind him with an arm locked over his chest.

Jerrick fought hard once he understood. His boots dug trenches through the dirt. His good hand clawed at Silas’s forearm. The back of his skull caught Silas high on the cheek and sent a sharp ache up under his eye, but Silas held on.

“Wait,” Jerrick gasped. “Wait, man.”

Silas set the point of his blade beneath Jerrick’s ribs.

“Graves—”

The knife went in clean.

Jerrick jerked against him. Silas kept his arm firm across the man’s chest until the boots stopped scraping. Warm blood ran over his hand and packed beneath his nails. Jerrick dragged in two more short breaths, and then the weight in Silas’s arm changed. It stopped being a man he was holding and became something he was holding up.

He lowered Jerrick to the ground.

The silence lasted until Jerrick’s head touched dirt. Then the crowd hit the fence all at once.

Veilmarks flew overhead. People climbed onto benches for a look at the body while the Cage Wardens shoved them back with shock batons. Silas pulled his blade free. On the platform, Mother Rae rose, and her bells rang once before the crowd swallowed the sound. By the time he looked again, she was gone toward the exit.

He checked himself the way he’d check for a cracked rib. Stomach steady. Hands steady. Nothing in his mouth but the copper from his own split lip.

Twenty-four, then.

The Quiet Staff came through the service gate with a stretcher and two bowls of salt. One of them crouched beside Jerrick. The other waited, bowl braced against his hip, for Silas to step clear of the blood before he poured.

The salt wasn’t only cleanup. Every fight ended with a rite to power the ward lines, and the Quiet Staff had already begun it, their hands working the shapes low and fast over the dirt. Silas had watched it enough times that he barely followed their hands anymore. By the time they lifted Jerrick onto the stretcher, Gutterlight would already be making room for the next match.

He wiped his blade across Jerrick’s shirt and stepped away.

“Match concluded,” the Floor Judge announced once the Quiet Staff had control of the Ring. His eyes had dropped to the record sheet before the words were all the way out of his mouth, and they never went back to Jerrick.

By morning, the official report would say Jerrick died after the match ended. Shadowmere paperwork could make damn near anything true if the right person held the pen, and somebody with the last name Graves usually did.

Silas left the lie to the people who got paid to write it and headed for the fighter’s gate.

Remy was waiting outside the Cage with a bottle of water in one hand and Silas’s route coat over his arm. His locs were tied back off his face, though one had worked loose near his ear. Behind him, the Quiet Staff carried Jerrick through the service entrance. Remy didn’t turn to watch. He was too busy looking over Silas’s face for whatever he’d have to explain later.

“You look terrible,” Remy said.

Silas took the bottle. “You say that every time.”

The first swallow burned his split lip. He finished half the bottle anyway, then poured the rest over his head. Water ran through his locs and carried blood down the side of his neck and into his collar.

Remy shoved the coat against his chest. “Put this on before Camille see you.”

“I’m hot.”

“And bloody.”

Silas took the coat. Remy’s mama and Camille had known each other too long for him to treat that like an empty threat. He got one arm into the sleeve. Pain caught in his shoulder before he managed the other, and Remy saw it.

“That man caught you?”

“Once.”

Remy’s mouth pulled to one side. He’d watched the whole fight. He knew exactly how many times.

Silas reached for the duffel under the bench before Remy could argue the number. “You find the runners?”

The joke left Remy’s face. “Two.”

“Alive?”

“One.”

Inside, the crowd had already started roaring for the next match. Silas tightened his grip on the bag. “Which one?”

“Seventeen. Block medic got him tucked away.”

“And the other?”

Remy shook his head.

Three names had gone onto Jude’s list. One could come off. Another was moving to a different list altogether.

Silas pulled the strap over his good shoulder. “What about the youngest?”

“Still nothing.”

Nineteen, then. Silas put that away with the rest of it.

Through the open Cage door, the runner who’d snatched Jerrick’s hidden knife was working the rows with a bucket, collecting side bets like his hands hadn’t been shaking five minutes ago.

“Jerrick send anything before the fight?”

“Bookkeeper found a route token in his locker.” Remy lowered his voice. “Dawnveil stamp.”

Silas turned all the way toward him. “Real?”

“Jude’s checking.”

A real Dawnveil token could move a person past two guarded tiers. Lowtorch furnace men didn’t come by that kind of access on their own. If the token was real, somebody had put it in Jerrick’s hands. If it was fake, somebody had risked getting a whole family Muted to make the route look clean.

Either way, the youngest runner was still somewhere behind it.

Silas held out his hand.

Remy looked down at the open palm. “Jude has it.”

“I wanna see it.”

“I know. He knows too.”

Which meant Silas wasn’t touching that token until Jude was finished with it. He dropped his hand.

A bell rang inside the Ring, calling the next fighters from their Cages. Remy waited until the sound faded before he stepped closer.

“North route got Shrouds posted near the tram. Two I saw. Could be more by Back Market.”

Shrouds didn’t waste time standing around Shadowmere. They came down, took who they wanted, and left the cleanup for everybody else. Two of them standing still meant they were waiting on somebody.

“Take south,” Remy said. “Don’t use Mercer Passage.”

Silas adjusted the strap. “Send me the names from Jerrick’s ledger.”

“I will. You go home.”

Silas started down the corridor.

“And don’t double back,” Remy called after him. “Text me when you get in.”

Silas lifted one hand without turning around. “Yes, Daddy.”

“Fuck you.”

Cold water struck his face the second he stepped outside.

Rain collected along the cavern ceiling and slipped through cracks in the rock, falling hardest near Gutterlight, where the heat kept the stone sweating. Violet mist covered the lower half of the street. Neon burned through it in broken patches of pink and blue and laid itself across puddles deep enough to hide a knife.

He left the route coat open while he walked. Closing it would hide the blood, but it would also slow his hand if somebody tried him, and between being looked at and being slow, he’d take looked at.

Food stalls still crowded the Back Market despite the hour. A woman stirred something dark in a metal pot while her daughter called prices beside her. Two relic sellers argued over a jar that kept rattling on its own, and neither one wanted to be the one holding it. At the mouth of a shop, a threshold worker redrew marks over the stone, stopping every few strokes to wipe the rain away before it could blur them.

A black lantern hung above the northern alley.

Silas took the south route.

People gave him room without staring long. A few noticed the blood on his shirt and found something else to look at. Shadowmere manners. Whatever a person carried home from the Pit belonged to them unless they dropped dead in the street.

The tram platform stood three blocks from Gutterlight. Half its lamps had gone out, so everybody had crowded under the ones that still worked. A woman pulled her little boy closer when she saw Silas. He shifted the duffel in front of his shirt and took a place near the rear entrance, far enough off that she could stop watching him.

The south tram came in with a hard scrape against the rail. Its doors opened, and everybody boarded fast. Nobody wanted to be caught on a platform if the Shrouds decided to close the line.

Silas took the back seat and rested his bad shoulder against the wall. A metal panel covered the window beside him, polished enough to hand him back pieces of his own face: one cheek starting to swell, the split in his lip, blood drying dull in his beard.

Camille was gon’ raise hell.

His phone vibrated before the tram pulled away.

Rhea: You alive?

Silas: Yeah.

Three dots appeared, disappeared, then came back.

Rhea: Ma at your apartment.

Silas closed his eyes.

Another message came through before he could decide whether ignoring his mother counted as self-preservation.

Jude: Remy told me what you did.

Silas: What I do?

Jude: He dead ain’t he?

Silas: Check the route token.

Jude: Mind your business.

Silas put the phone away before he had to admit how much Jude sounded like their mother.

The tram jerked forward. Buildings passed close on either side, their upper windows disappearing into the mist. Colored lanterns marked the doors below. Amber meant the route was open. Blue warned that somebody was watching. Red told folks to keep moving unless they wanted to become part of the problem.

A speaker crackled above his head.

“Movement between tiers requires active authorization under the Doctrine of Descent. Present all route permissions when requested. Unapproved travel will result in immediate detainment.”

Across the aisle, the woman from the platform tucked the edge of her son’s coat over the route patch on his chest. Nobody else reacted. Silas looked at the pieces of himself in the window until she was done.

Twenty minutes later, the tram let him off at his stop.

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane sat between a tailor shop and a threshold worker who charged Upper Tier prices for Deep Tier work. The brick had gone black from years of rain. Somebody had painted over the old building number twice, but the three still showed through.

Silas climbed to 3C and stopped outside long enough to listen. Rhea had already told him who he’d find, but habit didn’t care. The radiator knocked against the wall. A pencil scratched over paper. Someone shifted in a chair near the kitchen.

He unlocked the door and found his mother at the counter in a threadbare robe and house shoes that should’ve been thrown away before Jude learned to walk. Her glasses rested on top of her head. A crossword lay open beneath one hand, and a cup of tea cooled beside it.

She didn’t look up when he came in.

“Seven letters. Ancient city buried beneath ash.”

Silas locked the deadbolt behind him. “Pompeii.”

“Too obvious.”

“Stabiae.”

Camille counted the boxes before she wrote it in. “Better.”

The apartment had started out as his. Somewhere between his twelfth fight and Camille getting a key, half his things had been moved to make room for hers. Eucalyptus dried over the sink. Ward ledgers filled the shelf beside his unpaid tram notices. Spools of funeral thread sat in a wooden tray on the table, each one labeled in Camille’s small handwriting.

She’d put a plant in his window too. Silas didn’t know what it was, but keeping anything that green alive in Shadowmere felt like showing off.

“Shoes,” Camille said.

He set the duffel beside the door. Blood had darkened the edge of one boot. He took both off without arguing and shrugged out of the coat.

“Good,” she said. “You finally learned not to talk back.”

“Don’t get excited. I’m just tired tonight.”

Her pencil kept moving. “How many rounds?”

“One.”

“Mmm-hmm.” Camille moved to the next clue. “Nine letters. ‘To make a choice without force.’”

“Volition.”

“Something you apparently don’t believe in.”

Silas went to the sink and turned on the cold water. “I make choices every day.”

“You choose how to do what you already decided you was gon’ do.”

“Still a choice.” He shrugged, and his shoulder made him regret it.

Camille lifted her head from the crossword. “You kill him?”

Blood lifted from beneath his nails and spun down the drain. “Yeah.”

She studied his face, nodded once, and went back to the puzzle. Whatever else she thought about it could wait until she decided he needed to hear it. Silas had been her son long enough to know that was worse than yelling.

He washed the blade next. Jerrick’s blood had dried near the handle where the water couldn’t reach, so he used the small brush Camille kept beside the sink for cleansing tools.

“You eat?” she asked.

“Protein bar.”

Camille made a face. “That ain’t food. I made stew.”

Silas almost told her the protein bar had technically contained food. He wanted to eat sometime tonight, though, so he kept that to himself.

He dried the knife and returned it to the sheath. When he flexed his hand, a sharp sting cut across his knuckles, and the breath he sucked through his teeth brought Camille out of her chair before he could hide it.

“What happened?”

“He bit me.”

Her whole face changed. “Let me see.”

Silas gave her the injured hand before she started searching the rest of him. Teeth had opened the skin across two knuckles. The cut wasn’t deep, but the edges were already swelling.

Camille caught his wrist and turned it toward the light. “People carry all type of nasty shit in they mouth.”

She opened the cabinet under the sink and pulled out a shallow tin. Silas knew Jude’s ointment by the smell before she had the lid all the way off.

“Not that.”

Camille looked at him. “Then let your hand rot.”

The dark ointment went cold the second she packed it into the cut. Silas tried to pull back. Camille tightened her grip on his wrist.

“Damn, Ma.”

She gave him the same silence that had ended arguments between all three of her children since they were old enough to talk.

Silas shut up and let her finish.

When she let go, the chain beneath his shirt slipped free. One link had caught near his collar, dark with dried blood.

Camille’s fingers closed around it. “Silas.”

“I didn’t see it.”

She turned the link between her fingers, checking both sides, and then her attention dropped to his wrapped hand.

“You open?”

“No.”

Killing Jerrick hadn’t required that. Steel had been enough.

Camille held his stare another second before she let the chain go. “Then how you don’t see blood on your neck?”

“It ain’t mine.” He took the chain back to the sink, wet a cloth, and cleaned between each link, then dried it and tucked it under his shirt again.

Camille watched him finish before she picked the crossword back up. “Fourteen across. ‘A song for the dead.’ Seven letters.”

“Requiem.”

Her pencil moved across the boxes while Silas lifted the lid off the pot. The stew was thick with potatoes and mushrooms, and the meat looked expensive enough that Camille would lie if he asked where she got it. He didn’t ask. He fixed a bowl and took the seat across from her.

“Where’s Jude?” she asked.

“With Remy.”

Camille filled in another square without looking up. “They working?”

“Something like that.” He blew across the spoon. “I think he’s staying in Ashvein tonight.”

His mother’s pencil stopped halfway through the next letter. “Six letters. ‘The responsibility one carries.’”

“Burden.”

Camille made a quiet sound in her throat and wrote it in.

Silas looked across the counter at her. “You picked this puzzle for me?”

“I don’t control the newspaper.”

He almost smiled.

They let the apartment go quiet after that. Camille worked through three more clues while Silas ate, and the radiator knocked behind them whenever a tram passed beneath the street. His shoulder had started throbbing in time with his pulse. He’d need to soak it before morning if he planned to lift his arm without cussing.

Camille tapped the pencil against the page. “Six letters. ‘One who arranges trade between two parties.’”

“Broker.”

“Could be.”

Silas stopped chewing.

Camille finally looked at him. “So is Mercer.”

There it was.

He set his spoon down. “What Daddy want?”

“I ain’t say he wanted anything.”

“You ain’t have to.”

Camille pushed her glasses higher on her head. “He wants you in his office tomorrow. Noon.”

Silas leaned back from the counter. “He ask?”

The look she gave him answered before she did.

Dante Mercer asked Camille for things. He asked Rhea too, even when both of them knew she’d already made up her mind, and he kept asking anyway. His sons got times, places, and instructions.

“What’s it about?”

“Don’t know.”

Silas glanced at the duffel by the door. “Anybody else gon’ be there?”

“He didn’t say.”

Jerrick’s route token carried a Dawnveil stamp. Shrouds were posted on the north line before the match was even over. And now Dante wanted him in the office by noon.

Jude needed to hurry up.

Silas pushed his bowl away.

Camille turned the crossword around and slid it across the counter. “Eight down.”

He read the clue.

A formal summons. Four letters.

“Writ.”

“I know.”

Silas looked up. “Then why you ain’t fill it in?”

“Was waiting on you.”

Four empty boxes sat beneath the clue.

“I’ll be there,” he said.

“As if you had a choice.” Camille laughed under her breath and nodded at his bowl. “Hurry up. Stew’s getting cold.”

Silas took the pencil and filled in the first three letters.

The point snapped against the final T.

## Chapter 2: Isola

Tevin had said he wasn’t about to do shit six times, which gave Isola maybe four minutes before he did something.

She kept her eyes on the papers spread across the dining table.

Three work chits. Two letters from Micah’s foreman. One month of clean attendance, minus the morning he’d shown up late and talked the man out of writing it down. Danika wanted proof before she’d even consider sponsoring him into Ironcross. Proof Micah could keep a job, keep a curfew, and go thirty days without letting somebody else’s problem become his.

Today made thirty.

If Tevin went back out that door, Micah wouldn’t make it to thirty-one.

“I’m telling you,” Tevin said, wearing a path between the window and the table. “Something don’t feel right.”

He stopped at the front window with both hands buried in the pouch of a brown hoodie two sizes too big for him. Tevin wasn’t a small man. He was six-one and solid, with the shoulders of somebody who’d carried coal crates since before anybody paid him for it, and the hoodie only made him look wider. Isola knew better than to say so. He’d throw a tantrum about it, and she didn’t have time for one today.

He’d retwisted the front of his locs, too. The rest hung loose around his face, but those first two rows were neat and still shining with oil, parted clean enough to show the little scar at his hairline. Fresh. He’d sat down and done that this morning, before he ever walked in here talking about something not feeling right.

Nobody fixed their hair to stay in the house.

“Keep your mouth closed next time.” Micah coughed into his fist from the couch. “Pacing ain’t gon’ help shit.”

Tevin cut his eyes at him. “Shut up.”

Micah grinned at the ceiling. Isola went back to the sponsorship packet before either one of them caught her mouth twitch.

The front room ran long and narrow, cut into the same red-brown stone as the rest of their row. Years ago, back when raids on the west block kept ending with doors kicked off their hinges, Omari had lined these walls with dark metal panels. He’d fit every one of them so cleanly against the stone that most people who sat on that couch never realized the house could seal itself until it already had.

The front window was thick whisperglass behind black security mesh. It gave them a crooked view of the lane, where morning had arrived without anything resembling sunlight.

Ashvein didn’t have a sky.

It had work bells.

The first had sounded two hours ago, waking the miners and the children responsible for packing their food. The second sent the freight lifts groaning down toward Lowtorch. By the third, the market stalls were open, the gambling boards were taking names for fights happening below them in Shadowmere, and coal smoke had settled over the streets in a dark film that would be on everybody’s collar by noon.

Torchlight wavered against the cavern walls past the glass. Coded lanterns hung above every door. Amber for safe. Blue for watched. Black for a house in mourning.

The Wraithmoor lantern burned amber.

For now.

Isola pressed her thumb against the last work chit. The noon courier would carry the packet to Ironcross for twelve Veilmarks, fourteen if the route inspectors were out. She’d counted the money twice before Tevin came in mad, and she’d have to count it again once he left, because money counted in the middle of an argument never came out right.

After that, she wanted a few hours where nobody needed her.

She didn’t have plans for the day, and that was the plan. Micah’s packet would be gone. Tevin could stay out of trouble for one afternoon. Whatever Selah had written on Mother Rae’s order slip could wait. Isola wanted to shut her bedroom door, eat something before it went cold, and hear her own thoughts without somebody calling her name from another room.

Tevin passed the table again. “You heard what he said?”

Isola kept the work chits lined up beneath her palm. “I heard you the first time you told us.”

“And you cool with it?”

“I ain’t cool with nothing before breakfast.”

“Lo.”

She finally looked up. Tevin had stopped at the window again, and he wasn’t pacing anymore, which was worse. Pacing meant he was still talking himself out of trouble. Standing still meant he was done with that part and waiting on her to give him permission to go find some.

“What you want me to say?”

“That he can’t talk about Dani like that.” Tevin’s jaw tightened. “Nigga called her somebody’s kept bitch in front of everybody.”

Micah stopped smiling.

The pipes knocked behind the wall. Somewhere above the block, a drill bit into the cavern rock and sent a low vibration through the ceiling. Dust shook loose near the vent and drifted through the thin beam of amber light above the stove.

Isola felt the words land somewhere under her ribs and left them there. Somebody in this room had to not move.

She folded the packet closed. “Does he know Danika?”

Tevin frowned. “He ain’t got to.”

“Then why the hell you about to fight him over a story he made up?”

“He said it in front of everybody.”

“And if you put hands on him, they gon’ repeat it in front of more.”

Tevin pushed away from the window. “Let ’em.”

“You think this about your pride?”

“It’s about family.”

“No. It’s about you being mad and wanting a holy reason to hit somebody.”

He planted both hands on the edge of the table, right on top of the foreman’s letters. A pale scar crossed his left knuckle. He’d carried that scar for years and given the family three different stories about how he got it.

The back-room latch clicked.

Her father hadn’t come out, but he was listening now. Omari let people make their choices first. He got involved after that.

Tevin heard the latch too. When he spoke again, his voice had come down.

“So I’m supposed to let him disrespect us?”

“You supposed to think.” Micah pushed himself up straighter on the couch. “What good it do if we can’t find your ass this time around?”

Tevin dragged a hand over his mouth. “I handle that boy, the rest of ’em learn not to try us.”

“The rest of ’em learn exactly how to move you.” Isola slid the foreman’s letters out from under his hands and leaned back in her chair. “Say Dani name, you jump. Mention the money, you jump higher. Next time they want you somewhere, all they gotta do is run they mouth and wait.”

She’d known Tevin long enough to catch the moment anger made room for sense. The trouble was getting sense to stay. He’d been her first real friend, back before either of them knew friendship and blood weren’t the same thing. He’d followed her through a collapsed mining path because she dared him to, and years later he’d carried her three blocks after she twisted her ankle, complaining the whole way and never once putting her down.

Tevin loved his people loud, and Ashvein always heard him coming.

He held her stare a few seconds longer before his eyes dropped. “I can’t let it slide.”

“You can and you will.”

His jaw moved once. “I hear you.”

A lie. He was going out the first chance he got.

If he left, Isola could send word to the aunties near the east gate before he got far. He knew that as well as she did. He’d borrow Micah’s clothes to get past them, and taking his knife wouldn’t stop him either. Tevin would find something else sharp enough to carry.

She went back to the packet and waved a hand toward the hall. She’d given him all the eye contact he was getting today. “Go be cool somewhere I can’t see you.”

Tevin snatched his coat off the back of the chair.

“Leave that.”

He turned. “For what?”

“You don’t need a route coat in the bedroom.”

His eyes went from the coat to Isola. Whatever argument crossed his mind must not have been worth Omari hearing from the back room, because Tevin dropped the coat over the chair and went down the hall.

The spare-room door shut hard enough to rattle the framed names beside it.

Four generations of Wraithmoors covered that wall in handwritten chains. Given names, mother-line names, godbindings, block names. Some were stitched in funeral thread. Others had been pressed into metal by Omari’s grandfather. Ashvein families had been keeping their own records for generations, because trusting the Synod to remember somebody was an easy way to lose them.

The Wraithmoors went farther.

Their names answered.

The portrait beneath the chains had gone crooked when Tevin slammed the door. Isola got up to straighten it and stopped beside Micah’s section of the wall. His household copy of the Ironcross packet was folded behind the narrow strip that held his name. The official papers would leave with the noon courier. The copy stayed here.

“Kai.”

Micah looked over from the couch, already smiling. “It’s time?”

Isola nodded. “Hold still.”

She pressed two fingers to the metal.

Micah Amari Wraithmoor.

She spoke his full name in the cadence Omari had taught them, keeping the rhythm low and even. The words themselves weren’t anything special. Saying them right was the whole difference.

Warmth gathered under her fingertips. The funeral thread wrapped through Micah’s name pulled taut for one breath and settled again. A brief pressure crossed her palm, like something on the other side of the metal pressing back.

It held.

Isola kept her fingers there a second longer than she needed to. Then she tucked the copy more securely behind the strip and straightened the portrait.

Micah was already getting up when she turned around.

He was taller than Isola and leaner than Tevin, built quick instead of heavy. His short locs had been cleaned up around the sides, and the white shirt under his lightweight jacket made him look innocent enough to fool anybody who hadn’t raised him. His eyes did the worst damage. Wide, dark, expressive. They could turn apologetic before Micah had even decided what he was sorry for.

Isola had watched him learn how to lie. He still wasn’t good at it.

She put a hand flat against his chest before he reached the hallway. “Stay out of his shit, Micah.”

Micah leaned back against the arm of the couch instead of sitting down. “Who said I was following Tevin?”

“You did when you put your shoes on.”

His eyes dropped to them before he could stop himself.

Isola wiggled her fingers. Micah sucked his teeth, pulled the phone from his pocket, and slapped it into her palm. “This some bullshit.”

She set it on the table beside the Ironcross packet. “Whatever you say. Come on. Help me clear this table for breakfast.”

Micah had made it halfway to the dining end of the room when the back-room door opened. Omari stepped into the hall in a charcoal shirt, dark work pants, and the watch he’d owned since before Isola was born. The thing lost eleven minutes every day, but that ain’t stop him from winding it every morning. His heavy keyring hung from one belt loop, carrying enough house, storage, and route keys to open half the block.

His eyes moved over Micah, the sealed packet, then the names on the wall.

“Everything hold?”

“Yeah,” Isola said.

Omari nodded once. “Problem?”

“Handled.”

It was mostly true. Isola had decided mostly would do until noon.

“Good.”

He crossed the front room and checked the deadbolt. Once. Twice. His fingers stayed near the upper lock a moment before he let it go and went back the way he came.

Micah waited until the door closed. “He scary for no reason.”

Isola didn’t answer that. She tucked the courier envelope under her arm and headed for the kitchen before Micah found another reason to wander.

The kitchen smelled like scorched coffee, smoked roots, and the protective incense Selah lit every morning near the rear threshold. Bunches of dried eucalyptus and nightroot hung above the sink. Small packets of salt had been wedged into the corners of every window. A kitchen knife with a dark wooden handle rested beside the cutting board, sharpened so clean the edge disappeared under the light.

Her mother stood over the counter with a rag clenched in one hand, humming under her breath.

The same six notes, over and over.

Selah wore a long cream house dress under a brown apron. Her locs were wrapped in dark fabric, tied the same way she’d tied it for as long as Isola could remember, and the wrap left her round face, watchful eyes, and the protective charm at the base of her throat bare. She looked like an older version of Isola, with broad hips and heavy arms that had carried babies, supply crates, injured neighbors, and once a grown man who’d gotten himself stabbed outside their door.

“Mama.”

The humming went on.

Isola crossed the kitchen and touched her wrist. Cold bit into her fingers. “You take something?”

Selah shook her head. “Don’t need nothing.”

“You been rubbing your temples.”

Selah stopped scrubbing long enough to look at her. Her eyes settled on Isola’s face, then drifted toward the front room. Isola took the rag from her, and Selah let it go. That worried Isola more than the headache. Her mother would argue over a spoon if she thought somebody was trying to help without permission.

“Block feel hot,” Selah said.

A flash moved through the cavern beyond the kitchen vent.

Blue-white light crawled along the rock face across from the house, catching fire escapes, hanging bridges, and the crooked roofs pressed up under the ceiling. For half a second, Ashvein looked carved from bone.

Then it was gone, and every grounding wire in the house gave a faint metallic hum.

Isola wrung out the rag. “Boy from the east end been running his mouth about Dani money. Tevin heard. You think that’s what you feeling?”

Selah pressed two fingers to her temple. “Could be.”

“Mama.”

“The block loud this morning.” Her gaze went back to the vent. “Too many feet where they don’t belong. I don’t know what your cousin done got himself into, but Isola—”

Isola. Not Lo. Selah didn’t spend her whole name on small things.

Her mother turned from the counter to face her, and the cold coming off her seemed to reach Isola before the words did.

“Whatever it is, don’t go looking. A lie don’t care who tell it.”

Isola looked away first, out the kitchen window. Three boys stood beneath the coded lantern across the lane. One wore the red band of a west-block crew around his arm. Another had covered his colors with a work coat, though his boots gave him away.

None of them looked toward the Wraithmoor house. Boys that age looked at everything. These three were working at not looking.

“Ashvein always lying,” Isola said.

“Not like this.”

Selah laid one palm against the counter. The cold in her skin left a faint patch of fog on the metal trim, and the humming started again.

Isola turned off the burner under the roots before they blackened. The folded order slip sat in Selah’s apron pocket, one corner showing near her elbow. Isola knew she was going to regret it, but the words came out before she could stop them. “You need something, Mama. Just say it.”

“Funeral-grade salt.”

Isola exhaled through her nose. “What she want for it?”

“No telling. You know how your godmother is.”

Isola did. That was the problem.

Another flash lit the lane. The grounding wires whined higher.

Isola set the rag down. “Where Asha at?”

“Running errands or something.”

“What kind of errand?”

Selah looked at her.

“Right,” Isola muttered. “Nobody tells me shit till it go wrong.”

“You and your daddy the only two people care about the ending.” The corner of Selah’s mouth lifted. “Don’t need to bother y’all with the middle. Now gone round that corner and bring them dishes out your cousin’s room for me.”

Isola dropped the rag beside the sink. “Everybody in this house got legs but me.”

“And yet yours work the best.”

Isola sucked her teeth and left the kitchen before Selah could add anything else to the list. The hall was quieter than the front of the house. She slowed outside the spare room long enough to hear nothing. Tevin had finally stopped moving around in there, and she couldn’t decide whether that was better. The kitchen noise thinned behind her the farther she went.

Asha’s door was already open.

Isola leaned in before stepping through. “Asha?”

Nothing.

The dishes Selah wanted were on the desk by the window. Isola crossed the room and started stacking them, moving a shirt off the back of the chair when it got in her way. Most of Asha’s clothes weren’t in the wardrobe anyway. They were folded into the bags lined up against the wall.

Asha always said she liked being prepared.

Isola had stopped asking what for.

She picked up the bowl and reached past the nearest bag for the glass on the windowsill. One of her Ironcross rings caught the side pocket when she pulled her hand back, and the fabric tore.

“Damn it, Asha.”

She caught the bag before it hit the floor, but something inside slipped free. Metal struck stone and rolled under the desk.

Isola set the dishes back down. “What the hell?”

She crouched and reached underneath.

The coin stopped against her fingers.

Hexagonal. Dark silver around a black center. Quicksilver etched across the face.

A Veilmark.

She turned it over. The Veiled One’s face was stamped into the middle, with the prayer circling the edge. The quicksilver should have caught the light all the way across, but when she tilted it toward the lamp, the shimmer flashed near one corner and died before it reached the other side.

Isola frowned and reached into the hidden pocket of her skirt for one of Micah’s courier coins. She held them side by side on her palm. The cut matched. So did the black center and the prayer around the rim. She tapped them together. Micah’s coin rang low and full. The other one sounded flatter and lighter, as if the metal inside it wasn’t right.

She rubbed her thumb across the face. Grey-black residue came up out of the grooves.

It was good enough to pass in bad light. A tired vendor could take it without looking twice, and by the time anybody caught the difference, the coin would already have moved on.

Forging Veilmarks was High Heresy. The sentence was public Silencing, ritual suffocation carried out where the tier could watch. Isola had seen one. A woman from the mining district on her knees in the square while the Synod took her breath, and by the time it was over, she’d scraped her fingers bloody against the stone.

Isola looked back at the bags against the wall.

She had come in here for dishes. Now she was crouched in her cousin’s room with a counterfeit Veilmark in her hand, and every one of those neat, packed bags had started to look like an answer to the question she’d quit asking.

She ran a hand over her face. The Synod wouldn’t care how the coin got here if they found it first. A false Veil tied to this house could bind everybody under Omari’s roof to it before anybody bothered asking whose coin it was.

“Lo?” Selah called from the kitchen.

Isola looked toward the open door. “I’m coming.”

She slipped Micah’s coin back into her skirt and turned the counterfeit over once more.

Telling Selah right now would put the whole house in motion before Asha even made it home. Omari would lock everything down, and once that happened, Isola wasn’t getting an answer out of anybody before the whole family knew something was wrong.

She wasn’t ready for that yet.

She pushed the counterfeit through the tear in the side pocket and worked it under the lining until it disappeared. The fabric sat crooked where her ring had caught it, but she couldn’t do much about that without making the damage worse.

She stood the bag back up and gathered the dishes.

Down the hall, the clock in the front room ticked loud enough to hear from here.

Asha had until the noon courier. If she came home before then, Isola would ask her herself. If she didn’t, Micah’s packet was still going to Ironcross, and Isola would take the salt run into Shadowmere. At least that gave her a reason to cross tiers without anybody asking questions. She could get the coin checked while she was down there and find Asha before she brought any of this back to the rest of the house.

Isola balanced the dishes against her hip and stepped into the hall.

So much for nobody needing her today.

By the time she reached the kitchen, Selah had started humming again.

The same six notes.

Isola set the dishes beside the sink.

Selah glanced over. “Find all of ’em?”

“Yeah.”

Her mother looked at her for another second before she reached for the bowl.

Isola turned toward the front room before Selah could ask anything else.

Behind her, the humming stopped.

# Part Three: What I Changed and Why

Most sentences in the rewrite are still yours. I changed only what I could defend.

## Chapter 1

- **I paid off the countdown.** I added "He checked himself the way he'd check for a cracked rib… Twenty-four, then." The opening promises a number, and Silas registers that number the way he registers everything else: as a physical inventory. He doesn't feel guilty and he doesn't explain himself.
- **The body becoming weight.** "It stopped being a man he was holding and became something he was holding up." This replaces a plain report of the death with what Silas's arm actually notices.
- **I turned two repeats into callbacks.** "So that was what the Wardens had missed tonight" picks up your setup line instead of restating it. I cut "Jerrick was used to hurting and staying upright" and kept the new information, that pain wasn't going to work on him.
- **Silas does the math.** "Nineteen, then." He subtracts the runners by age himself. That's his mind doing the work, not new information.
- **I let the slur land in the room.** "He'd heard every word. So had every runner in the stands." This uses only the people you already put in the scene.
- **Small manners beats.** Silas stands where the frightened mother can stop watching him. He looks at his own reflection while she covers the patch. He listens at the door out of habit even though Rhea already told him who's inside. Each one is Shadowmere manners shown as behavior.
- **Camille.** "Silas had been her son long enough to know that was worse than yelling." This turns her nod into relationship knowledge.
- **Mechanics.** I changed sound-verb tags to beats. "Shrouds had closed the north line" is now "were posted," to match what Remy actually said. A few runs of sentences opening on "Silas" now open on the object or the other person. I merged one-line beats that weren't turns ("He raised his hands," "He dropped his hand").
