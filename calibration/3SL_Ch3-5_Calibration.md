## Chapter 3: Silas

Silas soaked his shoulder before leaving the apartment. It bought him enough movement to get dressed without help, which was about all the good it did. By the time he reached the Mercer building, rain had worked through his coat, and the muscle under it had started tightening again, one slow notch at a time.

Nothing on the outside said that it belonged to them, but everyone in Shadowmere knew it did. Three route marks were cut into the stone beside the frame: Access, Passage, and Return, their edges worn smooth by years of hands. People who needed the Mercers already knew the address. Everybody else could keep walking.

He stepped inside one minute before noon and pulled the wet collar away from his neck. One minute early was the only kind of early that didn't look like nerves, and the only kind of late Dante couldn't call late.

A man stood at Lorraine's desk with both palms flat on a route permission she hadn't accepted. Behind him, a courier held a wardcloth package against her hip, keeping it clear of the water dripping off everybody's coats.

"He said I could come through," the man told Lorraine.

"Then he can put his name on it." Lorraine slid the permission back under his hands and held out her palm for the courier's package. Her grey locs were wrapped high, leaving the old route marks along her neck bare. Silas had never asked about those marks. In this building, that counted as manners. She checked the number tied to the cloth against her ledger, then set the package out of the man's reach. "Move over. You blocking somebody who finished their business before they got here."

The man stepped aside just far enough for Silas to pass. His eyes dropped to the bandage across Silas's knuckles, climbed to the swelling under his eye, and went back to Lorraine. Whatever else he'd been about to say could apparently wait. Silas couldn't tell whether the man knew his face or only knew what a face like that usually meant. In Shadowmere, it came to the same thing.

"Mother Rae just left," Lorraine said.

Silas stopped wiping the rain off his face. He didn't like being the second stop on anybody's route, least of all hers. "She say what she wanted?"

"Now you know that woman don't tell none of us a damn thing." Lorraine turned a page in the ledger, already finished giving him whatever she had. She pointed the courier toward a broker waiting beside the inner hall, then caught the man reaching across her desk again without lifting her head. "That permission ain't changed since I handed it back."

Silas left her to it. A scanner chirped behind him as the broker checked the package. Farther down the hall, somebody argued through a half-open office door about a passage that had closed after payment, and the words cut off as the door shut. Silence-thread crossed the ceiling over his head, patched near the third office where the new stitching didn't quite match the old. He'd learned years ago that pausing under it wouldn't get him anything but Lorraine calling his name from the desk.

He passed the brokers' offices and knocked once on Dante's door.

"Come in."

Silas shut the door behind him and reached for the lock. Halfway there, he felt somebody watching his hand, and he stopped.

A woman sat on his father's sofa, holding a crushed envelope against her stomach. Both of her hands were on it. She'd pulled her feet up under the heavy skirt of a mourning dress, though one ruined slipper showed at the hem. Black silk covered her to the wrists and throat. The stones sewn into her collar were finer than anything sold near Gutterlight, finer than anything Silas had seen anywhere, now that he thought about it. The pins left in her crimson hair had mostly given up holding it out of her face.

"You missed my call," Dante said.

His father stood behind the desk, tipping a bottle over a short glass. He'd kept his shoes on. By noon they were usually under the desk, and his route coat would be thrown over whatever chair Silas normally took. The coat was there now, but the shoes were still on his feet, and the Mercer keys still hung from his belt. A man who planned to sit all afternoon took his keys off. Dante had either just come in or expected to go back out, and he hadn't decided which Silas should believe.

"You know how your wife gets," Silas said, glancing back at the woman.

Her eyes were green enough to stay green under Dante's dim-ass office lamps. Warm brown skin, full mouth. She was fine, but it wasn't Shadowmere fine. A woman who looked like that didn't pass through a lane. She got described afterward. Even with her face swollen from crying, nothing about her belonged to the nearby lanes. Shadowmere girls learned early how to sit so they could get up fast. She sat like nobody had ever needed her to clear the space when they walked into the room. She caught him looking and lifted her chin a little.

"Silas, this is Melena," Dante said.

Melena didn't offer her hand. Silas wasn't about to reach for it anyway. "What happened to her shoe?"

Her mouth tightened.

Dante set the bottle down. "She walked."

"From where?"

"Halflight."

Silas looked at the slipper again. The sole had split near the toe, and a dark stain climbed the silk where her foot had bled into it. Between Halflight and here was nothing but stairs, freight paths, and people who would have robbed her clean if she'd looked like she belonged in Shadowmere. Nobody unfamiliar with the lower tiers made that walk and arrived with only one ruined slipper. Either somebody had walked it with her, or she'd been lucky. Silas had stopped believing in luck around the same time he'd stopped throwing up after fights.

"By herself?" Silas raised an eyebrow.

Melena stood before Dante could answer. The dress pulled against her knees, and she set her bad foot down carefully, as if she'd spent the last few hours learning exactly how much it would take. A dark red seal had broken across the fold of the envelope in her hands. He couldn't make out the mark from where he stood, and he noticed she'd turned it so he couldn't. "You can direct your questions to me. I am standing in front of you."

Nobody down here said *I am* when *I'm* would do. She talked like somebody who'd been corrected for less. Silas looked up from her foot. "Did you come from Halflight by yourself?"

"Yes."

"You get followed?"

"No."

"You check?"

Her fingers tightened around the envelope.

Silas turned to Dante. "She got followed."

"I did not."

Dante took a drink before either of them could go another round. His silver ring clicked against the glass. "Rae opened the final routes, if you must know."

*If you must know* was what Dante said when he was handing over the least he could get away with. Rae had either walked the girl down herself or opened the doors ahead of her. Either way, she'd made sure she was gone before Silas could ask her anything about it. Melena's mouth tightened again. She didn't correct him, but her grip changed on the envelope. The incense smoke above Dante's desk wavered as if somebody had opened a door.

Nobody had.

Heat spread through the office, too fast to have come from the lamp or the radiator under the window. Melena pulled one hand inside her sleeve. The silk darkened where her fingertips caught it, and a thin curl of smoke rose beside the mourning stones.

Silas drew his knife and stepped between her and the desk. His shoulder caught halfway through the movement, a hot, bright stop that nearly knocked the blade from his grip, so he kept it in his good hand and held it low, where she could see it without looking down. Held high, a knife started arguments. Held low, it only told somebody where the line was.

Silver lit the doorframe. Cold pushed out of the wall seals and lifted the hair on the back of his neck, but the heat stayed on his face. He'd felt those seals answer before, and the room usually settled once they did. This time Melena stumbled, and the smoke kept coming.

Everything Silas knew that could do this to a room came out of the space between living and dead. Thanomancers. Root-Listeners. Lament workers. People who could pull memories up out of the dark. He'd been raised around that kind of work. All of it had a pull to it, a cold underneath, a mark somewhere you could find if you knew how to look.

This wasn't that at all.

"Take your hand out your sleeve," Silas said.

She dragged it free. Black had spread beneath her nails and over the ends of her fingers like charcoal. She stared at them, then shut her hand around the envelope again. "I'm not going to hurt anyone."

Silas believed that she meant it. He'd also watched a lot of people mean things right up until they couldn't help it.

"Put the knife away," Dante told him.

Silas didn't turn. The heat had worked its way under his collar. His father was close behind him, close enough that stepping aside would put Dante straight in front of it, and Dante would never thank him for standing there. Silas stayed anyway. "You see her hands?"

"I see her." Dante crossed to the wall and laid his palm flat against a cold seal. The silver in the doorframe steadied. "She crossed seven tiers without burning any of them down. Give her some kind of credit, son."

Seven tiers. Silas counted them up from Shadowmere without meaning to and didn't like where he ended up. Whoever she was, she'd started a long way from any street he knew.

He eased back a step. Melena's eyes followed the knife, then went to the locked door, and another breath of heat crossed the office. Whatever she'd done to get through seven tiers, it wasn't helping her much now.

"Who saw you come down?" he asked.

"I wore a veil until Shadowmere." Her voice stayed level. The envelope trembled between her hands.

Dante picked up Rae's bell and rang it once.

Silas knew the sound before it finished. It sounded like the bells Rae wore around her wrist. She'd left it here on purpose, which meant she'd known this might happen. The low note reached him through the pressure in his ears. Melena gasped. The heat pulled back so sharply that he felt it leave his collar, and her knees folded before she could catch the sofa.

He put the knife away and caught her wrist. Her weight dragged him forward, and his shoulder told him exactly what it thought, but he held her up long enough for her other hand to find the leather. Her skin was still hot. Under his thumb, her pulse struck fast, missed, then came hard again. He counted three of them before she found her footing.

"Let me go." Melena planted both feet and pulled against his grip.

Silas opened his hand as soon as she had her balance. She stayed braced against the sofa, breathing through her mouth. The envelope had crumpled where she'd caught herself.

"Do not touch me without asking," she said.

He looked at the hand she'd pressed flat against the leather. It was holding her well enough now. "All right."

He'd have caught her again. Next time he'd ask first if she had time to ask, and if she didn't, she could be mad at him upright.

Dante set the bell back on the desk. Silas wiped his palm on his coat and waited for the explanation his father made no move to give. Dante never explained things in front of the people they were about. He explained them later, in pieces, or he let Silas walk around believing he already had.

Instead, Dante opened the map-room door. "Come here a minute."

Melena's head lifted. His father stopped, one hand on the frame, and slid a plate of bread within her reach. "Sit down. Eat something."

"I don't need—"

"Water too," Dante said, without raising his voice.

Silas had been on the other end of that voice his whole life. It sounded like a suggestion until someone tried to treat it like one.

He followed his father into the private room. Through the whisperglass, he watched Melena lower herself onto the sofa with the envelope still in her hand. Dante shut the door, and the sound seal turned blue. The office went quiet on the other side of the glass.

His father had left the route map uncovered. Three threads shifted beneath it, drawing tight near the north line before disappearing past the edge of the frame. Remy had counted two Shrouds on the north route last night. Dante's map had apparently counted them too. Silas leaned his good hand against the table and waited while Dante pulled a black pin from a closed passage.

"You know what just happened in there," Silas said.

"Yes."

He waited another second. Dante fitted the pin over Back Market.

"You gon' tell me?"

"She needs somewhere she can stay without being seen. Rae's routes are being watched. Quietcasters been walking the edges of where Rae had her."

Quietcasters didn't walk anywhere for the exercise. If they were circling Rae's routes, the Synod had a reason, or believed it did. Silas thought about Jerrick's token sitting under Jude's lamp, stamped with a Dawnveil mark and the fifteen-year-old nobody had found yet. He thought about saying some of it. Dante paid for information by the piece, though, and Silas didn't have enough to make a fair trade with his father.

Through the glass, Melena had set the envelope beside the water. She tore a piece off the bread and held it without eating.

"So you bringing her through ours."

"I'm moving her into one of your mama's houses." Dante pressed the pin into place. A line opened beneath the glass, running south from the Mercer building toward Gutterlight. Silas knew the turn behind the tailor row before his father traced it. "You'll take her."

Silas studied the narrow stretch beside the market. At this hour, the freight crews would be backed up at the public passage, which helped. The lane behind the sheds would be full of people buying funeral goods, which didn't. Dante was playing with fire. "Why me?"

"Rhea's on a contract that touches the Synod. Jude's too busy doing whatever that boy does when he doesn't answer the phone. You know the routes, and you know Camille's thresholds." Dante moved another pin, closing the public approach behind the house.

Silas knew exactly what Jude was doing. He kept his face expressionless. "You got handlers."

"I got men who'd mistake her being scared for permission." His father met his eyes across the table. "You won't."

It sounded like trust. With Dante, trust usually came with a job attached, and you didn't find out how heavy the job was until you'd already picked it up.

Silas had moved people who fought him every step of a route and people who wouldn't lift their heads long enough to learn where they were going. He'd moved the sick and the hunted. None of them had done what Melena had just done in Dante's office. Knowing the streets wouldn't tell him what to do if it happened halfway across one.

"Bell going with her?" he asked.

"No."

"Then what am I supposed to do if that happens again?"

"Get her inside. Keep people away from her."

"You giving me a job and leaving out the part that can get us caught."

Dante turned his ring under his thumb. "I'm giving you what you need to move her. Keep the coat closed. Stay off the public approaches. Don't let anybody stop you to ask questions."

"Who's looking?"

"Lorraine counted two watchers near the front." Dante went back to the map, leaving the rest blank.

Silas had walked through that front door ten minutes ago and hadn't seen anybody watching it. He didn't like that Lorraine had. He liked even less what that said about how much last night was still sitting in his body.

Silas glanced at the closed public approach. "Why doesn't this sound harder than it should? What is it that you're not telling me?"

Dante checked the south line once more before sighing. "I need you to keep Shadowmere from eating her while she's too busy grieving to see its mouth open."

So, somebody had died, and Dante knew who. Silas didn't ask. He'd get the same nothing he'd gotten on everything else, and Dante would charge him for the question.

Through the glass, Melena finally put the bread in her mouth. She chewed slowly, staring at the paper beside her plate. Eighteen, maybe. Young enough that somebody should have been sitting with her while she grieved, instead of a sofa and a plate of bread. Mourning clothes were common in Shadowmere, sure, but people usually wore them with pride. Not…whatever the hell was written on Melena's face.

"Who decides when she leaves?" Silas asked.

"Mother Rae. Your mama will check on her later." Dante's hand stopped over the final pin. "She ain't regular cargo, and she ain't yours. You understand me?"

Silas understood him fine. She wasn't his to keep. He couldn't decide shit for her and once Rae came to collect her, he wasn't to answer for her if anyone came looking. Dante said it aloud so he wouldn't have to say it twice.

His father left the final pin where it was. Silas knew that tone. Arguing wouldn't reopen the route. Dante opened the map-room door instead. Melena had emptied the water. Half the bread was left, torn into pieces across the plate. She gathered the envelope before either man got close and smoothed one crushed edge against her knee.

Dante took his route coat off the chair. "You'll need this."

"Where are we going?" Her attention stayed on Silas. She'd already worked out which one of them would be walking with her. *Smart girl.*

"Safehouse." He stopped beside the sofa instead of crouching over her foot. "Your heel needs wrapping first. I can do it, or you can."

Melena looked down at the torn slipper. She held still long enough that he thought she might refuse, then lifted the hem clear of her ankle. "You may."

Nobody had ever told Silas he *may* do anything.

He brought the medical tin from Dante's cabinet and crouched on his good side. The slipper had rubbed the skin open above her heel, and the silk had dried into the wound. He eased it off slowly so it wouldn't tear the scab away, cleaned the skin, and laid a strip of cloth under her ankle before he started wrapping. Her hand tightened in her skirt when the damp cloth touched raw skin. He stopped and waited for her foot to settle before he finished, then closed the tin and put it in his coat.

Dante set a pair of boots beside them. Silas knew them by the cracked left heel. They were Rhea's, from back when Rhea still came by this office. Dante had kept them in a cabinet all this time like she might walk in and need them. Silas said nothing about it. Neither did his father.

Melena watched Silas check each boot for anything that might catch on the bandage, then took them from his hands and put them on herself. They had room at the toes, but the soles held when she stood.

"Rae's people checked your clothes?" Silas asked.

"Yes."

"I need to look too. Anything somebody can follow." He kept his hands where she could see them. "Seams and stones. You can stay sitting."

She looked toward Dante as if she expected him to intervene. Little did she know, Dante never made anybody's choice for them; he made them stand in it instead. After a moment, Melena sat back down and held out one cuff.

Silas checked what she offered first. The stones bore marks he couldn't make out even this close, in a language no one had taught him. He turned one far enough to see its back without pulling at the stitching. He worked around the collar and checked the pins she'd kept in her hair, stopping when she drew away so she could take out the last comb herself.

The damaged hem had picked up soot and residue from an old route. "Nothing I recognize."

Which wasn't the same thing as nothing but again, Dante loved information.

Dante held the coat open, and Melena slipped into it. Brown ward-thread lined the inside. It covered the silk to her calves, but her hair still showed above the collar. In this light it was the brightest thing in the room. Silas pointed. She gathered it up, and when he touched his own head, she pulled the hood over it all and fastened the front. She tucked the envelope inside the coat after one long look at its ruined corner.

"Walk on my left," he told her. He needed his right hand free, and his left wasn't good for much today anyway. "If I stop, you stop. Somebody calls you, keep moving."

"How will I know if they're calling me?"

He glanced at the black silk showing beneath the coat. "They'll find something to call you."

She pulled the coat lower and said nothing else.

Dante opened the south passage and put a brass token in Silas's hand. The route mark matched the line he'd set under the glass. Silas pocketed it, then looked back at him. "Who's staying tonight?"

"You. Outside her door." Dante's eyes went to Silas's shoulder, which he hadn't lifted since he walked in. "Let Camille look at that when she comes."

So Dante had noticed. Silas wasn't about to volunteer another injury for his mother to get into, though. He stepped into the passage and waited for Melena. She passed his father without offering her hand, and Dante closed the door behind them before she could look back. Silas couldn't tell whether that was mercy or habit. With Dante, he'd never found the line between the two.

Rain tapped against the grate over the southern exit. Silas stopped under it and checked the alley through the bars. A freight cart blocked half the street beyond. Two workers tried to turn it while a runner argued his package couldn't wait, and nobody had any reason to look toward the passage yet. He brought Melena out while the cart was still moving.

She stopped under the awning and tipped her head back. He followed her gaze past the hanging walkways to the cavern roof, where water fell through the violet mist. The only light up there came from a shop sign with two dead letters. She was looking for sky. Silas had seen people from the upper tiers do it for the first time this far down, searching the rock like it was a ceiling someone would eventually open. Nobody born under stone bothered. There wasn't anything up there for her to find.

He stepped into her line of sight. "This way."

She came with him, keeping close to the wall as the freight cart scraped past. He had to shorten his stride for the boots. She kept lifting the dress beneath the coat instead of letting the hem drag, and every time she did, black silk flashed at her ankles. At the next turn, he slowed enough for her to gather a fistful of it and hold it there.

Past the market, freight workers waited at the public passage with their route permissions out. A clerk scanned the first set and waved the cart through. Silas took Melena behind the sheds before anyone at the checkpoint noticed them. She looked back once but kept walking.

Behind the sheds, the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods, and the lane narrowed until the stall counters took up half of it. Silas knew these stalls as well as he knew his mother's handwriting. Half of them had sold Camille thread at some point, and the other half wished they had. Mourning thread hung in dark skeins above a basin catching water off an awning. Farther up, near the bend, a shopkeeper snapped a case of Hush shut as a boy drifted too close to the counter. The boy was too thin and too interested in everything at once.

There was no room to pass anyone without brushing coats. He kept Melena between himself and the freight wall and moved her along at a steady pace, because if she stopped to apologize to somebody, somebody would look at her face.

Pell's salt stall sat at the narrowest point, under a striped awning that leaked at one corner. A woman stood at the counter with a grey-paper bundle under her arm and her back to the lane, and as Silas came even with her, she said, "You'll make it up now," in a voice flat enough to set a cup on.

Pell reached for his scoop without a word. Silas had never seen Pell take an instruction without arguing it first. It snagged at the edge of his attention, like a sound from the wrong direction, and he let it go because up ahead, something crashed. Glass went across stone, and the shopkeeper yelled. The thin boy came out of the Hush shop at a dead run, straight down the lane at them with both arms wrapped around whatever he'd taken.

"Wall," Silas said.

Melena went to it. He stepped back, put himself between her and the boy, and walked his shoulders straight into someone behind him.

His left shoulder screamed through the whole turn, his bandaged right hand closing around a wrist that was already driving.

The punch stopped an inch from his chest.

A girl with rings. Three of them, heavy Ironcross rings across the knuckles. Then her feet. She'd set them before she swung, weight on the back one. Somebody had taught her that. People who'd never been taught swung from their shoulders and fell in after it. Red grit had dried on the toes of her boots. Ashvein dust, or somebody who'd walked through a lot of it. She was a foot shorter than him and hadn't seemed to notice.

It was only then that Silas realized she was cussing him out.

She'd started before her fist ever stopped, and she hadn't lost her place since. It wasn't the scattered kind of cussing people did when they got startled. It had an order to it. He was big as a damn door and couldn't look where he was going, and he had some nerve, and somebody should've taught him better than to back up on folks in a lane this tight, and she wasn't anywhere close to done.

She could hit. He'd have bet money on that even if her fist hadn't stopped an inch from his chest. The second thing he knew about her came right behind the first, with no manners at all.

Damn.

She was fine. Rain had caught in her locs and run off the ends where she'd pulled them back, and she had her face turned up to his with her whole chin in it, like being a foot shorter was his problem to solve. Her mouth was still going. It was a nice mouth to be cussed out by. He was still holding her wrist, and he noticed that about the same time she did.

He let go first. He stepped back and lifted both hands open in front of his chest where she could see them, and his shoulder had plenty to say about that too. He let it talk.

"You always swing first?"

"You always walk backwards?"

"Only when I'm in a hurry."

"Then slow down."

She hadn't lowered her fist all the way. The rings caught the light from Pell's lamp, all three lined up across her knuckles. "You was really gon' put all three of them in my chest."

"Still might."

He smiled before he could think better of it. It pulled at the split in his lip, and that hurt, and he kept smiling anyway, because she'd said it like a promise and he believed her. Her eyes dropped to his mouth when it happened. Only for a second. Then they came back up to his like they'd never left, and Silas thought, *Oh.*

"What's your name?"

"You ain't earned it."

"What's it cost?"

"More than you got on you."

"You don't know what I got on me."

She looked him over then, slow, from his face down and back again, and didn't bother pretending she wasn't doing it. "I know somebody else wrapped your hand. And somebody else already hit you in the face." One corner of her mouth went up. "Don't look like your day going too good."

Camille had wrapped that hand at the sink last night, and this woman had looked at it once and known he hadn't done it himself. His mother would have liked her. That was probably a bad sign.

"Day ain't over," he said.

It came out lower than he'd meant it to. She heard it. He watched her hear it, watched her decide not to smile and very nearly lose, and for maybe six seconds neither one of them said a thing. Water dripped off Pell's awning between them. Somebody behind her wanted to get by and didn't ask. He was close enough to smell the salt in the paper under her arm and the rain on both of them, and he wasn't in any hurry to fix that.

Then the rest of the lane came back, and with it the job.

He looked over his shoulder for Melena, and whatever he'd been about to say next went right out of him.

She'd pressed herself so hard into the freight wall that her hood had slid back. A length of red hair had come loose over the collar of Dante's coat, bright as a lamp in that grey lane, and anybody between here and the bend who'd turned around for the crash had gotten a good long look at it.

"Hood," he said.

Melena pulled it up with both hands. Silas moved her off the wall and up the lane without touching her, keeping his body on the stall side, and didn't let himself walk faster than the crowd. They passed the Hush shop, where the shopkeeper was down on one knee in the broken glass, cussing the boy and the boy's mother and the boy's whole block. The boy himself was long gone.

Just past the shop, Silas stopped and turned around.

She was still standing at Pell's counter with the bundle under her arm, and she was already looking at him. She didn't look away when he caught her at it. She held it, chin up, like she'd been waiting to see whether he'd turn around and had money on the answer.

"You made me lose my cousin," she said across the lane.

Silas hadn't known there was a cousin. From the way she said it, the cousin was going on his tab.

"Then I owe you."

He meant it more than a man should mean anything he said to a stranger in the Back Market. He took Melena around the corner before he could find out what she'd say back.

Melena didn't speak again until the market noise had thinned behind a wall of crates. "Did she take anything?"

He'd already checked. Token in his left pocket, tin in his right, knife where it belonged. "No."

"She tried to hit you."

"She tried."

Melena walked a few steps in Rhea's boots. "You smiled at her."

"That's just how my face sitting today."

"It was not sitting like that in my presence."

Silas didn't have anything for that, so he checked the crossing ahead instead. Melena let it go. He had a feeling it was the first thing since she'd come down here that had been any kind of funny to her, and he wasn't about to take it from her by arguing.

He moved her on toward the noise of Gutterlight with one eye on every doorway, and the whole way, he kept going back to that lane. *Still might.* The way she'd looked him over like she was pricing him and found him a little short. That corner of her mouth that never made it all the way to a smile, and how bad he'd wanted to see it get there. He went over it the way he'd go back over a fight, slow, piece by piece, and he got all the way to the end before he found the hole in it.

He'd asked her one thing, and she'd sent him off without it.

He didn't have her name. He had a salt stall, three Ironcross rings, a cousin, and a woman who thought he owed her. In Shadowmere, people had been found with less.

## Chapter 4: Isola

The noon courier took fourteen Veilmarks, two more than Isola had meant to pay. The route inspectors were out. She'd known they might be, and she'd brought the fourteen, and it still felt like being robbed by somebody polite.

He was a thin man with an Ironcross stamp on the back of his hand, and he checked the seal on Micah's packet twice before sliding it into the flat case at his hip. Isola counted the coins into his palm one at a time. She'd never listened to money before this morning. Now each one rang against the one before it on the way down, low and full, and she didn't let go of a single coin until she'd heard it.

Micah stood in the doorway behind her with his hands in his jacket, as if he could make the packet move faster by staring at it. When the courier stepped back into the lane, Micah let his shoulders drop for the first time since first bell.

"That's it?" he said.

"That's it." Isola shut the door. "Now you just gotta not do nothing stupid until Danika write back."

"I been not doing nothing stupid for thirty days."

"Twenty-nine and a morning." His eyes went to his phone on the table, and Isola picked it up before he could. "And you still ain't getting your phone."

He sucked his teeth and dropped onto the couch, which meant he'd given up for now. With Micah, *for now* usually lasted until he got hungry. Isola looked up at the clock over the name wall. The minute hand had slipped past twelve while the courier counted.

Noon had come. Asha hadn't.

That had been the deal Isola made with herself in Asha's room this morning. If Asha came home before the courier, Isola would ask her to her face. If she didn't, Isola would take the salt run and go find her. The clock didn't care which one Isola had been hoping for.

Asha's door was still open down the hall, the way Isola had left it. She went in and shut it behind her.

The bag stood where she'd left it against the wall, torn pocket turned toward the plaster. She'd folded the strap under it before she walked out this morning, and the strap was still folded. Nobody had touched it. Isola worked two fingers through the tear and felt along the lining until she found the coin and drew it out. She didn't look at it again. She'd already looked at it enough to know what it was.

She stood there with it closed in her fist and made herself think it through one more time. If she left it here, it stayed under Omari's roof, with every name on that wall in the front room standing right next to it. If she took it, the only person it could bind was the one who'd chosen to pick it up. And if Asha came home and went looking for it, she'd find the lining empty and know somebody had been in her things. Isola wanted her to know that.

She tucked it into the hidden pocket of her skirt, behind the seam where she kept the money, and stood the bag back up so the tear wouldn't show from the doorway. The rest of Asha's bags sat in their row against the wall, packed and buckled, the way they'd been sitting for two years. Isola had stopped asking what for a long time ago. Standing there with a counterfeit against her hip, she wished she'd kept asking.

In the kitchen, Selah was packing roots into a jar. The humming had started again sometime after breakfast and hadn't let up long enough for Isola to decide whether that was better or worse.

"I'm going for the salt," Isola said.

Selah wiped her hands on her apron and drew the order slip from her pocket. She didn't hand it over right away. She held it a second and looked at Isola the same way she'd looked at her over the dishes, then put it in her palm.

"Pell," Selah said. "Not nobody else."

"I know."

"Funeral grade."

"Mama. I know."

Selah went back to the jar. "Then you ain't got nothing to worry about."

Selah didn't repeat herself. When she did, she wasn't talking about the thing she was saying. Isola put the slip in her front pocket, where she'd see it every time she reached for money, and set Micah's phone face down on the counter. Selah looked at it, then at her, and moved the jar so it sat on top of the phone. It was as good as a lock.

Tevin's door was shut. Isola didn't knock, because knocking would tell him she was leaving. She checked the front room on her way out instead. His route coat was still hanging over the chair where he'd dropped it. Good.

The token Mother Rae had given her for runs hung on a cord under her shirt. Rae had never taught her to read the marks on its face, and Isola had never asked. She pulled it out in the lane and kept it in her fist all the way down the cliff stairs, past the shrine at the second landing and past the women hauling water up from the vent cisterns. One of them asked after Selah without stopping. Isola told her Selah was fine without stopping either. The stairs cost nothing and saved her the lift fare. She'd pay for the lift coming back if the salt was heavy.

The chasm bridge was loud with people trying to get across before the mist came up. It had come up twice already today, according to the boy selling grounding charms at the head of the span, and the second time three people had stood frozen in the middle for ten minutes because they couldn't see the rail. Isola could see the rail. She could see the whole bridge, the black stone arching over the vent and the violet glow breathing up out of the dark beneath it, and at the far end the Shadowmere guard post with its lamp and its line.

The line moved slowly. The guard was a big man in a mercenary's coat with half the buttons gone. He had a route scanner on a chain and a metal plate set into the counter in front of him. The woman ahead of Isola paid at the plate. She pricked her thumb on the needle beside it without being told and pressed it down, and the guard watched the plate drink until it had enough. The boy behind her either didn't have blood to spare or didn't want to give it. He gave a name instead, a crew name Isola recognized from the east gate, and swore to run for them whenever they called. The guard wrote it down without looking up.

Isola knew that crew. She watched the boy walk onto the bridge like he'd paid with something he could earn back, and she hoped somebody at home loved him enough to be mad about it.

She'd bled on that plate herself before Mother Rae gave her the cord.

She stepped up and opened her fist. The guard looked at the token before passing the scanner over it. The scanner made the short flat sound she'd been waiting for, and he lifted his chin toward Shadowmere without asking where she was going or what she had in her skirt. She kept her free hand on her bag strap the whole time, nowhere near the pocket. She didn't know whether a scanner that read tokens could read anything else, and she wasn't about to find out by touching her own hip in front of it.

Isola didn't like Shadowmere.

She'd never had to explain that to anybody at home, because nobody there liked it either. In Ashvein, a crew wore its colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were on before you stood on it, and if you didn't, somebody's auntie would tell you from a window. Shadowmere didn't have aunties in windows. It had neon and violet mist and rain falling out of rock, and people who smiled at you while they figured out what you were worth. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

*Running errands or something*, Selah had said about Asha this morning. Isola had a counterfeit in her skirt that said otherwise, and if Asha's errands ran anywhere, they ran through here.

That was why Isola went to the assayer before she went to Pell. If she was going to walk around Shadowmere looking for her cousin, she needed to know exactly what her cousin had left in that bag.

The woman kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain didn't reach. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Mother Rae and watching the woman weigh it on a little brass scale with weights no bigger than teeth. She could weigh anything. People brought her scrapings, filings, and coins that had been through fire, and she put on her loupes and told them what it was worth. Isola had never once heard her ask where any of it came from.

She was weighing something now for a man in a dock coat who was arguing with the result. Isola waited until he lost and left, then stepped up, set her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over.

The coin lay in the middle of her palm. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody passing could see more than a corner.

The assayer looked at it. Then she put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it. "I don't weigh High Heresy."

The man at the next counter, selling charm wire, stopped talking to his customer, and the customer stopped listening to him.

"Get it off my counter," the assayer said.

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

She walked away without hurrying, because hurrying in the Back Market was the same as asking to be followed. She'd known what the coin was since Asha's room. Knowing it by herself had been one thing. Hearing a woman who never asked questions call it by its name out loud, in front of strangers, was another. The words followed her out from under the arch.

High Heresy meant the square. It meant the woman from the mining district on her knees while the Synod took her breath, her fingers scraping at the stone until they bled. Isola had been a girl when she watched that. For one second, coming out into the rain, the hands on that stone were Asha's, and Isola had to stop walking.

She let the rain hit her face until the picture went away. Then she went looking for Pell, because the salt was the reason she was down here, and it was the only part of today she still knew how to do right.

His stall stood in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods. The lane was so narrow that the counters took up half of it, and everybody walking had to share the other half. Pell had a striped awning that leaked at one corner, a scale he didn't let anybody else touch, and a mouth that never stopped running, which was usually the only annoying thing about him. Mother Rae bought her funeral grade from him because he kept it dry and didn't cut it. Selah had made that clear the first time she sent Isola down here alone. Usually, by the time Isola got to the counter, Pell had asked after Selah, complained about the drip in his awning, and tried to sell her something she hadn't come for.

"Rae's girl," Pell said when she reached the counter. He was already reaching. "What she need?"

He hadn't asked about Selah. Isola set the slip down. He read it, nodded, and scooped from the open barrel at his elbow into a square of brown paper.

She watched the scoop go in and come out. The salt was grey and coarse, with dark grit caught between the grains, and it clumped where the damp had gotten to it. Isola had been packing salt into Selah's windows since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them. She'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name: fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it was trying to get away. Pell kept it under the counter in a locked drawer, wrapped in grey paper with his stamp on the fold, and every time Isola came for Rae, he'd unlocked that drawer without being asked.

He was folding the brown paper.

"That ain't funeral grade," Isola said.

Pell didn't look up. "It's what I got today."

"That's quarry salt."

"It's salt."

"It's quarry salt, and it's wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop." She put her hand flat on the slip. "Rae's slip say funeral grade."

"Rae can come get it herself."

He'd never said anything like that in front of her. Pell talked too much, sure, but he talked the way people did when they wanted to keep you happy, not the way they did when they wanted you gone. Isola looked at his hands. They were folding the paper too carefully, the corners squared up over and over, the way people kept their hands busy so their faces didn't have to do anything. She pulled the cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he had to look at it.

"She sent me," Isola said. "Same as last time. Same as the time before that. Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today."

Pell stopped folding.

He looked past her. Isola knew better than to turn around. She'd grown up in a house where you learned what was behind you by watching the face in front of you, and Pell's face had just gone blank.

Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end, and the other stood right beside her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word to Pell or to her. In Ashvein, she'd have known whose men they were by their sleeves before they ever reached the counter. These two wore plain dark coats and the kind of patience a person only had if they'd never once been told no at a counter.

Pell reached under the counter, farther back than the drawer. He pulled out an envelope and set it down. The man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking at it. The man at the end of the counter looked at Isola's hand, the one still holding Rae's token out in the open, and he kept looking long enough that she felt it up her arm. She closed her fist over the token. He followed the first man out of the stall, and the two of them went up the lane toward the bend without hurrying.

Pell didn't unlock the drawer until they'd been gone several minutes, and when he finally did, his hands were shaking so badly that the key took two tries.

Isola dropped the cord back under her shirt while she waited. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Pell owed it something. She'd just watched him pay.

He took out a grey paper bundle, set it on the scale, and watched the needle shake. He opened the bundle to show her the salt inside, and when he went to close it again, a thin stream of it ran onto the counter and off the edge into the wet at her feet. Pell watched it go and didn't say a word about it.

Isola had never seen him not say anything about anything.

"Who was that?" she asked.

"Who?"

She waited.

"Rae account," Pell said. His letters wandered across the line as he logged the purchase in his notebook. "Tell her I said hey."

"You want me to tell her who was here when I came for her salt?"

He tore the receipt out and held it toward her. When she didn't take it, he laid it on top of the bundle and pushed both across the counter. His eyes went up the lane, to where the men had gone.

"I want you to take your salt," he said.

Isola tucked the bundle under her arm and stood there a second longer than she needed to, looking at the grey grit still sitting in the brown paper he'd tried to hand her. She could see it now, all the way through. Pell hadn't been cheating Rae. He'd been trying to get Rae's girl gone before those two walked up and found her standing at his counter with Rae's token on her neck. *Rae can come get it herself* had been the only warning he could afford to give her.

She couldn't prove a bit of it. And Mother Rae would ask about the delay if she stood in this stall much longer.

"And you short," she said, because it was true and because she wasn't about to let him think she hadn't noticed. "It's on the ground, not in my paper."

"I'll make it up next time."

"You'll make it up now."

He reached for the scoop. Coats brushed behind her as somebody came up the lane, and Isola didn't look, because something farther up had caught her eye where the lane bent toward the crossing and Gutterlight's noise came through the stone.

Asha was standing at the bend.

Isola knew her by the way she stood before she ever saw her face, weight back, head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her. Isola had never seen Asha stand like that anywhere she was supposed to be. The coat was dark, fitted close at the waist, and fell to her knees. A structured tote hung from her shoulder, stiff-sided and new, the kind of bag that held its shape whether there was anything in it or not. Isola had never seen either one in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Bags like that too. Isola thought about the packed bags lined up against Asha's wall and the coin sitting against her own hip, and she didn't like how easily it all fit together.

She was already stepping away from the counter toward the bend, still talking over her shoulder. "And don't put none of that grey mess on her slip again or I'm telling her myself—"

Up ahead, something crashed. Glass went across stone, a shopkeeper yelled, and a boy came out of the Hush shop running straight down the lane at them. Somewhere in front of her a man's voice said one flat word, and a hooded shape moved toward the freight wall.

Then the man stepped back into Isola.

One bumps. One runs. You find out what's gone when you get home. Every story Isola had ever heard about the Back Market went like that, and here was a man's whole back in her chest and a boy running at her from the front, and the coin was in her skirt.

Isola couldn't afford to lose anything, so she did the one thing her father would have told her never to do.

She swung.

He turned before she could land it. His shoulder came around, and then her fist stopped like it had hit a wall. His hand had closed around her wrist. Her ring was an inch from the middle of his chest, close enough to touch his shirt.

She was cussing him before he finished turning and saw no reason to stop just because he'd caught it. He was big as a damn door and couldn't look where he was going, and he had some nerve, and somebody should've taught him better than to back up on folks in a lane this tight. She tipped her head back to tell him the rest, and then kept tipping it.

He was tall. Not regular tall. Tall enough that she had to put her chin up to keep cussing at his face, which she did. Somebody had hit that face recently. One cheek was swollen high under the eye, and his bottom lip was split, the middle healing dark. Rain ran out of his locs and slid down into his beard, and he didn't bother to wipe it. There was a bandage across the knuckles of the hand holding her, white gone grey at the edges and wrapped tight over two of them. Somebody who knew what they were doing had wrapped that. Not him. Nobody got a wrap that neat with one hand.

He was also fine, which was rude of him.

He was looking at her too. At first it was the look she expected, the quick one men in lanes like this gave you to decide whether you were a problem: her rings, her feet, the way she was standing. Then his eyes got to her mouth and stayed there, and that had nothing to do with whether she was a problem.

He let go of her wrist first. He stepped back and lifted both hands open in front of his chest, like she was the dangerous one. Isola liked that more than she should have.

"You always swing first?"

"You always walk backwards?"

"Only when I'm in a hurry."

"Then slow down."

His eyes went to her fist, which she hadn't put all the way down. "You was really gon' put all three of them in my chest."

"Still might."

He smiled. It pulled at the split in his lip, she watched it hurt him, and he kept doing it anyway. She looked at his mouth before she could help it. When she dragged her eyes back up, he was looking at her like he'd caught her at something, which was a lie, because she hadn't done anything.

"What's your name?"

"You ain't earned it."

"What's it cost?"

Of course he'd ask that. Everything down here had a price, and he'd asked like a man used to paying it. "More than you got on you."

"You don't know what I got on me."

So she looked. She took her time about it, too, from his face down and back up again, and she let him watch her do it. "I know somebody else wrapped your hand. And somebody else already hit you in the face." She couldn't keep the corner of her mouth from going up. "Don't look like your day going too good."

"Day ain't over," he said.

He said it low. Isola heard exactly how he said it.

Wait.

He was flirting with her. In the middle of the Back Market, with his face busted and a boy still running somewhere behind him, this man was flirting with her. And she'd been doing it right back since about *Still might*, and nobody had asked her permission.

She didn't smile. It was close. For maybe six seconds neither of them said anything, and water dripped off Pell's awning between them, and somebody behind her wanted to get by and didn't ask. He was standing close enough that she could see the rain sitting in his beard. She didn't step back. Neither did he. She had a cousin at the end of this lane, and she knew that, and she stood there anyway.

Then his eyes went over his own shoulder, and Isola followed them to a hooded girl pressed flat against the freight wall.

The hood had slid back when the girl pressed into the stone, and a length of hair had come loose over the collar of her coat. It was red. Isola had never seen that color on anybody's head in her life. The coat was a man's coat. The boots were somebody else's too, wide at the toe, and under all of it was black silk with stones on the collar, catching what little light the lane had. Mourning clothes, and good ones. Isola had grown up around funeral thread, and she knew what it cost to dress somebody's grief like that. Somebody had dressed this girl in whatever was nearest and hadn't had time to make any of it match.

"Hood," the man said, and the girl pulled it up with both hands.

He moved her off the wall and up the lane without touching her. He didn't have to. She went where he went, and Isola didn't know what that made them to each other, but she knew it wasn't nothing. And she'd just been standing in the rain flirting with him while that girl was waiting on him at the wall. Isola could have kicked herself.

She looked past them for Asha.

The bend was empty. Two runners crossed it with a box between them, and behind them there was nothing but the lane curving away toward the noise. Ten steps, maybe fifteen. That was how close she'd been. She'd had her, and she'd spent the time it would've taken to reach her telling a stranger what his day looked like.

When she looked back, the man had stopped just past the Hush shop and turned around. She was already looking. She didn't look away. If he wanted to catch her looking, he could catch her.

"You made me lose my cousin," she said across the lane.

"Then I owe you," he said, and he took the girl around the corner, and then he was gone too.

He did owe her. Isola meant to hold him to it.

She stood in the lane long enough for a woman with a cart full of thread to tell her to move.

She went to the bend. Nothing there told her where Asha had been standing or which way she'd gone. Isola checked the crossing and the doorways on either side and the mouth of the next alley. Isola kept at it until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her teeth. By then the crowd at the bend had doubled, and Asha could have been anywhere in it or nowhere near it.

She walked back toward Pell's stall slowly. She told herself it was so she wouldn't miss anything Asha might have been dumb enough to drop.

The real reason was him. Isola didn't like that either.

*Day ain't over.* He'd said it like he had plans for the rest of it. She kept hearing it in his voice, and she kept seeing that smile he couldn't afford with his lip like that, and it irritated her how easy it was to see. The worst part was she couldn't tell whether he knew he was fine. A man who knew would have been easier to put down and walk away from. This one had looked at her like he'd been caught off guard too, and she didn't know what to do with that.

She knew what to do with Asha, though. She knew Asha had been ten steps away in a coat Isola had never seen, carrying a bag Isola had never seen, standing at a bend in Shadowmere like she'd been told to wait there. She knew Asha hadn't come home by noon. And she knew a woman who never asked questions had just called the thing in Isola's skirt by its name.

That was what she was supposed to be thinking about. She made herself think about it.

Pell had a twist of grey paper waiting on the counter when she got back. He pushed it across with two fingers and went back to his notebook, though he wasn't writing anything.

Isola opened the twist. Funeral grade, fine and white, enough to cover what he'd spilled and maybe a pinch more. The pinch more was Pell's apology. It was the only kind he'd ever given her.

"Pell."

"I made it up."

"Who was the man in the coat? The tall one with his hand wrapped."

Pell looked up for the first time since she'd come back, and the color had come back into his face with him. Whatever those two men had taken out of him, her question had put some of it back. "You ain't know who that was?"

"Would I be asking?"

"If you don't know, then I ain't telling you." He laughed and shook his head like she'd told him a joke he didn't have time for. "Take your salt home, Rae girl."

She folded the twist into the bundle and left.

The line at the bridge was shorter than she'd expected. The salt wasn't heavy, but Isola paid for the lift anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might have decided to follow a girl with Rae's token and a coin nobody would weigh.

The lift groaned up the shaft with eleven people pressed into a cage built for eight. Somebody's elbow sat in her ribs the whole way up. Isola kept the bundle flat against her chest and her free hand over her skirt, right where the seam bunched around the coin. The cage rattled at every level marker, and the operator called them out without looking up from his lever. Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.

She spent the whole ride irritated by six seconds of him.

Six seconds. She'd taken longer than that to decide whether to trust a coin. She went back over *You ain't earned it* and still liked it. She went back over *What's it cost?* and liked that too, more than she wanted to. Then she got to *Then I owe you* and liked it a lot less, because she didn't know what he thought he owed her or how he planned to pay it. Owing came with a number and a day. He hadn't given her either one. If she ever laid eyes on him again, she planned on collecting.

Somewhere past Service Shaft, she caught her thumb working the middle ring around her finger, the one that had stopped an inch from his chest. She made herself quit. By Ashvein West, she was doing it again.

By the time the gate opened, the rain on her coat had started drying into dust. Coal smoke hung low over the lane, and she'd missed at least one bell while she was gone. The boys from across the street were gone from under their lantern. Isola trusted the empty spot less than she'd trusted the boys standing in it. Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge, and Isola looked at that little pile longer than a pile deserved.

The lantern above the door still burned amber.

She stood under it a moment longer than she meant to. Then she knocked the dust off her boots against the frame and let herself in. Micah was asleep on the couch with one arm hanging off the cushion and his shoes still on. He'd given up on the day, but not on the shoes. Tevin's coat hadn't moved from the chair, and his door was still shut. She listened at it on her way past and heard him breathing on the other side, slow, the way he breathed when he was pretending not to be awake. Good. He could pretend all evening if he wanted.

Asha's door was open. Isola didn't go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she'd left it. If Asha had come home, she'd come and gone without touching it.

In the kitchen, Selah was at the stove. She turned when Isola came in and held out both hands for the bundle before Isola reached the counter. "Pell?"

Isola laid the receipt on the counter. "He tried me with quarry salt first."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Her mother untied the grey paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. It ran the way it was supposed to. She nodded once and folded the paper closed again.

"You find your cousin?"

This morning, Selah had told her not to go looking. Now she hadn't asked whether Isola had looked. She'd asked whether she'd found her. Isola reached for a rag that didn't need reaching for. "Saw her. Lost her in the Market."

"Lost her how?"

A coat on Asha's back Isola couldn't have bought with three months of courier fees. A bag to match it. A coin the assayer wouldn't touch. Two men who never said a word, and Pell, quiet. And a tall man with his hand wrapped who'd backed into her at exactly the wrong second and smiled at her like it had been the right one.

"Crowd," Isola said.

It was the second time today she'd handed one of her parents a word that was only mostly true.

Selah held her stare long enough that Isola wiped the same spot on the counter twice. Then her mother turned back to the stove.

"Wash up," Selah said. "Food almost ready."

Isola went to the sink and turned on the water. She held her hands under it and watched the Shadowmere rain go down the drain with the red dust from the stairs, and she prayed that whatever Asha had gotten herself into, it wouldn't end with her back in Shadowmere.

## Chapter 5: Silas

The roar came through the stone before they reached the next crossing.

Melena stopped so suddenly that Silas had to stop with her. Her hand went inside Dante's coat, over the place where she'd put the envelope. "What is that?"

"Gutterlight." He waited for the crowd to settle enough that she could hear him. A match had just let out. Somebody had won money, and somebody else was probably being carried through the service gate on a stretcher with two bowls of salt behind him. Twelve hours ago, that had been Jerrick. Silas's shoulder still remembered it, even if the rest of him had already moved on.

"You're taking me near an arena?"

He checked the crossing beyond her shoulder. A pair of runners was hauling a box between them and arguing about which door they'd been paid to use. "Nobody hears much else when a match is on."

Noise was the best cover Shadowmere had, and Gutterlight made more of it than anywhere else on the tier. People came out of a fight night loud and stupid and looking at each other, not at a girl in a borrowed coat. Melena looked at the wall the sound had come through like she expected it to open. He gave her time for one breath, then started for the closed tailor shop at the end of the row. She followed without needing the second instruction he'd been ready to give her.

The shop's shutter was down. Silas checked beneath it, then the narrow gap beside the false utility panel, where the stone had been patched in a slightly different grey. Nobody had pried at it. Nobody had left anything wedged in the seam. He took the brass token from his pocket and pressed it into the center of the panel. Ward lines showed under his palm, thin and pale, and the panel released without pulling anything from him. Dante's routes asked for the token. They didn't need to ask for more. He held the panel open for Melena and watched the alley until she had both feet on the stairs, then stepped through and closed it behind them.

Her borrowed boots sounded too loud in the narrow stairwell. She heard it too and placed the next step more carefully. Silas kept to the side so she could use the rail and took the climb slower than he normally would. His shoulder didn't object to that part.

Camille's seal waited at the top. He'd have known her work anywhere by the cuts around the frame, deep and even, every line finished all the way to the end the way she finished everything, from a threshold to an argument. The brass token wasn't going to be enough for this door. Dante's marks let you through. Camille's wanted to know who you were.

Silas drew his knife, nicked the side of his thumb, and pressed the fresh blood into the seal. He held it against the stone until the line closed, then took his hand away and put the small cut to his mouth. Behind him, Melena had stopped on the last stair.

"Was that necessary?" she asked.

Silas wiped the blade and put it away. "For this door? Absolutely."

He pushed it open before she could ask what made it different from the one downstairs.

Warm amber light reached the landing. Silas let Melena pass him, then checked the inner edge of the threshold before he stepped across. The room was bigger than the stairwell promised: a dining table with a bowl of fruit and a jar of fresh flowers, two chairs with cushions somebody had beaten the dust out of, a kitchen along the back with its cabinets shut tight. Melena stopped near the table. She looked past the flowers to the back wall, then turned to the wall beside her, and then the next one.

She was looking for a window. There wasn't one in the whole place. He didn't say anything. She'd get there on her own, and it would go down easier if he wasn't standing there watching it land.

He left her in sight of the open door while he checked the bathroom and the bedroom. Fresh clothes filled the wardrobe, folded and stacked by size. The bed was made. The flowers on the table hadn't started to drop. Somebody had readied this place in the last day, which meant somebody had been told about Melena before Silas had. That was starting to feel like the theme of his afternoon.

He checked the hidden emergency route out of habit. Only Mercer blood opened it, which made it the one door in the place nobody else could come through, and the one door Melena couldn't use either.

Back in the main room, he lifted the corner of the rug nearest the kitchen. The ward line under it ran unbroken from wall to wall, Camille's hand again, patient and exact. He laid the rug flat, checked the seals on the food, then opened the cabinet for water. Behind the bottles, a locked grate held a short row of small dark ones labeled for emergency Dimming. Hush, if you bought it in the Back Market from a man who'd snap the case shut when a thin boy drifted too close. Dimming, if somebody official had written it on a label. Silas checked that the lock on the grate held and looked at the bottles a second longer than he needed to. Then he shut the cabinet and set the water within Melena's reach.

She'd taken off Dante's coat and laid it over a chair. Without it, the burned cuff showed against the table, a dark edge eaten into silk that had probably cost more than this room. She had the envelope out again and was smoothing it flat, working carefully around the place her hand had crushed it in the office.

"How long am I staying here?" she asked.

"Until Mother Rae decides where you go."

"And if I decide first?"

Silas set the medical tin where she could reach it. "You need to stay inside the ward line."

Melena lifted her face. "I did not come here to be imprisoned."

"Right now they use the same door."

He didn't like saying it. He'd said it anyway, because it was true, and because she'd hear it from the lock in an hour whether he said it or not. Moving people was one thing. A route ended somewhere, and everybody on it knew they were going toward the end. This door didn't come with an end anybody had bothered to tell him about.

She looked past him to the threshold. "You don't know what I came here for."

His attention dropped to the envelope. She slid it under her palm before he could read so much as the outside. He looked back at her face. "No. I don't."

Heat gathered near the table. A flower at the edge of the jar curled, its lowest petal browning against the glass. Melena pulled her hand off the paper. Black was creeping back beneath her nails.

He didn't reach for the knife this time. He stopped on the other side of the table instead and kept his hands where she could see them. "Your heart still running like it was in the office?"

She pressed her lips together, then gave him one small nod.

"Let me check?" He held out his hand and waited.

She looked at his hand for a long moment, like she was deciding whether the question had been real or only polite. Then she set the envelope down and offered her wrist. She watched his fingers settle over the pulse. It was fast, though steadier than after the bell, and her skin was still hot enough that he could feel it through the calluses. Another petal browned while he counted. He let go before she had to ask.

"You feel it coming?"

"Sometimes." She drew her wrist back against herself.

"What stops it?"

Melena stared at the blackened edge of the flower. "Nothing for long."

She didn't say it like she wanted him to feel sorry for her. She said it like somebody reading off a ledger she'd been keeping on herself for a while.

Silas looked toward the door. Camille's seal hadn't changed. He could check every line his mother had put in this house and still not know whether any of it had been built for what Melena had carried in with her. Camille built for the dead and for the people they left behind. As far as Silas could tell, this was neither.

"My mama's coming later," he said. "She gon' want a look at you."

"Who is your mama?"

"Camille." He checked the kitchen latch on his way back toward the door. "You got a problem with me, tell her. You want the placement changed, she can take it to Mother Rae."

It was the most useful thing he had to offer her. Camille would listen to a complaint about him with real interest.

Melena watched him for a moment. "And until then?"

"I'll be outside."

He stepped into the corridor and kept his good hand on the door until it closed. Two marks waited beside the frame. He pressed his palm across the entry seal first, then the containment mark, and silver spread over the lock and settled into the metal.

The handle turned from the inside.

Silas would have tried it too. He listened to it stop, then try once more, harder, and then go still.

He dragged the chair from the opposite wall and set it where he could see the lock without turning his head. Gutterlight roared again on the other side of the eastern wall, closer here, the sound coming through the stone into the legs of the chair. He lowered himself into it carefully, kept his bad arm close against his ribs, and watched the handle.

His phone buzzed against his hip.

Remy: You home?

Silas: No.

Remy: Dante?

Silas looked at the door for a while before he answered.

Silas: Working.

Three dots came and went. Remy had known him long enough to hear everything that one word was leaving out, and long enough not to push on it through a phone.

Remy: Ledger names in the morning. Eat something.

Silas: Yes, Daddy.

Remy sent back the same two words he'd sent last night. Silas put the phone away.

The corridor settled around him. Rain ticked somewhere in the walls. Behind the door, he could hear Melena moving now and then, a chair scraping, footsteps going to the back wall and stopping and coming back. Looking for a window again. Or just walking, because walking was the only thing left in there that was hers to decide.

He thought about the fifteen-year-old. Somewhere behind a Dawnveil stamp and a line Jerrick had sold, a boy was either alive or he wasn't, and Silas was sitting in a chair in a corridor, guarding a girl who hadn't done anything to anybody except come down seven tiers with a letter she wouldn't let him see. He didn't resent her for it. He'd been raised better than that, and she'd had a worse day than him. But he knew what he'd rather be doing, and it wasn't this.

Once, somewhere in all that, his mind went back to the lane without asking him. *Still might.* He let it stay there for about as long as it had taken her to say it, and then he put it back where it belonged.

When the corridor had been quiet long enough, he got up and laid his palm across the containment mark again, just to check.

The silver was warm.

Not hot. Warm, like a hand had been resting against the other side of it for a while. Silas kept his palm there until he was sure he wasn't imagining it, then sat back down, a little closer to the door this time, and didn't take his eyes off the lock.

No windows. He'd been glad about that when he walked the rooms. It meant the only way out of that apartment was this door, and the only thing between this door and Shadowmere was him.

It took longer than he expected. Long enough for Gutterlight to call two more matches through the wall. Long enough for his shoulder to set like cooling iron, and for him to decide, more than once, that the warmth had been the corridor's and not hers.

The smell reached him first. Hot metal, and under it something like singed cloth. Silas was on his feet before he'd decided to stand, and his shoulder charged him for every inch.

The silver over the lock had gone from pale to bright. While he watched, it ran. The containment mark didn't break so much as give up, its lines sliding out of shape like wax down the side of a candle, and the lock behind it glowed dull red at the center, then a hard, clean orange.

"Melena." He kept his voice level, the way he'd kept the knife low in his father's office. "Step back from the door."

The answer was heat. It came through the wood like a hand in his chest and moved him back a step whether he meant to go or not. The lock gave with a sound like a bone going. The door swung in, then out, and Melena came through it with her hood down, her hands black to the second knuckle, and her eyes already on the stairs.

He reached for her with his left arm because it was the one between them. It didn't come up. His shoulder locked halfway, every minute of last night in the Ring arriving at once, and by the time he'd turned to bring his right hand around, she was past him and on the stairs, Rhea's boots loud on every step.

He went after her. He didn't look at what she'd done to Camille's frame on the way by. He'd hear about that later, at length.

At the bottom, the panel stood open, its edge scorched black where she'd put her hands to it. Silas came through it into the rain and found the alley empty in both directions. Then he found her by the way people at the far end were turning around to look at something. She'd gone toward the light. People who didn't know Shadowmere always did. The lit end of the row ran straight back toward the market crowd, and she was halfway down it in a mourning dress with her hair loose, and every single person she passed was going to remember her.

Silas didn't follow her down the row. He cut behind the tailor shops, through a gap between two walls that only people raised on these streets knew was a gap, and came out ahead of her where the row narrowed past a freight door. She saw him and turned hard into the nearest alley.

It was the wrong alley. He'd known it would be. It ran forty feet and stopped at a wall of wet stone with a drain grate at the bottom, and by the time she reached the end of it and turned around, he was standing in its mouth with his good hand open at his side.

"Don't," she said. Heat came off her in waves he could see, bending the rain before it reached her.

"I'm not coming any closer."

"I am not going back in there."

"Then where you going?"

She opened her mouth. Nothing came out of it. She didn't know. She'd burned through a Mercer lock and run out into a tier she couldn't name three streets of, and she had nowhere in Shadowmere to run to. Silas watched her understand that, and he watched it make her angrier, and the heat climbed.

Then the sound went out.

The rain didn't stop. He could see it falling, streaking past the lamp at the alley mouth and breaking on the stones at his feet, but the hiss of it was gone, all at once, like somebody had shut a door on it. Gutterlight's roar went with it. The drip off the gutters went. When Silas breathed, he couldn't hear that either.

Silence. He knew it the way he knew the cold of his mother's seals.

Out on the row, people had stopped moving. A woman with a basket stood frozen with her mouth open on a word that wasn't coming. Past her, three figures came down the middle of the street with no sound to them at all, veiled from crown to collar, pale bone marks showing through the cloth where their faces should have been. Nobody looked at them. Everybody on that row had learned a long time ago what happened to people who got to say afterward that they'd seen something.

Melena's hair was loose and red. Her hands were giving off light at the knuckles like coals somebody had blown on. If one of those veils turned this way, there wasn't a shadow in Shadowmere deep enough to hide her in.

There was no time to ask. There wasn't even sound to ask with.

Silas crossed the alley in four steps, put her back to the wall, and opened his coat around her. He pressed her into the stone with his body between her and the street, pulled the coat closed over her hair, and put his right hand flat over both of her burning hands to cover the light.

It was like closing his palm on a stove.

He didn't move it. She went rigid against him, every inch of her furious, and he could feel her trying to shove him off and finding nothing to push against but the wall. Her heat came through his shirt and up into the bones of his hand, and he kept his hand where it was and his head turned so his face was the only thing the street could see, just another man in a dark coat leaning on a girl in an alley, which was nothing anybody on that row would look at twice.

The three veils passed the alley mouth. One of them slowed. Silas didn't breathe. He couldn't have heard himself if he had. The veiled head turned a quarter of the way toward the dark where they stood, held there for a count he felt in his teeth, and then turned back to the street and kept going.

Sound came back slowly, the way feeling came back to a foot that had been sat on. First the rain. Then the gutters. Then, from very far off, Gutterlight. Then his own breath, ragged, louder than he'd wanted it to be.

He let go of her hands and stepped back.

His palm was blistered across the middle, raw and shining. He didn't look at it long. Melena came off the wall like she'd been thrown from it and put three feet of alley between them. Her hands had stopped glowing. She was shaking, and he didn't think it was all the cold.

He owed her the truth more than he owed her an apology, so he gave her that.

"I touched you without asking," Silas said. "I'd do it again."

She looked at his hand. She looked at his face. Whatever she found there didn't soften anything about her own.

"Then don't ask me to thank you."

He didn't. He jerked his chin toward the mouth of the alley, and after a long moment, she went, because she'd seen the same three veils he had and she wasn't stupid. He walked her back down the row with his burned hand held away from his coat and his eyes on every doorway, through the scorched panel and up the stairs, past what was left of the lock.

The door wouldn't latch anymore. He got her inside and pulled it shut as far as it would go, and when that wasn't far enough, he set the chair in the doorway and sat down in it with his back against the frame, half in the corridor and half in the room, his bad arm against his ribs and his good hand open on his knee, throbbing in time with his pulse.

Behind him, Melena sat down at the table with the envelope and didn't say a word.

Last night, at least, he'd known what he was fighting.
