# CHAPTER 14: SILAS

The practice ring under Gutterlight was the only place in Shadowmere that smelled the same at noon as it did at midnight.

Sweat and old canvas and the liniment the cutmen rubbed into everybody's shoulders whether they wanted it or not. Silas had been coming down here since he was fifteen, back when Dante still thought he was going to grow out of it, and the smell of the place had gotten into him somewhere below thinking. He walked in the door and his body loosened before he told it to. Even today, with his left shoulder held together by stubbornness and Camille's liniment and his right hand still wrapped to the wrist, he felt it go.

He couldn't spar. He knew that. Camille had told him so in four different ways that morning when she came up to 3C to change the wrap on his hand, and Dante had told him so by pulling every route mark he owned, and his own body had told him so every time he tried to lift his arm above his shoulder. So he sat on the bottom rope with his back against the corner post and his bad arm in his lap, and he watched Remy hit the heavy bag.

Remy had never been a fighter. He hit the bag the way some people sang in the washroom, loud and badly and for nobody's benefit but his own. But he'd been coming down here with Silas for so long that his body knew how to do it anyway, and he worked the bag in long, lazy combinations, jab-jab-cross, jab-cross-hook, sweat running down his face and his locs tied back with a strip of cloth that had probably been somebody's sleeve once. He'd been up all night on Camille's couch with a girl asleep on his shoulder. He hadn't slept a minute of it, and he'd walked out of the building that morning looking like a man who'd been given something.

Silas had watched him go down the stairs at first bell to buy bread from the stall on the corner and come back up them with two kinds, because he couldn't remember which one she'd liked.

"You're dropping your left," Silas said.

"I'm not dropping nothing."

"You're dropping it every time you throw the hook."

Remy threw the hook. His left came down. He threw it again, and it came down again, and he stopped and stood there breathing hard with both gloves resting on the bag.

"You gon' just sit there and coach me all day?"

"I can't do nothing else."

"You could go home."

"Ma's at my house."

"Your ma's at your house *every* day."

"Today she's mad about it."

Remy laughed into the bag. Then he hit it a few more times, not as hard, and stopped again, and leaned his forehead against the canvas and stayed like that for a while.

Silas waited. He'd known Remy his whole life. When Remy got quiet, you let him.

"I like her," Remy said into the bag.

Silas didn't say anything.

"I know." Remy pushed off the bag and turned around, pulling at the laces of his gloves with his teeth. "I *know*, Si. Don't look at me like that."

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me like your mama looks at a lock somebody else installed." He got one glove off and threw it on the canvas. "She's leaving. I know she's leaving. She's—" He stopped. He looked around the empty ring, at the cutmen's table in the corner with nobody at it, at the high windows full of grey light. He lowered his voice anyway. "She's who she is. She's going back up to wherever that is the second Rae finds her a door. I know all that."

"Okay."

"And I like her anyway."

"Okay."

Remy got the other glove off and sat down on the canvas with his back against the bag and his elbows on his knees. He looked at his hands. The back of the left one was still blistered from Blue Circle, the skin gone shiny and tight where the heat had gotten it.

"Last night," he said, "before she fell asleep. She was talking. The blackroot had her talking, and I asked her what she used to do when she couldn't sleep, back home. When she was little. And she said she used to lie there and pretend she was somebody else. A girl with a different name. A girl who wasn't anybody's daughter and wasn't anybody's anything, who could just go wherever she wanted and nobody would care." Remy rubbed his thumb along the blister. "Mira. That's the name she made up. Mira."

"Mira," Silas said.

"She said nobody's ever called her that. Not once. Not her mama, not anybody. It was just hers." Remy looked up at him. "I called her that this morning. When she woke up. I didn't even think about it. I said, *Mira, you want tea,* and she looked at me like I'd—"

He stopped.

"Like you'd what?" Silas asked.

"I don't know." Remy looked back down at his hands. "Like I'd found her."

Silas sat on the rope and didn't say anything for a while. He thought about Melena on his mother's couch with the letter in her lap, saying *I'm the Imperial Princess of Remnia* in the same flat voice she'd used to tell him not to touch her. He thought about everything that name carried, every room it would open and every door it would close and every person in Shadowmere who'd sell her for it. Then he thought about a girl lying awake somewhere he'd never be able to picture, giving herself a name nobody else got to use.

He understood, a little, why Remy had looked like a man given something.

"Be careful with that," Silas said.

"With what?"

"With her." He nodded at Remy's hands. "With the name."

Remy looked at him a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, and picked his gloves up off the canvas.

"Yeah," he said. "I know."

---

Lorraine called while Remy was in the washroom.

"Your father wants you."

"I'm suspended."

"I know you're suspended. I'm the one who struck the ledger." She didn't sound sorry about it. She didn't sound anything. "It's not about routes. It's about godchildren. He said to tell you that, specifically. And bring Remy if he's with you, because he's going to hear about it from his mama by supper anyway."

Silas looked at the phone after she hung up.

*Godchildren.*

---

Dante was standing in the map room when they came in, and the map was lit.

Silas had always liked the map room best when it was lit. In the daytime, with the lamps off, it was just a table under glass with threads and pins, a thing you had to lean over and squint at. But when Dante woke the board, the threads under the glass glowed, every route the Mercers tracked laid out in thin lines of light across the whole long table, Shadowmere and Ashvein and the tiers above them, pale gold for open, blue for watched, red for closed. It looked like the veins in the back of a hand. Silas had stood over it when he was nine, the first time Dante let him in, and thought it was the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen.

Dante didn't turn around when they came in. He was looking at something on the wall.

There was a narrow shelf there, beside the map-room door, that Silas had walked past a thousand times. Two strips hung on it, side by side, small and old and worn soft at the edges. Thread wound through each one. Silas had always known what they were, the way he'd always known what the route marks by the front door were. Dante's godchildren. He'd stood up for them both in Ashvein a long time ago, before Silas could remember, and he'd brought their names back down with him, and he'd hung them where he'd see them every time he went into this room.

Silas had never seen his father look at them. He was looking at them now.

"Micah's warmed up," Dante said, without turning around. "About an hour ago."

Silas came and stood beside him. He could see it. The left strip, the one with MICAH stitched along the top in thread gone grey with age, had a faint glow to it, like a coal under ash. Not bright. But there.

"What's that mean?"

"Means he's thinking about calling." Dante's voice was very quiet. "Means he's sitting somewhere in Sixth Ward with his name in his mouth and mine right behind it, deciding whether it's bad enough." He looked at the strip a moment longer. "He won't. Not today. He's a proud boy, and his sister would skin him. But he's thinking about it."

"Sixth Ward," Silas said.

Dante finally turned his head and looked at him.

"Synod audited the whole ward this morning. Every house. Started at the east gate before first bell and worked their way in, with Shrouds on the lanes and gold on the doors." He said it plainly, the way he'd read a route report. "The Wraithmoor house got read at the wall. Micah's Ironcross sponsorship was stamped void in front of his family. A courier recorded entering Blue Circle was recorded as belonging to that house, and has been ordered to the ward hall at third bell to explain herself."

Silas felt it go through him slowly, the way cold water went through a coat.

*Herself.*

He thought about the courier at Ora's table with red dust on her heels and her face going still when she saw him. He thought about a woman at a little table on the Ironcross line saying *I told Rae it was in my house*, and her hand closing over the coin, and *I watched you not break a man's arm yesterday, you ain't gone break mine.*

He looked at the second strip.

It was the same age as Micah's. Same shelf, same worn edges, same grey thread. ASHA stitched along the top.

It was cold. Not dark, not dead. Just cold, and still, like a coal somebody had stopped breathing on.

"That one," Silas said.

"That one hasn't warmed in a long time." Dante looked at it. "Months, maybe. I noticed in the spring. I thought she'd just stopped thinking about me." His jaw moved once. "Children do."

"That's the courier."

Dante didn't answer. He didn't have to.

"You knew," Silas said. "You knew yesterday. When Rae called. *Your godson's sister.* You knew whose house that coin came out of."

"I knew Rae's goddaughter brought a coin to Rae and wouldn't say where it came from." Dante turned back to the map. "I could guess. I didn't know."

"You could've told me."

"I told you to stay on your side of the line." Dante put both hands on the edge of the map table and leaned over the glass. "And I told you to keep your hands off her."

Behind Silas, in the doorway, Remy made a very small noise and turned it into a cough.

Silas didn't turn around.

"There's more," Dante said. "Danika Wraithmoor filed for a private watch on her brother's record about an hour after the stamp came down. That's Ironcross. That's a woman who works in Ironcross records putting her own name on her brother's file so nobody can touch it without her seeing. That's a woman who knows something's coming and wants to be standing in the door when it does." He tapped the glass over Ironcross with one finger. "And the Synod searched Blue Circle this morning too. Sealed it. Took Ora's front stairs apart, what was left of them."

"And Blue Six?"

Dante looked at him.

"Jude's been on it since last night," Silas said. "He'd have told me if anybody came."

"Nobody came." Dante straightened. "The Synod put Shrouds on every lane in Sixth Ward and gold on every door, and they searched a burned Hush den down to the tile, and they didn't send one clerk to Letter Row." His voice went very flat. "They searched the hands carrying it. Not the box filling them."

Silas stood there and let that sit.

He thought about Jude in his stained shirt, saying *These are built to pass. Not down here. Up.* He thought about the theft mark under the gold on Jerrick's token. He thought about the fifteen-year-old with no name in a holding ward on warehouse row, and about who'd have the reach to steal a blank from a ward's own cabinet and the quicksilver to make a compound you couldn't buy.

"Somebody up there knows what's in that box," he said.

"Somebody up there," Dante agreed, "would very much prefer nobody else did."

---

The board went dark about ten minutes later.

It didn't flicker. It just went, all at once, the way the bathhouse windows had lit up all at once on Bath Row. One second the whole long table was glowing under the glass, every thread a vein of light, and the next second the light died out of all of it at the same time, and the map room was just a room again, grey and close, with a table in it.

Dante had his hand flat on the glass before Silas could open his mouth.

Under his palm, very faintly, the threads came back. Not all of them. A few. Shadowmere, mostly, the lines nearest the Mercer building flickering up pale and thin like a lamp being relit. The rest stayed dark.

"Blue Six," Dante said quietly.

Silas leaned over the glass. The spot on Letter Row where Jude had pinned his watch last night was black. Not red. Not closed. Just nothing, like the thread had been cut.

"Box went cold." Dante didn't sound surprised. "Whatever was feeding it stopped. Or got moved."

"Jude."

"I'll call Jude." Dante's hand didn't move off the glass. His eyes had gone farther up the table, to the north edge, where the lines ran up out of Shadowmere toward the tier above it.

Ashvein was dark.

Not part of it. All of it. The whole tier, every lane and every route and every stair Silas had ever seen glow on that table, gone black. Like somebody had laid a hand over it.

"Synod sealed the ward," Dante said. "That's what that is. They've closed every route in and out of Sixth Ward until the audit's done." He paused. "Every route they know about."

Silas didn't hear the rest. He'd already taken his phone out.

He didn't decide to. His hand just went to his coat, the way it went to his knife. He stood there in the grey map room with his father's hand on the glass and his oldest friend in the doorway, and he opened the thread.

He'd read it more times than he'd ever admit to anybody. He'd read it on the tram that morning, and in the ring, and twice in the washroom while Remy was in the next stall talking about bread. He'd read it last night, after it went quiet, sitting at his table in 3C with the radiator knocking, scrolling up from the bottom to the top and back down again like there might be something in it he'd missed the first eleven times.

*You make it home?*

*I'm sitting in my mama's kitchen.*

*That's not Ashvein. That's a disease.*

*Who did wrap it?*

*That's the saddest thing anybody's ever told me about a building.*

*My cousin keeps her bags packed.*

He'd stopped on that one last night. He stopped on it again now, with the board dark and Ashvein dark and his father's voice going on somewhere beside him about seals and audits. *My cousin keeps her bags packed. Has for two years.* He'd read it last night and thought it was the realest thing anybody had said to him in a long time. He read it now and saw a courier at Ora's table with a structured tote, and a cold strip on a shelf, and a woman sitting in her mama's kitchen at midnight telling a stranger the one thing she hadn't told anybody.

*Goodnight, Wraithmoor.*

*Can I ask you*

That was the last one. Half a question with nothing at the end of it. It had come in after midnight, and it had sat there on his screen all night, and he'd looked at it and looked at it and hadn't answered, because she was asleep, and because he didn't know what she'd been about to ask, and because some part of him had liked not knowing. Had liked the idea that tomorrow she'd wake up and finish it.

She hadn't finished it.

"Who got you checking your phone like that?"

Silas looked up.

Remy was leaning in the doorway with his arms crossed and an expression on his face that Silas had seen maybe four times in their whole lives, and every one of those times, Silas had wanted to hit him.

"Nobody."

"Nobody," Remy said. "Okay. Nobody's got you scrolling up and down like you're reading scripture." He tipped his head. "*Somebody* who heard what the runners said behind a lye vat on Bath Row. *Somebody* you trust. That somebody?"

Silas didn't say anything.

Remy's face did something slow and delighted. "Oh, that's *her*. That's the salt girl. That's Micah's—" He stopped. His eyes went to the shelf by the door, to the two strips, one warm and one cold. His face changed. "Oh," he said, differently.

Dante said nothing at all. But he'd turned his head, and he was looking at Silas, and Silas knew that look too. He'd seen it on his father's face once before, years ago, the first night he'd come home from this same practice ring at fifteen with his knuckles split open and a grin on his face he couldn't get rid of.

He put the phone face down on the map table.

The board lit.

Not all of it. One thread. Up near the top of the dark, where Ashvein should've been, where the lines ran down out of the sealed tier toward Shadowmere, a single short line flared under the glass, bright and hard and very red.

Dante looked at it. His hand went still on the glass.

"Halflight," he said.

Silas knew it before his father said it. He'd sat at a table on that line yesterday morning. He'd watched a woman walk away from him toward that gate and stop three steps out and turn around and give him her name.

The red line pulsed under the glass, slow, like a heartbeat.

"That's the Ashvein gate," Dante said. "That's somebody at the Ashvein gate trying to get through a Synod seal." His voice had gone very careful. "Silas."

Silas had already picked the phone back up.

He didn't think about what to say. He'd been thinking about it for a day and a half without knowing he was doing it, every time he scrolled up and down that thread looking for something he'd missed. He opened the thread, and he typed it, and he sent it before Dante could finish whatever he was about to say.

*Tell me you good.*

It went. It landed on his screen right under hers, right under *Can I ask you*, so that the two of them sat there together, her half a question and his whole one, one on top of the other, with nothing after either of them.

The red line pulsed under the glass.

The thread stayed quiet.


# CHAPTER 15: ISOLA

Micah didn't cry. That was the part Isola would remember longest.

He sat on the couch after the reader left with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them, and he looked at the floor, and he didn't say anything at all. Not about Danika. Not about the thirty days. Not about the three work chits and the two letters from his foreman and the fourteen Veilmarks that had gone to Ironcross in a sealed case and come back as a word stamped in a ledger. He just sat there. Selah stood behind him with one cold hand on the back of his neck, and every so often she moved her thumb, and he let her.

Tevin was standing at the window with both hands flat against the whisperglass. He hadn't said a word either. He didn't need to. Isola could see the whole thing building in him from across the room, the jaw and the shoulders and the way he kept looking at the door, and she knew that the second the gold was out of the lane, he was going to go out looking for somebody to blame, and there was nobody to find.

Omari stood in the middle of the room exactly where he'd stood through the reading. He hadn't moved. His keys were still on his belt. His watch was still on his wrist, losing its eleven minutes. He looked at the wall where four generations of Wraithmoors hung in thread and ink and metal, and he looked at it for a long time, and then he went to it and laid his hand flat against Micah's strip.

Isola watched it warm under his palm. She watched her father close his eyes.

Then she went to find Asha.

---

Asha was in the kitchen, and she had her coat on.

She was standing at the stove where Isola had stood an hour ago, with her tote over her shoulder and her boots laced, and she was looking at the little iron door at the back of the ash chute. She didn't turn around when Isola came in. She'd known Isola would come.

"Third bell," Isola said.

"I heard him."

"You going?"

Asha didn't answer.

"The ward hall," Isola said. "Third bell. To explain your business at Blue Circle. You going, or you just gon' stand there looking at the stove like the coin's gon' climb back up out of it and tell you what to do?"

"I'm not going to the ward hall."

"Then where you going?"

Asha turned around.

Her face was very calm. It was the same calm she'd had standing at the front window with the reader saying her name, the same calm she'd had smiling at the vent. Isola had thought, watching her then, that it was the calm of somebody who'd given up. Standing in the kitchen now, close enough to see her cousin's eyes, she understood it was the other thing. It was the calm of somebody who'd already decided.

"I have to finish it," Asha said.

"Finish *what*?"

"The balance. The strip. Six deliveries, a hundred and eighty Veils, and the package burned, Isola. You heard the runners. They charge it to the den, and the den charges it to the courier." She laughed, not warm. "The courier's me. If I don't make it right, it doesn't just stay with me. It goes to whoever I'm registered to. You know who I'm registered to? You know whose name is on my strip, right under mine?"

Isola didn't.

"This house," Asha said. "Omari Kade Wraithmoor. Household of record."

The kitchen went very quiet.

"So I'm going to finish it," Asha said. "I'm going to go get whatever they want, and carry it wherever they want, and close the balance, and then it's done, and my name clears, and nobody in this house ever has to hear the word Blue Circle again."

"Your name clears," Isola said. "Your name's *already* clearing, Asha. I felt it. I felt it go this morning, in front of a Synod reader, and I held it on the wall with my own hands under the table while he looked at it, so he wouldn't see what you're doing to it."

Asha's face moved. Just for a second.

"You did that?"

"Somebody had to."

"Nobody asked you to."

"Nobody ever *asks* me to!" It came out of Isola too loud. She heard it bounce off the tile. She didn't care. "Nobody in this house ever asks me to do anything. You all just leave it lying where I'll trip over it. Micah's packet. Tevin's mouth. Your *bags*. Two years, Asha. Two years you've had those bags packed against that wall, and I've walked past them every day, and I've never once asked, because I thought if I didn't ask, you'd stay. And now I'm holding your name on a wall with my hands under a table so a man from Crownlight won't see it trying to leave."

"So let it leave."

Isola stared at her.

"Let it," Asha said. She came a step closer. Her voice had gone low and fast. "Let it go, Isola. It's my name. It's mine. Not yours. You don't get to hold it for me just because you're scared of what happens if you don't."

"I'm not scared."

"You're terrified. You've been terrified since you were twelve years old." Asha's eyes were wet now and she didn't seem to notice. "And I'm sorry. I am. But I can't be one more thing you hold."

She went for the vent.

Isola should have seen it. She'd seen Asha look at it that morning, standing at the front window with that smile on her face. She'd seen the iron grating in the wall by the kitchen floor every day of her life, the old vent that went down through the house and under the street into the trench routes that ran beneath all of Sixth Ward. Every child in this family had been told a hundred times never to go near it. Asha had been told the same as all of them.

Asha was already on her knees beside it. She had the grating out of the wall in one pull, like she'd loosened the bolts weeks ago. She had her tote off her shoulder and through the opening, and then her legs, and then she looked back up at Isola once, just once, from the dark.

"Don't follow me," she said, and was gone.

---

Isola followed her.

She didn't think about it. She was on the floor and into the vent before she'd finished hearing Asha say not to, her skirt catching on the edge of the iron, her elbows scraping the brick. The vent ran down at an angle, steep and narrow and black, slick with a century of coal smoke, and Isola went down it on her back with her heels braced and her hands out against the walls, sliding. Somewhere above her, through the brick, she heard Selah say her name.

She didn't stop.

The vent dropped her into the trench.

She landed on her feet, mostly, in a long stone channel just tall enough to stand up in, with water running ankle-deep along the bottom and old miners' lamps fixed to the walls at intervals, most of them dead, a few of them burning low and blue. She knew the trenches. Every child in Sixth Ward did, whether they'd been told to stay out of them or not. They ran under all the old blocks, cut a hundred years ago to carry water and coal and miners between the shafts, and when the shafts closed, the trenches stayed, and the people who needed to get places without being seen learned how to use them.

Asha was maybe forty feet ahead, walking fast through the water with her tote held up against her chest.

"*Asha.*"

Her cousin didn't turn around.

Isola went after her. The water was cold enough to make her shins ache. She caught up at the place where the trench widened into an old miners' station, a low room cut into the rock with stone benches along the walls and a row of iron lockers bolted into the far side, rusted, numbered in white paint gone grey. Some of them hung open. Most were shut.

Asha stopped in front of one of the shut ones and took a key out of her coat.

114.

She opened it. Inside was a single envelope, black, the same stiff paper as the route strip. Asha took it out and broke the seal and read it, standing in the blue light of a dying lamp with the water running around her boots, and Isola watched her cousin's face go grey.

"What is it."

Asha didn't answer. Isola took it out of her hands.

It was short. Printed, not written, in the same white ink as the strip. *Replacement order.* Then a line of route marks Isola couldn't read. Then, plain as anything: *12 coins. Blue Six. Before final bell.* And beneath that, smaller:

*Package lost under courier's carriage. Replacement owed by courier. On failure, liability transfers to courier's household of record.*

Isola read it twice. Then she looked up.

"Twelve coins," she said. "Where are you supposed to get twelve coins?"

Asha took the paper back and folded it, very carefully, and put it in her coat. "Same place I get all of them."

"Where?"

"You don't want to know."

"I want to know more than I've ever wanted anything in my *life*—"

"No, you don't." Asha turned around. Her face was wet, and her voice was steady, and Isola had never seen her look so much like Selah. "You don't want to know. You want to fix it. That's different. That's always been different with you. You don't want to know where the coins come from. You want to know where they come from so you can go stand in front of it and take the hit for me. And then come home and wash your hands at the sink and not tell anybody, and carry it around for the rest of your life, and every time you look at me, I'll see it."

"That's not—"

"You love us needing you," Asha said. "That's what it is. You love it. You'd rather hold every one of us up till your arms break than let any of us fall and find out we can get up on our own." She shook her head. "I'm not falling, Isola. I'm *walking*. There's a difference."

Isola opened her mouth.

The sound went out.

---

She didn't understand it at first. The water was still running. She could see it running, sliding cold and black over the toes of her boots. She could see Asha's mouth, still open on whatever she'd been about to say next. But the sound of the water was gone, and the drip from the trench ceiling was gone, and the low hum of the dying lamps was gone, all at once, like somebody had put a hand over the world.

She'd heard about it her whole life. She'd seen it that morning through the front window, a dog barking with no bark. She'd never been inside it.

Asha's face had gone white.

The Shroud came down the trench from the direction they'd come.

It didn't walk the way a person walked. It didn't splash. It moved through the ankle-deep water without disturbing it, a tall grey shape veiled from crown to collar, and the water closed behind it without a ripple. Where its face should've been, the cloth lay against something that wasn't a face. Pale shapes showed through it. Strips. Narrow strips layered one over the next, the way the name chains hung on the wall at home, bone-white, dozens of them, maybe hundreds.

It had something in its hand.

A strip. Just one. Held out in front of it between two long grey fingers, the way you'd hold out a ticket to a clerk. Isola could see writing on it, small and close.

ASHA NOELLE WRAITHMOOR.

The last letters were black.

Not written black. Going black, while she watched. The ink at the end of the strip, the R and the O and the M, was spreading, soaking into the paper the way water soaked into salt, darkening it, and as Isola watched, the black reached the H, and then the T, and started up into the I.

Beside her, Asha made a sound with no sound in it. Her hand went to her own throat.

Isola knew what she was looking at. She didn't know how she knew. Nobody had ever told her what a Shroud did with a name, or what it meant when one came walking through Silence holding yours. But she'd held that name on the wall that morning with her hands under a table, and she'd felt it go slack two nights ago under her fingertips in the dark, and she knew the shape of a Wraithmoor name the way she knew the shape of her own hand. The Shroud was taking it. Letter by letter. From the end.

When it reached the front, there wouldn't be anything left of Asha to hold.

Isola reached out and took the strip.

She didn't grab it. She didn't think about it long enough to grab. She just reached past her cousin into the Silence and closed her hand around the end of the strip, the end that wasn't black yet, the A and the S and the H, and pulled.

It came out of the Shroud's fingers.

She held it against her chest with both hands, and she said her cousin's name.

There was no sound. She said it anyway. She said it the way Omari had taught them, low and even, each part set down carefully before the next, the cadence her father used for the wall and nowhere else. She couldn't hear herself. She could feel it, in her mouth and her chest and her hands, the rhythm of it, the old shape. *Asha. Noelle. Wraithmoor.* The words weren't anything. Saying them right was everything.

The black stopped.

It stopped in the middle of the I, half in and half out, a hard line across the letter like a door shut on a hand. Isola felt it stop. She felt the name catch in her fists the way it had caught under the table that morning, slow and stubborn and heavy, a full bucket coming up out of a cistern. She held it.

The Shroud turned its head and looked at her.

It hadn't looked at her before. She understood that now. It had been looking at Asha, or at the name, or at whatever a Shroud looked at, and Isola had been nothing, a shape in the trench. Now it turned the not-face toward her slowly, all those pale strips shifting under the veil, and she felt it see her, and she felt it understand what she'd done.

She'd taken its work out of its hands.

It reached for her.

You *know* she can't say yes, you're standing there with your hand on her chest because she's not breathing, and you want to put your name on her? You want to put *her* name on *you*? Without her?"

"Si." Remy, from the doorway. His voice was very quiet. "Si, don't."

He didn't look at either of them.

"Take it back," Rhea said again.

"I know who," Silas said.

Rhea's fingers went still on his arm.

He didn't know what he meant by it. He'd said it before he thought it. But he heard it come out of his mouth, and he knew it was true. He knew who. He knew whose name he was asking the dead to hand him. He wasn't claiming a body on a table. He was claiming the woman who'd stood at Pell's counter with her hand in her skirt and told him to watch where he was backing up. Who'd sat on a cold stoop on Bath Row all day so she could follow him to a rail bridge and not come out. Who'd closed her fist over a coin and said *Ask*, and taken black three only after he moved his hand away, and turned around three steps from the Ashvein gate to give him her first name.

Her first name. Not the rest of it. She'd kept the rest. She'd given him exactly what she wanted him to have and kept everything else, and he'd taken that from her and been grateful.

He didn't know her full name. He'd never asked. He'd been waiting for her to give it to him.

The dead gave it to him instead.

He felt it come. Not in his ears. In his marks, the ones on the inside of his left wrist, the oldest ones, the names Camille had bound into him first. He felt the dead reach down into the dark under Shadowmere, down the roads they walked, and find it. Find her. Because Wraithmoor names answered to being held. He'd watched her prove it on the Halflight floor, holding her cousin's name against a Shroud with nothing but her own voice. A Wraithmoor name, held tight enough by anybody, would answer.

He was holding hers tighter than anybody had ever held anything.

*Isola.*

He felt it first. Before he saw it. A burning on the inside of his wrist, between two of the old names, in a space he hadn't known was there. Then he looked down.

Letters.

They were opening on his skin, one at a time, the way a cut opens. Wet and black. Not ink. Something darker than ink, something that shone. The first one opened slow, a long stroke and a dot, an *I*, sitting in the space between two names his mother had put there before he could walk.

*Amara.*

He hadn't known that one. The dead said it, and he felt it go into him, and he knew it now.

*Renae.*

Rae's. The name her godmother had given her in front of witnesses. The one that meant somebody had chosen her.

*Wraithmoor.*

The whole of her. Every name she had, every name her family had written on a wall so the Synod could never take her by striking out just one. Given to him by the dead, without her saying a word, while she lay on his table not breathing.

The first name kept opening. Letter by letter. *I.* *S.* Slow. Wet. Black.

He thought about her at Halflight, three steps from the gate. Turning around. Chin up. *Isola.* Just that. Just the first one, the one her mama picked, the one that was hers before anybody else got a say in it.

*O.*

That was the one he was taking. Not the whole chain. Just the one she'd given him. He was taking the one thing she'd handed him on purpose, and the dead were giving him the rest of her to go with it, and she'd never be able to take any of it back.

*L.*

He knew. He knew exactly what this was. He'd want, later, to tell himself he hadn't understood, that it had all happened too fast, that he'd been out of his mind with the dead in his ears and his father's shoulders at the door. It wouldn't be true. He understood all of it. He understood it better than he'd ever understood anything.

He leaned down. He put his mouth close to her ear. She couldn't hear him. She was past hearing anything. He said it anyway.

"I'm sorry."

He said it quietly. It wasn't for Rhea or for Remy or for the girl in the doorway with fire in her hands. It wasn't for the dead. It was for her. It was the only thing he had to give her that she hadn't already given him first, and he knew it wasn't enough, and he knew it wasn't anywhere close.

He didn't stop.

*A.*

The last letter closed.

---

Isola took a breath.

He felt it under his hand. All at once. Her chest coming up off the table under his palm, deep, so deep, the kind of breath a person takes coming up from the bottom of a cistern. Her whole body arched with it. Her eyes didn't open. Her hand tightened on the strip against her heart.

Then she let it out. And took another.

Down her leg, the black let go of her hip.

He saw it in Melena's light. The highest branch, the one that had been climbing, the one that had been an inch from the door, stopped. It hung there for a long moment on the inside of her thigh, dead and dark. Then it turned. Slowly. It bent away from her hip like a plant bending toward a window, and it turned, and it pointed down her leg, and across the linen, toward his hand.

Toward him.

Through the gap in the door, the shape with his father's shoulders stood very still. Then it stepped back. Silas didn't see where it went. He just saw the landing, empty, and the gap in the door, and nothing in it.

The dead in the room went quiet.

Not gone. They were still there, still hooked into his marks, still crowded close. But they'd stopped saying his names. They'd gotten what they'd come for. They'd gotten a claim spoken in the old words, by a Graves, over a body that would've been theirs in another minute, and they'd heard it, and they'd answered it, and they were satisfied.

Silas looked down at his wrist.

*Isola.* Wet and black and shining, between two names his mother had put there before he could walk. Five letters. Not the rest of it. Just the one.

He felt it pulse. Once. In time with her heart under his other hand.

Then he felt his own heart stop.

It wasn't pain. He'd expected pain. It was quieter than that. It was like something in his chest had reached the end of a long rope and found nothing there, and just let go. He felt it skip, and then not come back. He felt his lungs reach for air and find that they'd forgotten how. He was still standing. He was still looking at his wrist. And everything inside him was going very slow, and very far away, and very quiet, like somebody knocking on a door from deep inside a house.

He knew what it was. He'd been told. Every Graves child was told. Your body took the shape of the ending you touched. He'd put his hand on a dying woman and made her his, and his body had heard him, and done what bodies did.

"Silas," Rhea said, from somewhere.

His knees went. His hand slid off Isola's chest. He felt the linen go out from under his fingers, and the edge of the table go by, and the floor come up, and then he was lying on the boards of his own kitchen with his cheek against the wood and his black right hand flung out beside him and his left wrist up, so that he could see her name on it in Melena's light, shining.

He tried to breathe. Nothing came.

Above him, on the table, he heard Isola take another breath, deep and steady, all on her own.

That was all right, then.

He thought that was all right.


# CHAPTER 20: ISOLA

The first thing Isola knew was that somebody else was breathing for her.

That wasn't how she'd have said it, if anybody had asked. Nobody asked. She lay in the dark with her eyes shut, and she breathed, and every breath she took came in a little after she meant it to, like there was a second set of lungs somewhere pulling on hers and hers were following. She'd reach for air, and there'd be a catch, and then it would come, and it would come from slightly the wrong direction. She lay there feeling it for a long time before she understood that it wasn't a dream.

Then she felt the heartbeat.

Not hers. Hers was there, fast and thin and too high in her chest. But underneath it, half a count behind, there was another one. Slower. Heavier. Not in her ears. Not in her chest. Somewhere she didn't have a name for, somewhere inside her that had never had anything in it before, a second pulse going *thud* just after hers went *thud*, like an echo coming back from the other side of a wall.

She opened her eyes.

Ceiling. Low and stained, with a long brown water mark over the window that looked like a map of somewhere. A lamp, lit, swinging very slightly on its chain. She didn't know the ceiling. She didn't know the lamp. She was lying on something hard and flat with cloth under her, and her right leg was cold, so cold, cold the way the cistern water was cold in winter, and she couldn't feel her foot.

She turned her head.

He was on the floor.

He was sitting with his back against the leg of the table she was lying on, his knees up, his head tipped back against the wood so that she was looking almost straight down at his face. His eyes were closed. His skin had gone a bad color, grey under the brown, the way hers had gone on the Halflight stairs. His right hand lay on the floor beside him, palm up, and it was black. Not bruised. Not dirty. Black, from the fingertips to past the second knuckle, like he'd dipped it in a well of ink and left it there.

His left arm was up on the edge of the table beside her. His wrist was turned up. His hand was very close to hers on the cloth. Not touching. Close.

On the inside of that wrist, in among a row of small dark marks she didn't know, there was a word.

*Isola.*

She looked at it for a long time.

It was her name. Written on his skin in something wet and black that shone in the lamplight. Five letters. Not *Amara*, not *Renae*, not *Wraithmoor*. Just the first one. The one her mama picked. The one she'd turned around at the Ashvein gate and given him, on purpose, three steps from leaving, because she'd wanted him to have it.

It was on his skin like it had always been there.

"Don't," somebody said, from across the room.

Isola didn't know she'd started to move until she heard it. Her hand had come up off the cloth, her left, the one that wasn't holding anything. It was reaching for his wrist.

She stopped.

A woman was standing at the window. Tall. Hair wrapped high in dark cloth. Marks inked across the backs of both her hands, the ink gone dull and cracked like it had been used up. She had a face Isola had never seen before, and she was looking at Isola with an expression Isola didn't like at all, because it was the expression Selah wore when one of them was sick and she didn't know yet if they'd get better.

"Don't touch it," the woman said. "Not yet."

"Who are you."

Her voice came out of her wrong. Hoarse and thin, like she'd been screaming. She didn't remember screaming.

"Rhea." The woman didn't come closer. "His sister."

*Rhea.* The name went through Isola's head and didn't land on anything. Then it did. *Jude's brother*, Micah had said at the sink. *Jude, Silas, and Rhea.* Dante Mercer's children. Micah's godsiblings.

"Where am I."

"Third floor. Number 3 Shadowmere Lane. His kitchen table."

Isola looked up at the ceiling again. At the water stain over the window. *My building's got a number painted over on the front,* he'd written her, two nights ago, at midnight, while she sat in her mama's kitchen. *Somebody did it twice. You can still see the three under it.*

She was lying on his kitchen table.

"Asha," she said.

Rhea's face didn't change.

"Lie still," Rhea said, "and I'll try."

Isola didn't lie still. She looked down at herself instead. Her coat was gone. Someone had folded it and set it on a chair by the wall. Her skirt was pulled up past her knee, and her right leg lay on a white sheet with the lamp shining full on it, and she looked at it, and she didn't recognize it.

It was grey. The whole calf, the ankle, up past the knee. Grey and wrong and sunken, like something left out in the rain too long. And through it, in long black lines, like cracks running through glass, the marks. Three of them climbing the outside of her ankle. One going up behind her knee and leaving a trench in her flesh that she could see the shape of from here. And a single dark point on the inside of her shin, like the head of a nail.

None of it was moving. She'd watched it move on the Halflight stairs, climbing, and she'd held on. It wasn't moving now. It just lay there in her skin, dead and black.

Except one.

One line, the highest one, ran up the inside of her thigh to a place about a hand's width below her hip. And there, it turned. It bent away from her hip and curled back down and across the sheet, toward the edge of the table, and stopped at the very edge of her skin like it was pointing.

Pointing at his wrist.

Her left hand was still holding something. She looked down at it. A strip of paper, narrow and bone-white, crumpled almost to nothing in her fist. She opened her fingers.

ASHA NOELLE WRAITHMOOR.

Black to the middle of the I.

She'd held on. She remembered that much. She remembered the Halflight floor and the bars against her back and saying her cousin's name with no sound until there was nothing left in her to say it with. She remembered the Shroud coming off the bottom step. She remembered thinking his was the last voice.

She didn't remember anything after that. Not clearly. Pieces. Iron stairs turning in the dark. A man's coat in her fist. His throat against her face, warm. Telling somebody they were holding her wrong.

She closed her hand back over the strip.

"What happened," she said.

Rhea didn't answer.

On the floor, Silas opened his eyes.

---

He didn't move his head. He just opened his eyes and looked up at her from the floor, from where he was sitting against the table leg, and she looked down at him, and for a long moment neither of them said anything.

The second pulse in her chest picked up. Just a little. She felt it go. She felt her own heart, high and fast, and his, slow and heavy and half a count behind, and she felt his speed up toward hers, like somebody hurrying to catch up.

Then something else happened, and she hated it immediately.

She settled.

It was her body. Not her. Her body, lying on this table with her leg dead and her name on a stranger's wrist, and the second his eyes opened and found hers, it eased. Her breath came easier. The cold in her leg went down a notch. The catch in her chest, the one that had been there since she woke up, the one that came from somebody else's lungs pulling on hers, let go a little, like a knot loosening. She felt it happen. She felt every muscle in her back come down off the table half an inch, like she was a dog that had heard its owner's step on the stairs.

She had never in her life been so angry at her own body.

"That's not me," Isola said.

Her voice shook. She made it stop. She lifted her free hand off the sheet, slowly, and pointed. Not at him. At his wrist. At the five black letters on the inside of it.

"That's not me," she said again. "That's *this*. That's whatever *that* is. That's not me."

Silas looked at her finger. Then at his wrist. Then back up at her face.

"I know," he said.

He said it quietly. He didn't argue. He didn't try to tell her it was something else, or that she was wrong, or that she should be grateful. He just agreed with her, flat and plain and immediate, like she'd told him the sky was up.

She hated that he agreed. She hated it more than anything else in this room. She'd wanted him to argue. She'd wanted something to push against.

"What is it," she said.

He didn't answer.

"What *is* it. On your wrist. My name's on your wrist, and my leg's—" Her voice cracked. She stopped. She started again. "Somebody tell me what that is."

Nobody told her.

So she reached for it.

Rhea said "*Don't*" again from the window, sharp, and started across the room. Isola didn't stop this time. She reached across the edge of the table to where his wrist lay against the wood, and she pressed two fingers against her own name.

His skin was cold. The letters were colder. They were wet. Not on the surface. Under it. She could feel them under her fingertips the way she could feel a name on the wall at home, the thread through the strip, the shape of it.

She said her name.

She said all of it. The whole chain. The way Omari had taught them, low and even, each part set down carefully before the next. The cadence her father used for the wall and nowhere else. She'd said it for Micah's strip and for Asha's under the table and for Asha's in the trench and on the Halflight floor. She'd never said it for her own. She'd never needed to. Her name had never once in her life tried to leave her.

*Isola. Amara. Renae. Wraithmoor.*

The name answered.

She felt it. Under her fingers, in the black letters on his wrist, the warmth came up the way it came up out of the strips on the wall at home, the thread pulling taut, the pressure on the other side of the metal pressing back against her palm like a hand.

It pressed back. But not toward her.

It pressed toward *him*. She felt it go. She'd spoken her own name in her own father's cadence, the way she'd spoken a hundred other names, and every one of those names had answered *her*. Had pressed back against *her* hand. This one didn't. This one took her voice and her cadence and her four names, all of them, the whole chain, and turned them, and gave them to the man on the floor. She felt the warmth go into his wrist. She felt his pulse jump under her fingers, half a count behind hers. She felt her name settle into him like a key settling into a lock.

It held. To him.

Isola took her hand back.

She lay on his table and stared at the ceiling and didn't say anything for a long time. She heard Rhea stop a few feet away. She heard Silas breathe on the floor below her, slow and heavy, and felt her own lungs pull with his.

Nobody answered.

"I'm not asking *her*." She didn't look away from the water stain. "I'm asking you. What did you do."

From the floor, after a while, he said, "I said *mine*."

She didn't know what that meant. She knew it was something. She could hear it in the way he said it, and in the way Rhea went completely still beside the table, and in the silence that came after it, which had the weight of a thing that had been decided a long time ago by people she'd never met.

"What does that mean."

Silas didn't answer.

She turned her head and looked at Rhea. "What does it mean."

Rhea looked back at her with that same expression, Selah's expression, the sickbed one. "I'm not the one who should tell you that."

"Then who."

From the doorway, a voice she didn't know: "Not me."

Isola turned her head. A man was standing in the door of the kitchen. Green coat. Silver cuff on his ear. His locs tied back, coming loose. The man from Bath Row, from the cut by the laundry, the one who'd been talking the whole time. He had a burned hand and a face like he hadn't slept in a week.

"I'm sorry," he said, and he sounded like he meant it. "It's not mine to tell. I'm sorry."

She looked back down at the floor. At Silas, with his head against the table leg and his black hand in his lap and her name on his wrist.

"What does it mean," she said again. To him.

He looked at her for a long time.

"Later," he said.

Isola didn't see it start. She'd turned her head away and was looking at the wall, at a shelf of thick ledgers and a stack of what looked like unpaid tram notices, and a plant in the window that somebody had kept alive out of pure spite. She heard Remy say something low to Rhea. She heard Rhea say something back. She heard them both crouch down by the table leg, and Silas say *I'm fine*, and Remy say *You're on the floor, Si*, and then the scrape of somebody being lifted.

The room went white.

It started in her chest, in the place where his pulse had been sitting half a count behind hers. It started there and went out in every direction at once, like a knife going in and turning. She heard someone scream. She didn't know it was her until it ran out of air.

Her lungs stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. They reached for air the way they'd been reaching all night, waiting for that second set of lungs to pull, and there was nothing to pull with. There was nothing on the other end. She lay there with her mouth open and her chest locked, and her heart went fast, faster, slamming, and she couldn't make it do anything.

Across the room, at the same moment, she heard a sound like a man choking.

"*Put him down*," Rhea said. "Put him down, Remy, *put him back*, put him—"

The scrape again. Something heavy hitting the floor beside the table, too hard. A hand, his, the left one, landing on the edge of the table by her hip. Not touching her. Close.

Her lungs pulled.

Air. All at once, cold and huge, coming in from slightly the wrong direction. She heard him take a breath on the floor at exactly the same time, ragged, desperate, a man coming up out of deep water. The pulse came back into her chest. His, half a count behind. She lay there and breathed and felt it settle back where it had been, felt her whole body come down off the table half an inch, felt the cold in her leg ease.

She lay there and hated every bit of it.

Nobody tried to move him again.

It wasn't hers this time. It came from below, from the second floor, thin at first through the boards and then loud, and with it came heat. Isola felt it. She felt it come up through the wood of the table under her back, warm and then hot, like lying on a stove somebody had just lit. The lamp over the table swung on its chain. Somewhere below, glass broke.

She'd seen that girl. In the doorway of this kitchen, before Isola had ever opened her eyes. She didn't remember it, but she knew it somehow, the way she knew she'd told somebody they were holding her wrong. A girl in the doorway with fire in her hands, holding it low, holding it careful. The red-haired girl from Pell's stall. From the cut on Bath Row.

The fire girl.

Rhea's voice was very quiet. "With Dante."


# CHAPTER 21: SILAS

Camille nailed him to a board a little after midnight, and he let her.

She didn't ask. She'd taken one look at him on the floor, at the black creeping up his right hand and the grey in his face, at the way his chest moved when Isola's did and stopped when hers caught, and she'd gone straight to her bag. She came out with a funeral board, the narrow kind she kept for moving bodies through tight stairwells, pale wood worn smooth by a lot of years and a lot of dead. She came out with a fistful of grave nails, iron, black at the heads. She came out with a mallet.

"On it," she said.

Remy and Rhea got him onto it without moving him more than a foot from the table. They'd learned that much. Camille laid him flat with his head toward the table leg and his left arm up along the edge of it, his wrist still turned to the lamp, close enough to Isola's hand that he could feel the warmth coming off her skin without touching it. Then she knelt beside him on the floor.

"This is going to hurt," she said.

"Okay."

"I wasn't asking."

She set the first nail through the cuff of his shirt at the wrist, the right one, the black one, and drove it into the board. It didn't go through him. It went through the cloth beside him, close, so close he felt the cold of the iron against his skin. Then the next one, on the other side of his wrist. Then his elbow. His shoulder. His collar, both sides. His hips. Down the length of him, nail by nail, until he was pinned flat to the board in a frame of black iron heads.

Every nail she drove, the dead let go of him a little.

He felt them do it. They'd been hooked into him since Halflight, a hundred cold fingers under his marks, pulling soft and constant, the way a current pulls at your legs when you're standing in a river. Each nail Camille set gave them something else to hold. He felt them move off him and onto the iron, one at a time, reluctant, the way people move out of a doorway when somebody bigger needs through. When she was finished, they were still there. They were still all around him. But they were holding on to the nails now instead of to him.

He breathed. Really breathed, for the first time since he'd said *mine*. Not all the way. Not on his own. But his chest came up off the board and went back down without waiting for hers.

On the table above him, he heard Isola breathe at the same time, and then, a half count later, on her own.

Camille sat back on her heels.

"That'll hold till morning," she said. "Maybe." She was looking at his wrist. At the five letters. Her face didn't do anything at all. "Then I'm going to try to close it."

She tried at first light.

Rhea had stood at the window all night. Remy had gone downstairs around two to sit with Melena, who'd stopped crying sometime after the letter burned and gone very quiet, and he hadn't come back up. Isola had slept, on and off, on the table, her hand curled around the strip with her cousin's name on it. Silas hadn't slept at all. He lay on the board in his frame of nails and listened to her breathe and felt every breath she took go through his chest a half count after it went through hers.

When the window went grey, Camille got up from the chair where she'd been sitting since midnight. She knelt beside the board. She took his left wrist in both her hands and turned it to the light.

She looked at the name for a long time.

He'd watched his mother look at a lot of work. Other people's thresholds, other people's stitching, other people's knots in funeral thread. She looked at bad work with her mouth gone flat. She looked at good work with her eyebrows up. "Every name on this arm," Camille said quietly, "I put there." She ran her thumb along the old marks without touching the new one. "This one. And this one. This one's your grandmother. This one's the route. This one's for everything that comes through and needs putting back." Her thumb stopped a hair from the first letter of *Isola*. "I didn't put this one."

She set the point of it under the first letter.

Under the *I*. Just the edge. Just the very bottom of the stroke where it met his skin.

She lifted.

On the table above him, Isola screamed.

It was the same scream from last night, from when Remy tried to move him. It went through the room like a blade. Silas felt it before he heard it. He felt it in his own chest, in his wrist, in the place where her pulse sat on top of his. He felt it in the black on his hand. And he felt something else, worse, under all of it.

The deathwork moved.

Not in him. In her. He felt it through the name. He felt the black in her leg, that highest line, the one that had turned away from her hip when the last letter closed and pointed at his hand. He felt it turn back. He felt it start to climb again, toward the door, toward her hip, fast, faster than it had climbed on the Halflight stairs, like something that had been held back and was angry about it.

Camille took the hook out.

You did it exactly right. That's the worst part."

She stood up.

She stood there over him for a moment, looking down at her son nailed to a funeral board on his own kitchen floor with a stranger's name on his wrist, and then she looked at the stranger, lying on the table with her eyes open now, staring at the ceiling, tears running sideways into her hair. Then she picked up her shawl from the hook by the door.

She left. They heard her on the stairs, heavy and quick, all the way down. They heard the building door. Then nothing.

Mother Rae called a little after that.

She listened again. Her hand tightened on the phone.

*Asha.* The name on the strip in her fist. The name he'd watched her mouth shape on the Halflight floor, over and over, holding on.

She'd sent her cousin away from the Shroud, and her cousin had made it.

He found that he was very glad, and that it had nothing to do with the tether at all.

"I want my mama," Isola said.

It was full morning by then. Rhea had made tea that nobody drank. Remy had come up from the second floor with a face like he'd been awake for three days, which he had, and Melena had come up behind him. She stood in the kitchen doorway in a borrowed sweater with her arms wrapped around herself, and her hands were clean and brown and still, and her eyes were raw.

"I don't care."

I know what's wrong with *this*—" She jerked her chin at Silas's wrist without looking at it. Nobody said anything.

Rhea looked at him.

"Under the building. Past the boiler. Ma uses it." He was looking at the ceiling too. At the water stain. "It runs under half the old blocks on Third Lane. It goes up the service line to Ashvein. Rhea looked at him for a long moment.

"And you go with her," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I don't have a choice."

"No," Rhea said. "You don't. Do you." And something went across her face, fast, and was gone, and he knew she wasn't talking about the tether.

"I'm not staying here alone," she said. "I'm not staying anywhere alone. I did that. "She comes," Remy said.

So she came.

Getting him off the board was the worst of it.

Camille's nails had to come out one at a time. Rhea did it, with the claw end of the mallet, slow, and every nail that came out gave the dead one less thing to hold, and they came back to him. He felt them hook back in under his marks, one after another, cold and patient. By the time the last nail was out, his chest had gone tight again, and his breath had started catching every time Isola's did, and the black on his right hand had climbed another half inch past the knuckle.

They couldn't carry him. Remy tried, and Silas felt the distance between his chest and the table open up by about a foot and a half, and Isola made a sound like she'd been stabbed, and his own lungs locked, and Remy put him back down so fast he hit his head on the floor.

They had two of them in the cellar. They'd always had two of them in the cellar. Silas had walked past them a thousand times. They got Silas onto the other one, still on Camille's board, and they wheeled the two trolleys side by side out of his kitchen and to the stairs, and they kept them so close together that the rails touched.

Isola reached across the gap.

She didn't look at him. She reached across the rails with her left hand and found his left hand, the one that still worked, the one with her name on the wrist, and she took hold of his fingers. Not his hand. His fingers. Two of them. On the second-floor landing, the trolleys bumped, and Isola's grip slipped off his fingers, just for a second.

She screamed.

He felt her lungs stop. He felt his own stop with them. For one long white second, both of them hung there in the stairwell with no air in them at all, and Melena lunged across the gap and grabbed Isola's wrist and shoved it back against his hand, and Isola's fingers closed on his again, hard, so hard her nails went into his skin.

Air.

They kept going.

---

The burial rail ran out of the back of the cellar, through a low iron door past the boiler that Silas had always thought led to a coal store. It didn't. It led to a narrow brick platform lit by a single blue lamp, and a track, and a short car waiting on the track, black, with no windows and a long open bed in the back meant for laying things down.

Rhea opened the doors. She put Underchant on the frame with her inked hands, low, fast, a string of words Silas knew. Funeral terms. The ones you said over a cart so the gates would know what it was carrying. *Here lies. Here passes. Here goes down and does not come back.* Every gate on the service line would read those terms and see what they said. A car full of the dead, going home.

They lifted the trolleys in side by side.

Melena got in. Then Remy. Then Rhea, last, pulling the doors shut behind her and laying her palm flat against them until they hummed. The car jerked once, and began to move.

It was dark in the back of the car. The blue lamp on the platform slid away behind them and was gone, and there was nothing but the sound of the wheels on the old iron track, and the sway, and breathing.

Silas lay on Camille's board on his trolley with his head turned toward the wall of the car, and Isola lay on hers with her head turned toward the other wall, and between them, across the rails, she held on to his first two fingers.

The car went over a joint in the track.

It wasn't much. A jolt. The kind of jolt every tram in Shadowmere made a hundred times a day. But the two trolleys slid apart on the floor of the car, an inch, two inches, and her fingers slipped, and the gap opened.

His lungs seized. Just like that. Like somebody had put a hand inside his chest and closed it. He heard her breath stop in the dark at exactly the same moment, a short hard catch, and then nothing.

He couldn't move his hand. His right was dead and his left was stretched as far toward her as it would go and it wasn't far enough, and he lay there in the dark with no air and her not breathing three inches away, and he thought, very clearly, *this is what I did.*

Her hand found his.

Not his fingers. His whole hand. She found it in the dark like she'd known exactly where it would be, and she didn't take hold of his fingers this time. She took his wrist. She pulled it across the gap between the trolleys, his whole arm, hard, until his shoulder screamed, and she laid his open palm flat against her own side, against her ribs, just below her breast, over her heart.

She held it there.

Air came back. He felt it come into her under his palm, felt her ribs lift against his hand, felt her heart going hard and fast just under the bone. He breathed with it. He couldn't help it. His lungs pulled when hers did, and they both breathed, and the dark of the car came back into the shape of a car.

She didn't let go.

After a while, she moved his hand. She pushed it higher. Just a little. Up her ribs, an inch, two, so that the heel of his palm sat right over the place where her heart was loudest. She pressed his hand there with her own, flat, and held it.

Something in her eased. He felt it. Her whole body, under his hand, coming down a degree. Her breath going long and slow. The cold in her, the cold that had been in her since the Halflight floor, backing off a little where his skin was against hers.

He felt the same thing in himself, and he didn't let himself have it.

Neither of them looked at the other. She kept her face to the far wall of the car. He kept his to the near one. In the dark, with the wheels going under them and Remy murmuring something low to Melena up at the front and Rhea breathing very steadily by the door, he lay there with his hand on a woman's ribs that she'd put there herself, and he made himself name it.

He made himself name it exactly, the way Camille had taught him to name every part of a death tether on a cold man's leg. You didn't get to look away. You didn't get to round it off. You named what was there.

It was pain. That was the first thing. She was in pain, and so was he, and this made it stop. That was all. That was what this was. His hand on her was relief, for both of them, the same way a hand pressed to a wound was relief, and it wasn't anything more than that.

It was her choice. She'd made it. She'd reached across the gap in the dark and taken his wrist and put his hand where she wanted it. He hadn't reached for her. He hadn't asked. He wouldn't have. But she'd made the choice with no air in her lungs and a dead leg and a stranger's tether pulling on her heart, and he knew what a choice made under that much weight was worth. It was worth something. It wasn't worth everything. It wasn't what she'd have chosen at a little table on the Ironcross line with her knees an inch from his and black three warm on the table between them.

And it wasn't his. That was the last part, and the hardest. Nothing about this was his to keep. Not the way she'd eased under his hand. Not the way her heart had slowed. Not the way, when she pushed his palm higher, he'd felt something go through him that had nothing to do with breathing. He'd put her name on his wrist without asking her. He didn't get to take anything else.

Pain relief. Her choice, under pressure. Nothing he gets to keep.

He said it to himself three times in the dark. He made himself hear all three parts every time.

Then the car began to slow.

He felt it in the board under his back. The wheels changing their sound on the track. The sway going out of it. Up at the front, Remy stopped murmuring. By the door, Rhea stood up, and laid her palm flat on the frame again, and Silas heard the low hum of her terms come up out of the iron.

Somewhere above them, very faint, through the roof of the car and the rock above it and whatever was above that, a bell was ringing. Not a work bell. Not first bell or third bell or final. A long, flat, steady tolling, over and over, that Silas had never heard before but knew all the same.

The bell they rang in Ashvein when a ward was under seal.

Isola's hand tightened on his.

The car stopped.

The burial rail came up under Sixth Ward in a cinder pit.

Silas hadn't known that. He'd known the service line ran up into Ashvein. He'd known the funeral cars went up there empty and came back down full, and that Camille had used it more than once for things she never explained. He hadn't known where it stopped. When the car's doors opened onto a long, low cave of black slag and old ash, with a stone platform along one side and iron rungs set into the far wall going up into the dark, he lay on his trolley and looked at it and didn't understand where he was.

Isola did.

He felt her know. Her hand tightened on his where it held his palm against her ribs, and her heart under it jumped, and she turned her head for the first time since the car started moving and looked up at the rungs in the wall.

"That's ours," she said.

"Yours what?"

"Our pit. The cinder pit. Under the row." Her voice was very strange. "That's the ash chute. Up there. That's Daddy's stove."

They couldn't get the trolleys up the rungs.

Then there was a scrape, and a clang, and a square of dim yellow light opened in the ceiling of the pit, and a man's voice came down through it, low and very hard.

"Who's down there."

Isola's whole body went tight under Silas's hand.

"Daddy," she said. Not loud. She didn't need to be loud. "Daddy, it's me."

The light in the ceiling went still.

He was flat on Camille's board on a funeral trolley at the bottom of a cinder pit with a dead hand and a stranger's breath tied to his, and everything happened above him or around him or too fast. Then another man came down it, a big man in work clothes with keys on his belt that rang when he moved, and he went straight past Silas like Silas wasn't there and crouched beside Isola's trolley and put his hands on her face.

"Daddy."

Omari Wraithmoor didn't need anybody to read him a name. He'd been reading names on walls since before Silas was born. "Lucia?" her mother said.

Isola heard it. She was lying on her own dining table with her leg dead under her mother's hand and a stranger's sister holding the black in it still and a stranger's pulse going half a count behind hers somewhere inside her chest, and she heard her mother say a name she'd never heard her say in her life.

She didn't know any Lucia. Nobody in Sixth Ward was named Lucia. Nobody on the wall was named Lucia. She'd have known.

She turned her head.

The red-haired girl was standing in the kitchen doorway. Hood down. All that hair coming loose over the shoulders of a borrowed sweater, red as the inside of a coal, the same red Isola had seen slip out from under a hood at Pell's counter. The fire girl. She was staring at Selah's hands on the table, at the frost spreading out pale from under them, and her face had gone white.

"That was my mother," the girl said.

The hum didn't stop. Isola could feel it. It was coming down through Selah's palm into Silas's hand on the table and from his hand, somehow, into her, the same six notes she'd heard her mother hum at the stove every morning of her life, over and over, low and steady. She'd never once thought they meant anything. She'd thought they were just Selah.

"Lucia," Selah said again, softer. Not a question this time.

"My mother's name was Lucia." The girl's voice was shaking. "How do you know her name? How do you know *that*?" Her eyes went to Selah's hands again. "That's the Elders' Prayer. That's what it's called. My mother told me. She told me its name when I was small. She said she couldn't do it herself, because she wasn't a Frost like my father. She said it takes a Frost." She looked at Selah's arms, at the frost lace climbing them, and something in her face broke open. "How are you doing it?"

Selah's eyes stayed on the girl's face. The hum went on.

"I don't know what a Frost is, baby," Selah said, between the notes. "I know what living blood feels like."

It went on a long time after that.

Isola didn't know how long. She lost track somewhere in the middle of it. She lay on the table and felt her mother's cold come into her through the man's hand on the wood beside her hip, and she felt it go into her leg, and she felt what it did there.

It hurt. She'd expected that. She hadn't expected how. It wasn't the hurt of the Shroud, the cold going in, the nail. It was the other way. It was like something deep in her leg had been torn loose and was being pulled back, slowly, toward where it was supposed to be. The muscle. She could feel it. All the meat of her calf that the black had gone through and left in rags, being drawn back in toward the bone, slow, slow, like her mother was pulling it with a thread. She bit down on the inside of her cheek until she tasted blood. She didn't scream. She'd decided she wasn't going to scream in front of the fire girl.

Then her foot got warm.

She hadn't been able to feel her foot since the trench. She'd stopped thinking of it as hers. It was just there at the end of her leg, cold, gone, like something somebody had set down and forgotten. And now it was warm. She felt it start in her heel and go up into the arch and out into her toes, a slow, prickling heat, pins and needles like after you'd sat on it too long, and she lay there and felt her own blood come back into her own foot for the first time in a day, and she started to cry and couldn't stop.

She didn't look at anybody while she did it. She looked at the ceiling. She let it run sideways into her hair.

Beside her, on the floor, she felt the man's heart stop.

It just went. She'd been feeling it under hers all this time, slow and heavy, half a count behind, answering her mother's hum on the fourth note. And then it didn't. There was a beat, and a space where the next beat should be, and nothing in it. She felt her own heart speed up, fast, slamming, like it was trying to do the work for both of them. She felt her lungs pull, and pull, and find nothing on the other end pulling back.

"*Mama*—"

Selah's hand pressed down.

Isola saw it. Her mother's palm, flat on top of his on the table, pressing. Not hard. Steady. The frost on Selah's arms flared white, all at once, up past her elbows. The hum didn't break. It went around again, the same six notes, and on the fourth one, very faintly, under Isola's own heart, his came back.

On the sixth, he breathed.

Isola lay there and breathed with him and hated how glad she was.

Selah didn't stop.

Isola looked too. She made herself. The grey was gone from her calf. The skin was brown again, her skin, warm-looking under the lamp.
