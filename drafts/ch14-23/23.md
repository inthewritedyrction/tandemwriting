# CHAPTER 23: ISOLA

"Lucia?" her mother said.

Isola heard it. She was lying on her own dining table with her leg dead under her mother's hand and a stranger's sister holding the black in it still and a stranger's pulse going half a count behind hers somewhere inside her chest, and she heard her mother say a name she'd never heard her say in her life.

She didn't know any Lucia. Nobody in Sixth Ward was named Lucia. Nobody on the wall was named Lucia. She'd have known.

She turned her head.

The red-haired girl was standing in the kitchen doorway. Hood down. All that hair coming loose over the shoulders of a borrowed sweater, red as the inside of a coal, the same red Isola had seen slip out from under a hood at Pell's counter. The fire girl. She was staring at Selah's hands on the table, at the frost spreading out pale from under them, and her face had gone white.

"That was my mother," the girl said.

The hum didn't stop. Isola could feel it. It was coming down through Selah's palm into Silas's hand on the table and from his hand, somehow, into her, the same six notes she'd heard her mother hum at the stove every morning of her life, over and over, low and steady. She'd never once thought they meant anything. She'd thought they were just Selah.

"Lucia," Selah said again, softer. Not a question this time.

"My mother's name was Lucia." The girl's voice was shaking. "How do you know her name? How do you know *that*?" Her eyes went to Selah's hands again. "That's the Elders' Prayer. That's what it's called. My mother told me. She told me its name when I was small. She said she couldn't do it herself, because she wasn't a Frost like my father. She said it takes a Frost." She looked at Selah's arms, at the frost lace climbing them, and something in her face broke open. "How are you doing it?"

Selah's eyes stayed on the girl's face. The hum went on.

"I don't know what a Frost is, baby," Selah said, between the notes. "I know what living blood feels like."

---

It went on a long time after that.

Isola didn't know how long. She lost track somewhere in the middle of it. She lay on the table and felt her mother's cold come into her through the man's hand on the wood beside her hip, and she felt it go into her leg, and she felt what it did there.

It hurt. She'd expected that. She hadn't expected how. It wasn't the hurt of the Shroud, the cold going in, the nail. It was the other way. It was like something deep in her leg had been torn loose and was being pulled back, slowly, toward where it was supposed to be. The muscle. She could feel it. All the meat of her calf that the black had gone through and left in rags, being drawn back in toward the bone, slow, slow, like her mother was pulling it with a thread. She bit down on the inside of her cheek until she tasted blood. She didn't scream. She'd decided she wasn't going to scream in front of the fire girl.

Then her foot got warm.

She hadn't been able to feel her foot since the trench. She'd stopped thinking of it as hers. It was just there at the end of her leg, cold, gone, like something somebody had set down and forgotten. And now it was warm. She felt it start in her heel and go up into the arch and out into her toes, a slow, prickling heat, pins and needles like after you'd sat on it too long, and she lay there and felt her own blood come back into her own foot for the first time in a day, and she started to cry and couldn't stop.

She didn't look at anybody while she did it. She looked at the ceiling. She let it run sideways into her hair.

Beside her, on the floor, she felt the man's heart stop.

It just went. She'd been feeling it under hers all this time, slow and heavy, half a count behind, answering her mother's hum on the fourth note. And then it didn't. There was a beat, and a space where the next beat should be, and nothing in it. She felt her own heart speed up, fast, slamming, like it was trying to do the work for both of them. She felt her lungs pull, and pull, and find nothing on the other end pulling back.

"*Mama*—"

Selah's hand pressed down.

Isola saw it. Her mother's palm, flat on top of his on the table, pressing. Not hard. Steady. The frost on Selah's arms flared white, all at once, up past her elbows. The hum didn't break. It went around again, the same six notes, and on the fourth one, very faintly, under Isola's own heart, his came back.

On the sixth, he breathed.

Isola lay there and breathed with him and hated how glad she was.

The frost kept climbing. Isola watched it. It had been at Selah's wrists when this started, a pale lace over the brown. Then her forearms. Then past her elbows. Now it was reaching for her shoulders, white and fine and creeping, and Selah's breath had started to smoke in the warm kitchen air, and her hands on the table had gone a color Isola had never seen a living hand go.

"Mama."

Selah didn't answer. Her eyes were closed. The hum went on.

"Mama, your arms—"

"Stop."

It was him. On the floor. His voice was wrecked, hoarse and thin, but it was his. Isola turned her head. He'd opened his eyes. He was looking up past the edge of the table at Selah.

"Ma'am," he said. "Stop. That's enough."

Selah didn't stop.

"You go that far," he said, "I can't come get you."

Isola didn't know what that meant. She knew he meant it. She heard it in his voice, the same flat, plain way he'd said *Debt's mine* at Pell's counter. Like it was just a fact, and he was telling her mother because her mother ought to have it.

Selah opened her eyes.

She looked down at him for a long moment. Then she looked at her own arms, frost to the shoulders. Then she let the hum go.

She did it slowly. She let it come down, one note at a time, the way you'd let a full bucket down into a cistern instead of dropping it. The frost on her arms stopped climbing. It didn't go away. It just stopped. She lifted her palm off his hand, and her other hand off Isola's ankle, and she held both of them up in front of her and looked at them.

Her left hand wouldn't close. Isola watched her try. Watched her mother look at her own fingers and tell them to curl, and watched them not.

"Mama."

"I'm all right."

"Your hand—"

"I said I'm all right." Selah set both hands down on the edge of the table, very carefully, like they were made of glass. "Hush, now. Let me look at you."

She looked at the leg.

Isola looked too. She made herself. The grey was gone from her calf. The skin was brown again, her skin, warm-looking under the lamp. The trench behind her knee was still there, but shallower, smoother, like the muscle had been pulled back in to fill most of it. Her toes moved when she told them to. All five.

But the black was still there.

All of it. The three branches above the ankle. The one behind the knee. The point on her shin where the nail had gone in. Dead and dark and still. Selah's frost had run right alongside every line of it, close, the way you'd walk beside somebody in the dark, and hadn't touched a single one.

"Mama," Isola said. "Can I walk?"

Selah looked at her for a long time.

"No, baby," she said.

Isola lay there.

Selah touched her face with the back of her good hand, cold, so cold. "The bone's still broke. I can set that. I'll set it tomorrow when I got my hands back. And the meat's back where it belongs, mostly. But the roads that thing killed in you." She looked at the black lines. "The little roads. The ones the blood runs down. Those are dead. I can't bring those back. Nobody can." She smoothed Isola's hair off her forehead. "I'm sorry."

On the floor, the man was breathing on his own. Isola could feel it. Not half a count behind her anymore, not pulling. Just there, slow and heavy and steady, holding by itself. Her mother hadn't closed whatever was wrong in him. Isola knew that without being told. Selah had just gotten his living body far enough from whatever was trying to end it to keep breathing beside it.

That was all. That was something.

She turned her head and looked at him.

It was the first time she'd looked at him since the cinder pit. Since her father had hauled him up off the floor by the shirt. Since he'd gotten Daddy's arm in that hold, the one she'd watched from the rail bridge, and taken it right to the edge, and looked at her, and let go. She'd seen him decide. She was the only one in this house who knew what she'd seen. She hadn't been able to look at him since.

She looked at him now.

He was sitting on the floor against the table leg with blood dried in his beard and his black hand in his lap and her name on his wrist. He was looking back at her.

"I want this off me," Isola said.

He didn't look away.

"That don't make what you did okay," she said.

"I ain't say it did."

He said it right away. Plain and flat. No excuses after it. No *but*. He didn't tell her she'd have died. She knew she'd have died. She'd been there. He didn't tell her he'd had no choice, or that his sister had tried to stop him, or that it was the only way. He just looked at her and agreed that what he'd done wasn't okay, and left it there between them on the kitchen floor where she could see it.

It was the second time he'd agreed with her today.

She still hated it.

She looked away first.

---

The vent opened a little after that.

Isola heard it before anybody else did. She'd been listening for that sound her whole life without knowing it, the scrape of the iron grating by the kitchen floor coming loose from the wall. Tevin, in the doorway, spun around. Micah went for the knife on the counter. Omari, at the sink, didn't move at all, but Isola watched his shoulders go tight.

Asha came out of the wall.

She came out feet first, the way Isola had gone in, sliding down out of the dark with her coat torn and her face black with coal smoke and her hair coming down out of its wrap. She landed on the kitchen floor on her hands and knees. She got up. She looked around the room, at all of them, at the strangers, at Selah with her frozen hands, at the man on the floor.

Then she looked at the table, and at Isola's leg, and her face fell apart.

"No," Asha said.

She came across the kitchen. She didn't run. She walked very slowly, like the floor might go out from under her, and she stopped at the edge of the table and looked down at the black lines in Isola's skin, at the trench behind the knee, at the place on the shin where the nail went in.

"No," she said again. "No. No, no, no, I told you, I *told* you to let it go, I told you not to hold it, I—"

"Asha."

"*I told you*—"

"I know you did." Isola reached up with her free hand, the one that wasn't holding the strip, and caught her cousin's wrist. Pulled her down. Asha came down onto the edge of the table with her face in Isola's shoulder, sobbing without any sound at all, the way she'd sobbed in the Silence in the trench. Isola held on to her. She didn't say anything. There wasn't anything to say. She'd known exactly what it would cost when she reached past her cousin and took that strip out of the Shroud's hand. She'd done it anyway.

She'd do it again. She thought Asha probably knew that too.

After a while, Asha lifted her head. She wiped her face with the back of her hand and left a long streak of coal black across her cheek.

"I came from Godmother's," she said. "Through the trench. They're—Isola, they're everywhere. All down the cloth route, all over Shadowmere, there's people asking. Synod people. And not Synod people." She looked at the floor. At the man. At his sister standing by the stove. At the red-haired girl in the doorway with her arms wrapped around herself. "They're asking about Halflight. About whoever opened it. They've got a description." Her eyes stayed on Silas. "And they're asking about the fire girl."

Nobody in the kitchen moved.

"It's not a description anymore," Asha said. "Not for her. It's a name. I heard it in the trench, by the old shaft head. Two of them, talking, just walking, like it was nothing." She looked at the doorway. "Melena Lockwood."

The red-haired girl went very still.

"And I heard—" Asha stopped. Swallowed. "They said Blackwood. Right next to it. Right after her name. Blackwood wants to know where she's at." She turned back to Isola. "They're asking for me too. For the courier. For the package that burned. And they're asking about her, about her *debt*, in the same breath, like it's the same thing. Like whoever's collecting on me is collecting on her."

Isola looked at the doorway.

*Melena Lockwood.* She turned it over. It didn't sound like Ashvein. It didn't sound like Shadowmere. It didn't sound like anywhere she had a name for. It sounded like the stones she'd seen on that girl's collar at Pell's counter, too fine for the Back Market, too fine for anywhere.

"Who the hell," Isola said, "is Lucia?"

Nobody answered her.

The red-haired girl didn't seem to have heard. She was standing in the doorway with her arms around herself, and her face had gone from white to grey, and Isola watched the black start to creep out from under her fingernails, thin, at the quick, and felt the kitchen get warm.

Then the man in the green coat was beside her.

He didn't grab her. He didn't touch her shoulders or her face or her arms. He just reached down and took her hand. Her left one. The one with the black creeping under the nails. He took it in his like it was the most ordinary thing in the world, and held it, and said, very quietly, close to her, in a voice Isola wasn't sure she was supposed to hear:

"Not down here."

The girl looked at him.

"Not down here," he said again. "Down here, you're Mira."

The name went through the kitchen. Isola had heard it before, last night, through the floor of a stranger's building, a man saying it over and over to a girl screaming in a burning room until the room stopped burning. She heard it now, in the daylight, in her mother's kitchen, and it sounded different. It sounded like a name off a wall.

"You hear me?" Remy said. "You don't say that other name. Not in Shadowmere. Not in Ashvein. Not anywhere down here, not to anybody, not ever again. Not even to me." His thumb moved over the back of her hand. "They know it now. You heard her. They've got it. And if they hear it come out of your mouth, out of *your* mouth, then it's not a rumor anymore. Then it's true. And if it's true that you're down here—" He stopped. Isola watched him decide how much to say in a room full of strangers. "Then it's war."

The girl looked at him for a long moment. The black under her nails had stopped creeping.

"Promise me," Remy said.

"I promise."

"Say it."

"I promise," the girl said, "I won't say my name." Her voice shook and held. "Down here, I'm Mira."

The warmth in the kitchen went down.

---

Selah stood up.

She hadn't sat down. She'd been standing at the edge of the table the whole time, through the Prayer and the frost and Asha coming out of the wall, with her hands held careful in front of her like something somebody had handed her that she didn't dare drop. Now she came around the end of the table. She walked past Isola. She walked past Asha, sitting on the edge with coal on her face. She walked across the kitchen, slowly, and stopped in front of the girl in the doorway.

Mira. Melena. Whoever she was.

Selah looked at her for a long time. At her face. At her mouth, and her eyes, and her hair coming down.

"You look just like her," Selah said. "Round the mouth. I knew it soon as you came in."

The girl's eyes filled up.

"She came through here," Selah said. "Years back. More than once. I didn't know her name for a long time. She didn't give it. She'd come up out of Shadowmere with somebody, a girl or a boy or a whole family sometimes, somebody who needed to get up out of the tiers and didn't have nobody to claim them, and she'd need somewhere to sit them down for a night before she took them on. And somebody told her about this kitchen." Selah's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "Wasn't hard. Everybody in Sixth Ward knows about this kitchen."

"She sat here," the girl said. Barely a whisper.

"She never sat." Selah shook her head. "That's what I'm telling you. She'd come in that door with whoever she was bringing, and she'd sit them down at that table, and she'd make sure they ate. But she never sat. Not once. Not in all the times. She'd eat standing up at my counter, right there, with her plate in her hand." She nodded at it. At the counter by the stove, where Isola had stood a hundred times drying dishes with Micah. "I asked her once. Why she wouldn't sit down. And she said if she sat down in anybody's kitchen, she'd start to feel like a guest. And she said she couldn't afford to be a guest anywhere. Not up there and not down here. She said a guest has to leave when they're told."

The girl in the doorway put her hand over her mouth.

"That's what I know about your mama," Selah said. "That's the true thing. She ate standing up at my counter, and she laughed with her whole face, and she never once in her life let anybody tell her when to leave." She reached out, slow, with her good hand, and touched the girl's cheek with the back of her cold fingers, the way she'd touched Isola's. "I don't know what they wrote about her up there. Wherever up there is. But I don't expect they wrote that."

The girl started to cry.

She didn't make any sound. She just stood in the doorway of the Wraithmoor kitchen with Remy holding her left hand and Selah's cold fingers on her cheek, and she cried, and the room didn't get warm at all.

Isola lay on the table and watched it.

She didn't know who Lucia was. She didn't know who this girl was, not really, not past a name she wasn't supposed to say and a fire that burned when she was sad. She didn't know why a woman from somewhere too fine to have a name for had come up out of Shadowmere with strangers and stood at her mother's counter with a plate in her hand.

But she knew that look. The one on the girl's face, standing there with her mouth covered. She'd seen it on Micah's face when Danika's letter came in the spring. She'd seen it on Asha's face a minute ago, coming out of the wall. She'd seen it on her own face in the mirror more mornings than she'd ever say.

It was the look of somebody finding out that people somewhere had known the ones they'd lost. That there was a kitchen. That somebody had been paying attention.

The girl was going to stay. Isola could see it already. Whatever she'd come down seven tiers to do, whatever that letter had said that she'd burned last night, whoever Blackwood was and whatever debt he thought she owed, she wasn't going to leave this tier now. Not when the woman holding her face had fed her mother.

Isola looked away from the doorway.

She looked down at the floor instead, at the man sitting against the table leg. He wasn't watching the girl. He wasn't watching Selah. He was looking at his own left wrist, turned up in his lap, at the five black letters on it. He was looking at them the way Isola had seen Omari look at the wall this morning. Like he was reading something he'd never be able to put back.

She felt his pulse under hers. Half a count behind. Steady. On its own.

She didn't look away from him this time. She just watched him read her name on his own skin, in her mother's kitchen, with her family all around them and her leg dead on the table and the seal bell still tolling, slow and flat, somewhere out over Sixth Ward. She watched him until he felt it and looked up.

Then she held it. A count of three.

Then she turned her face to the wall.
