# CHAPTER 14: SILAS

The practice ring under Gutterlight was the only place in Shadowmere that smelled the same at noon as it did at midnight.

Sweat and old canvas and the liniment the cutmen rubbed into everybody's shoulders whether they wanted it or not. Silas had been coming down here since he was fifteen, back when Dante still thought he was going to grow out of it, and the smell of the place had gotten into him somewhere below thinking. He walked in the door and his body loosened before he told it to. Even today, with his left shoulder held together by stubbornness and Camille's liniment and his right hand still wrapped to the wrist, he felt it go.

He couldn't spar. He knew that. Camille had told him so in four different ways that morning when she came up to 3C to change the wrap on his hand, and Dante had told him so by pulling every route mark he owned, and his own body had told him so every time he tried to lift his arm above his shoulder. So he sat on the bottom rope with his back against the corner post and his bad arm in his lap, and he watched Remy hit the heavy bag.

Remy had never been a fighter. He hit the bag the way some people sang in the washroom, loud and badly and for nobody's benefit but his own. But he'd been coming down here with Silas for so long that his body knew how to do it anyway, and he worked the bag in long, lazy combinations, jab-jab-cross, jab-cross-hook, sweat running down his face and his locs tied back with a strip of cloth that had probably been somebody's sleeve once. He'd been up all night on Camille's couch with a girl asleep on his shoulder. He hadn't slept a minute of it, and he'd walked out of the building that morning looking like a man who'd been given something.

Silas had watched him go down the stairs at first bell to buy bread from the stall on the corner and come back up them with two kinds, because he couldn't remember which one she'd liked.

"You're dropping your left," Silas said.

"I'm not dropping nothing."

"You're dropping it every time you throw the hook."

Remy threw the hook. His left came down. He threw it again, and it came down again, and he stopped and stood there breathing hard with both gloves resting on the bag.

"You gon' just sit there and coach me all day?"

"I can't do nothing else."

"You could go home."

"Ma's at my house."

"Your ma's at your house *every* day."

"Today she's mad about it."

Remy laughed into the bag. Then he hit it a few more times, not as hard, and stopped again, and leaned his forehead against the canvas and stayed like that for a while.

Silas waited. He'd known Remy his whole life. When Remy got quiet, you let him.

"I like her," Remy said into the bag.

Silas didn't say anything.

"I know." Remy pushed off the bag and turned around, pulling at the laces of his gloves with his teeth. "I *know*, Si. Don't look at me like that."

"I'm not looking at you like anything."

"You're looking at me like your mama looks at a lock somebody else installed." He got one glove off and threw it on the canvas. "She's leaving. I know she's leaving. She's—" He stopped. He looked around the empty ring, at the cutmen's table in the corner with nobody at it, at the high windows full of grey light. He lowered his voice anyway. "She's who she is. She's going back up to wherever that is the second Rae finds her a door. I know all that."

"Okay."

"And I like her anyway."

"Okay."

Remy got the other glove off and sat down on the canvas with his back against the bag and his elbows on his knees. He looked at his hands. The back of the left one was still blistered from Blue Circle, the skin gone shiny and tight where the heat had gotten it.

"Last night," he said, "before she fell asleep. She was talking. The blackroot had her talking, and I asked her what she used to do when she couldn't sleep, back home. When she was little. And she said she used to lie there and pretend she was somebody else. A girl with a different name. A girl who wasn't anybody's daughter and wasn't anybody's anything, who could just go wherever she wanted and nobody would care." Remy rubbed his thumb along the blister. "Mira. That's the name she made up. Mira."

"Mira," Silas said.

"She said nobody's ever called her that. Not once. Not her mama, not anybody. It was just hers." Remy looked up at him. "I called her that this morning. When she woke up. I didn't even think about it. I said, *Mira, you want tea,* and she looked at me like I'd—"

He stopped.

"Like you'd what?" Silas asked.

"I don't know." Remy looked back down at his hands. "Like I'd found her."

Silas sat on the rope and didn't say anything for a while. He thought about Melena on his mother's couch with the letter in her lap, saying *I'm the Imperial Princess of Remnia* in the same flat voice she'd used to tell him not to touch her. He thought about everything that name carried, every room it would open and every door it would close and every person in Shadowmere who'd sell her for it. Then he thought about a girl lying awake somewhere he'd never be able to picture, giving herself a name nobody else got to use.

He understood, a little, why Remy had looked like a man given something.

"Be careful with that," Silas said.

"With what?"

"With her." He nodded at Remy's hands. "With the name."

Remy looked at him a long time. Then he nodded, slowly, and picked his gloves up off the canvas.

"Yeah," he said. "I know."

---

Lorraine called while Remy was in the washroom.

"Your father wants you."

"I'm suspended."

"I know you're suspended. I'm the one who struck the ledger." She didn't sound sorry about it. She didn't sound anything. "It's not about routes. It's about godchildren. He said to tell you that, specifically. And bring Remy if he's with you, because he's going to hear about it from his mama by supper anyway."

Silas looked at the phone after she hung up.

*Godchildren.*

---

Dante was standing in the map room when they came in, and the map was lit.

Silas had always liked the map room best when it was lit. In the daytime, with the lamps off, it was just a table under glass with threads and pins, a thing you had to lean over and squint at. But when Dante woke the board, the threads under the glass glowed, every route the Mercers tracked laid out in thin lines of light across the whole long table, Shadowmere and Ashvein and the tiers above them, pale gold for open, blue for watched, red for closed. It looked like the veins in the back of a hand. Silas had stood over it when he was nine, the first time Dante let him in, and thought it was the most beautiful thing he'd ever seen.

Dante didn't turn around when they came in. He was looking at something on the wall.

There was a narrow shelf there, beside the map-room door, that Silas had walked past a thousand times. Two strips hung on it, side by side, small and old and worn soft at the edges. Thread wound through each one. Silas had always known what they were, the way he'd always known what the route marks by the front door were. Dante's godchildren. He'd stood up for them both in Ashvein a long time ago, before Silas could remember, and he'd brought their names back down with him, and he'd hung them where he'd see them every time he went into this room.

Silas had never seen his father look at them. He was looking at them now.

"Micah's warmed up," Dante said, without turning around. "About an hour ago."

Silas came and stood beside him. He could see it. The left strip, the one with MICAH stitched along the top in thread gone grey with age, had a faint glow to it, like a coal under ash. Not bright. But there.

"What's that mean?"

"Means he's thinking about calling." Dante's voice was very quiet. "Means he's sitting somewhere in Sixth Ward with his name in his mouth and mine right behind it, deciding whether it's bad enough." He looked at the strip a moment longer. "He won't. Not today. He's a proud boy, and his sister would skin him. But he's thinking about it."

"Sixth Ward," Silas said.

Dante finally turned his head and looked at him.

"Synod audited the whole ward this morning. Every house. Started at the east gate before first bell and worked their way in, with Shrouds on the lanes and gold on the doors." He said it plainly, the way he'd read a route report. "The Wraithmoor house got read at the wall. Micah's Ironcross sponsorship was stamped void in front of his family. A courier recorded entering Blue Circle was recorded as belonging to that house, and has been ordered to the ward hall at third bell to explain herself."

Silas felt it go through him slowly, the way cold water went through a coat.

*Herself.*

He thought about the courier at Ora's table with red dust on her heels and her face going still when she saw him. He thought about a woman at a little table on the Ironcross line saying *I told Rae it was in my house*, and her hand closing over the coin, and *I watched you not break a man's arm yesterday, you ain't gone break mine.*

He looked at the second strip.

It was the same age as Micah's. Same shelf, same worn edges, same grey thread. ASHA stitched along the top.

It was cold. Not dark, not dead. Just cold, and still, like a coal somebody had stopped breathing on.

"That one," Silas said.

"That one hasn't warmed in a long time." Dante looked at it. "Months, maybe. I noticed in the spring. I thought she'd just stopped thinking about me." His jaw moved once. "Children do."

"That's the courier."

Dante didn't answer. He didn't have to.

"You knew," Silas said. "You knew yesterday. When Rae called. *Your godson's sister.* You knew whose house that coin came out of."

"I knew Rae's goddaughter brought a coin to Rae and wouldn't say where it came from." Dante turned back to the map. "I could guess. I didn't know."

"You could've told me."

"I told you to stay on your side of the line." Dante put both hands on the edge of the map table and leaned over the glass. "And I told you to keep your hands off her."

Behind Silas, in the doorway, Remy made a very small noise and turned it into a cough.

Silas didn't turn around.

"There's more," Dante said. "Danika Wraithmoor filed for a private watch on her brother's record about an hour after the stamp came down. That's Ironcross. That's a woman who works in Ironcross records putting her own name on her brother's file so nobody can touch it without her seeing. That's a woman who knows something's coming and wants to be standing in the door when it does." He tapped the glass over Ironcross with one finger. "And the Synod searched Blue Circle this morning too. Sealed it. Took Ora's front stairs apart, what was left of them."

"And Blue Six?"

Dante looked at him.

"Jude's been on it since last night," Silas said. "He'd have told me if anybody came."

"Nobody came." Dante straightened. "The Synod put Shrouds on every lane in Sixth Ward and gold on every door, and they searched a burned Hush den down to the tile, and they didn't send one clerk to Letter Row." His voice went very flat. "They searched the hands carrying it. Not the box filling them."

Silas stood there and let that sit.

He thought about Jude in his stained shirt, saying *These are built to pass. Not down here. Up.* He thought about the theft mark under the gold on Jerrick's token. He thought about the fifteen-year-old with no name in a holding ward on warehouse row, and about who'd have the reach to steal a blank from a ward's own cabinet and the quicksilver to make a compound you couldn't buy.

"Somebody up there knows what's in that box," he said.

"Somebody up there," Dante agreed, "would very much prefer nobody else did."

---

The board went dark about ten minutes later.

It didn't flicker. It just went, all at once, the way the bathhouse windows had lit up all at once on Bath Row. One second the whole long table was glowing under the glass, every thread a vein of light, and the next second the light died out of all of it at the same time, and the map room was just a room again, grey and close, with a table in it.

Dante had his hand flat on the glass before Silas could open his mouth.

Under his palm, very faintly, the threads came back. Not all of them. A few. Shadowmere, mostly, the lines nearest the Mercer building flickering up pale and thin like a lamp being relit. The rest stayed dark.

"Blue Six," Dante said quietly.

Silas leaned over the glass. The spot on Letter Row where Jude had pinned his watch last night was black. Not red. Not closed. Just nothing, like the thread had been cut.

"Box went cold." Dante didn't sound surprised. "Whatever was feeding it stopped. Or got moved."

"Jude."

"I'll call Jude." Dante's hand didn't move off the glass. His eyes had gone farther up the table, to the north edge, where the lines ran up out of Shadowmere toward the tier above it.

Ashvein was dark.

Not part of it. All of it. The whole tier, every lane and every route and every stair Silas had ever seen glow on that table, gone black. Like somebody had laid a hand over it.

"Synod sealed the ward," Dante said. "That's what that is. They've closed every route in and out of Sixth Ward until the audit's done." He paused. "Every route they know about."

Silas didn't hear the rest. He'd already taken his phone out.

He didn't decide to. His hand just went to his coat, the way it went to his knife. He stood there in the grey map room with his father's hand on the glass and his oldest friend in the doorway, and he opened the thread.

He'd read it more times than he'd ever admit to anybody. He'd read it on the tram that morning, and in the ring, and twice in the washroom while Remy was in the next stall talking about bread. He'd read it last night, after it went quiet, sitting at his table in 3C with the radiator knocking, scrolling up from the bottom to the top and back down again like there might be something in it he'd missed the first eleven times.

*You make it home?*

*I'm sitting in my mama's kitchen.*

*That's not Ashvein. That's a disease.*

*Who did wrap it?*

*That's the saddest thing anybody's ever told me about a building.*

*My cousin keeps her bags packed.*

He'd stopped on that one last night. He stopped on it again now, with the board dark and Ashvein dark and his father's voice going on somewhere beside him about seals and audits. *My cousin keeps her bags packed. Has for two years.* He'd read it last night and thought it was the realest thing anybody had said to him in a long time. He read it now and saw a courier at Ora's table with a structured tote, and a cold strip on a shelf, and a woman sitting in her mama's kitchen at midnight telling a stranger the one thing she hadn't told anybody.

*Goodnight, Wraithmoor.*

*Can I ask you*

That was the last one. Half a question with nothing at the end of it. It had come in after midnight, and it had sat there on his screen all night, and he'd looked at it and looked at it and hadn't answered, because she was asleep, and because he didn't know what she'd been about to ask, and because some part of him had liked not knowing. Had liked the idea that tomorrow she'd wake up and finish it.

She hadn't finished it.

"Who got you checking your phone like that?"

Silas looked up.

Remy was leaning in the doorway with his arms crossed and an expression on his face that Silas had seen maybe four times in their whole lives, and every one of those times, Silas had wanted to hit him.

"Nobody."

"Nobody," Remy said. "Okay. Nobody's got you scrolling up and down like you're reading scripture." He tipped his head. "*Somebody* who heard what the runners said behind a lye vat on Bath Row. *Somebody* you trust. That somebody?"

Silas didn't say anything.

Remy's face did something slow and delighted. "Oh, that's *her*. That's the salt girl. That's Micah's—" He stopped. His eyes went to the shelf by the door, to the two strips, one warm and one cold. His face changed. "Oh," he said, differently.

Dante said nothing at all. But he'd turned his head, and he was looking at Silas, and Silas knew that look too. He'd seen it on his father's face once before, years ago, the first night he'd come home from this same practice ring at fifteen with his knuckles split open and a grin on his face he couldn't get rid of.

He put the phone face down on the map table.

The board lit.

Not all of it. One thread. Up near the top of the dark, where Ashvein should've been, where the lines ran down out of the sealed tier toward Shadowmere, a single short line flared under the glass, bright and hard and very red.

Dante looked at it. His hand went still on the glass.

"Halflight," he said.

Silas knew it before his father said it. He'd sat at a table on that line yesterday morning. He'd watched a woman walk away from him toward that gate and stop three steps out and turn around and give him her name.

The red line pulsed under the glass, slow, like a heartbeat.

"That's the Ashvein gate," Dante said. "That's somebody at the Ashvein gate trying to get through a Synod seal." His voice had gone very careful. "Silas."

Silas had already picked the phone back up.

He didn't think about what to say. He'd been thinking about it for a day and a half without knowing he was doing it, every time he scrolled up and down that thread looking for something he'd missed. He opened the thread, and he typed it, and he sent it before Dante could finish whatever he was about to say.

*Tell me you good.*

It went. It landed on his screen right under hers, right under *Can I ask you*, so that the two of them sat there together, her half a question and his whole one, one on top of the other, with nothing after either of them.

The red line pulsed under the glass.

The thread stayed quiet.
