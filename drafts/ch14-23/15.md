# CHAPTER 15: ISOLA

Micah didn't cry. That was the part Isola would remember longest.

He sat on the couch after the reader left with his elbows on his knees and his hands hanging between them, and he looked at the floor, and he didn't say anything at all. Not about Danika. Not about the thirty days. Not about the three work chits and the two letters from his foreman and the fourteen Veilmarks that had gone to Ironcross in a sealed case and come back as a word stamped in a ledger. He just sat there. Selah stood behind him with one cold hand on the back of his neck, and every so often she moved her thumb, and he let her.

Tevin was standing at the window with both hands flat against the whisperglass. He hadn't said a word either. He didn't need to. Isola could see the whole thing building in him from across the room, the jaw and the shoulders and the way he kept looking at the door, and she knew that the second the gold was out of the lane, he was going to go out looking for somebody to blame, and there was nobody to find.

Omari stood in the middle of the room exactly where he'd stood through the reading. He hadn't moved. His keys were still on his belt. His watch was still on his wrist, losing its eleven minutes. He looked at the wall where four generations of Wraithmoors hung in thread and ink and metal, and he looked at it for a long time, and then he went to it and laid his hand flat against Micah's strip.

Isola watched it warm under his palm. She watched her father close his eyes.

Then she went to find Asha.

---

Asha was in the kitchen, and she had her coat on.

She was standing at the stove where Isola had stood an hour ago, with her tote over her shoulder and her boots laced, and she was looking at the little iron door at the back of the ash chute. She didn't turn around when Isola came in. She'd known Isola would come.

"Third bell," Isola said.

"I heard him."

"You going?"

Asha didn't answer.

"The ward hall," Isola said. "Third bell. To explain your business at Blue Circle. You going, or you just gon' stand there looking at the stove like the coin's gon' climb back up out of it and tell you what to do?"

"I'm not going to the ward hall."

"Then where you going?"

Asha turned around.

Her face was very calm. It was the same calm she'd had standing at the front window with the reader saying her name, the same calm she'd had smiling at the vent. Isola had thought, watching her then, that it was the calm of somebody who'd given up. Standing in the kitchen now, close enough to see her cousin's eyes, she understood it was the other thing. It was the calm of somebody who'd already decided.

"I have to finish it," Asha said.

"Finish *what*?"

"The balance. The strip. Six deliveries, a hundred and eighty Veils, and the package burned, Isola. You heard the runners. They charge it to the den, and the den charges it to the courier." She laughed, not warm. "The courier's me. If I don't make it right, it doesn't just stay with me. It goes to whoever I'm registered to. You know who I'm registered to? You know whose name is on my strip, right under mine?"

Isola didn't.

"This house," Asha said. "Omari Kade Wraithmoor. Household of record."

The kitchen went very quiet.

"So I'm going to finish it," Asha said. "I'm going to go get whatever they want, and carry it wherever they want, and close the balance, and then it's done, and my name clears, and nobody in this house ever has to hear the word Blue Circle again."

"Your name clears," Isola said. "Your name's *already* clearing, Asha. I felt it. I felt it go this morning, in front of a Synod reader, and I held it on the wall with my own hands under the table while he looked at it, so he wouldn't see what you're doing to it."

Asha's face moved. Just for a second.

"You did that?"

"Somebody had to."

"Nobody asked you to."

"Nobody ever *asks* me to!" It came out of Isola too loud. She heard it bounce off the tile. She didn't care. "Nobody in this house ever asks me to do anything. You all just leave it lying where I'll trip over it. Micah's packet. Tevin's mouth. Your *bags*. Two years, Asha. Two years you've had those bags packed against that wall, and I've walked past them every day, and I've never once asked, because I thought if I didn't ask, you'd stay. And now I'm holding your name on a wall with my hands under a table so a man from Crownlight won't see it trying to leave."

"So let it leave."

Isola stared at her.

"Let it," Asha said. She came a step closer. Her voice had gone low and fast. "Let it go, Isola. It's my name. It's mine. Not yours. You don't get to hold it for me just because you're scared of what happens if you don't."

"I'm not scared."

"You're terrified. You've been terrified since you were twelve years old." Asha's eyes were wet now and she didn't seem to notice. "And I'm sorry. I am. But I can't be one more thing you hold."

She went for the vent.

Isola should have seen it. She'd seen Asha look at it that morning, standing at the front window with that smile on her face. She'd seen the iron grating in the wall by the kitchen floor every day of her life, the old vent that went down through the house and under the street into the trench routes that ran beneath all of Sixth Ward. Every child in this family had been told a hundred times never to go near it. Asha had been told the same as all of them.

Asha was already on her knees beside it. She had the grating out of the wall in one pull, like she'd loosened the bolts weeks ago. She had her tote off her shoulder and through the opening, and then her legs, and then she looked back up at Isola once, just once, from the dark.

"Don't follow me," she said, and was gone.

---

Isola followed her.

She didn't think about it. She was on the floor and into the vent before she'd finished hearing Asha say not to, her skirt catching on the edge of the iron, her elbows scraping the brick. The vent ran down at an angle, steep and narrow and black, slick with a century of coal smoke, and Isola went down it on her back with her heels braced and her hands out against the walls, sliding. Somewhere above her, through the brick, she heard Selah say her name.

She didn't stop.

The vent dropped her into the trench.

She landed on her feet, mostly, in a long stone channel just tall enough to stand up in, with water running ankle-deep along the bottom and old miners' lamps fixed to the walls at intervals, most of them dead, a few of them burning low and blue. She knew the trenches. Every child in Sixth Ward did, whether they'd been told to stay out of them or not. They ran under all the old blocks, cut a hundred years ago to carry water and coal and miners between the shafts, and when the shafts closed, the trenches stayed, and the people who needed to get places without being seen learned how to use them.

Asha was maybe forty feet ahead, walking fast through the water with her tote held up against her chest.

"*Asha.*"

Her cousin didn't turn around.

Isola went after her. The water was cold enough to make her shins ache. She caught up at the place where the trench widened into an old miners' station, a low room cut into the rock with stone benches along the walls and a row of iron lockers bolted into the far side, rusted, numbered in white paint gone grey. Some of them hung open. Most were shut.

Asha stopped in front of one of the shut ones and took a key out of her coat.

114.

She opened it. Inside was a single envelope, black, the same stiff paper as the route strip. Asha took it out and broke the seal and read it, standing in the blue light of a dying lamp with the water running around her boots, and Isola watched her cousin's face go grey.

"What is it."

Asha didn't answer. Isola took it out of her hands.

It was short. Printed, not written, in the same white ink as the strip. *Replacement order.* Then a line of route marks Isola couldn't read. Then, plain as anything: *12 coins. Blue Six. Before final bell.* And beneath that, smaller:

*Package lost under courier's carriage. Replacement owed by courier. On failure, liability transfers to courier's household of record.*

Isola read it twice. Then she looked up.

"Twelve coins," she said. "Where are you supposed to get twelve coins?"

Asha took the paper back and folded it, very carefully, and put it in her coat. "Same place I get all of them."

"Where?"

"You don't want to know."

"I want to know more than I've ever wanted anything in my *life*—"

"No, you don't." Asha turned around. Her face was wet, and her voice was steady, and Isola had never seen her look so much like Selah. "You don't want to know. You want to fix it. That's different. That's always been different with you. You don't want to know where the coins come from. You want to know where they come from so you can go stand in front of it and take the hit for me. And then come home and wash your hands at the sink and not tell anybody, and carry it around for the rest of your life, and every time you look at me, I'll see it."

"That's not—"

"You love us needing you," Asha said. "That's what it is. You love it. You'd rather hold every one of us up till your arms break than let any of us fall and find out we can get up on our own." She shook her head. "I'm not falling, Isola. I'm *walking*. There's a difference."

Isola opened her mouth.

The sound went out.

---

She didn't understand it at first. The water was still running. She could see it running, sliding cold and black over the toes of her boots. She could see Asha's mouth, still open on whatever she'd been about to say next. But the sound of the water was gone, and the drip from the trench ceiling was gone, and the low hum of the dying lamps was gone, all at once, like somebody had put a hand over the world.

She'd heard about it her whole life. She'd seen it that morning through the front window, a dog barking with no bark. She'd never been inside it.

Asha's face had gone white.

The Shroud came down the trench from the direction they'd come.

It didn't walk the way a person walked. It didn't splash. It moved through the ankle-deep water without disturbing it, a tall grey shape veiled from crown to collar, and the water closed behind it without a ripple. Where its face should've been, the cloth lay against something that wasn't a face. Pale shapes showed through it. Strips. Narrow strips layered one over the next, the way the name chains hung on the wall at home, bone-white, dozens of them, maybe hundreds.

It had something in its hand.

A strip. Just one. Held out in front of it between two long grey fingers, the way you'd hold out a ticket to a clerk. Isola could see writing on it, small and close.

ASHA NOELLE WRAITHMOOR.

The last letters were black.

Not written black. Going black, while she watched. The ink at the end of the strip, the R and the O and the M, was spreading, soaking into the paper the way water soaked into salt, darkening it, and as Isola watched, the black reached the H, and then the T, and started up into the I.

Beside her, Asha made a sound with no sound in it. Her hand went to her own throat.

Isola knew what she was looking at. She didn't know how she knew. Nobody had ever told her what a Shroud did with a name, or what it meant when one came walking through Silence holding yours. But she'd held that name on the wall that morning with her hands under a table, and she'd felt it go slack two nights ago under her fingertips in the dark, and she knew the shape of a Wraithmoor name the way she knew the shape of her own hand. The Shroud was taking it. Letter by letter. From the end.

When it reached the front, there wouldn't be anything left of Asha to hold.

Isola reached out and took the strip.

She didn't grab it. She didn't think about it long enough to grab. She just reached past her cousin into the Silence and closed her hand around the end of the strip, the end that wasn't black yet, the A and the S and the H, and pulled.

It came out of the Shroud's fingers.

She held it against her chest with both hands, and she said her cousin's name.

There was no sound. She said it anyway. She said it the way Omari had taught them, low and even, each part set down carefully before the next, the cadence her father used for the wall and nowhere else. She couldn't hear herself. She could feel it, in her mouth and her chest and her hands, the rhythm of it, the old shape. *Asha. Noelle. Wraithmoor.* The words weren't anything. Saying them right was everything.

The black stopped.

It stopped in the middle of the I, half in and half out, a hard line across the letter like a door shut on a hand. Isola felt it stop. She felt the name catch in her fists the way it had caught under the table that morning, slow and stubborn and heavy, a full bucket coming up out of a cistern. She held it.

The Shroud turned its head and looked at her.

It hadn't looked at her before. She understood that now. It had been looking at Asha, or at the name, or at whatever a Shroud looked at, and Isola had been nothing, a shape in the trench. Now it turned the not-face toward her slowly, all those pale strips shifting under the veil, and she felt it see her, and she felt it understand what she'd done.

She'd taken its work out of its hands.

It reached for her.

Not with its hand. With something else. Isola felt it before she saw it, a cold that wasn't the water, wrapping around her right ankle below the hem of her skirt. She looked down. There was nothing there. There was nothing there, and then there was, thin black lines threading up out of the water and into her skin like ink into paper, like the black going into her cousin's name.

It didn't hurt at first. That was the worst part. It felt like cold. Like stepping into a cistern in winter. Three black branches climbed the outside of her ankle, slow, and she watched them go, and she couldn't make her foot move. One of them turned and started up the back of her calf toward her knee. Another one sank inward, toward the bone, and that one she felt, deep and terrible, like a nail being driven into her from the inside.

Isola held on to the strip.

She didn't let go. She thought, very clearly, the way you think things when everything else has gone quiet: *this is the price.* This was what holding somebody's name cost when something else wanted it. She'd taken it. Now it was hers to carry, and the thing that had wanted it was going to take its payment out of her instead.

Fine. Let it.

She looked at Asha.

Her cousin was standing pressed against the lockers with her hands over her mouth and her eyes enormous, and she was looking at Isola's leg, at the black climbing it, and she was saying something over and over with no sound at all. Isola knew what it was. She'd heard Asha say it on storm nights when they were small, curled up in the same bed with the blue-white flashes crawling across the window.

*No. No. No.*

Isola reached out with one hand, the other still holding the strip against her chest, and took her cousin's wrist. She pulled her close. She put her mouth right up against Asha's ear, though there was no sound to carry anything, and she said it anyway, slow, so Asha could feel the shape of the words against her skin.

*Go to Godmother.*

Asha shook her head.

*Take the cloth route. Go to Rae. Go now.*

Asha shook her head harder.

Isola held up the strip. She held it up between them, black to the middle of the I, and let her cousin see it. Then she pressed it flat against her own chest, under her coat, against her heart, where black three already sat warm beside the cord of Rae's token.

*I got what it wants,* she said, slow, against Asha's ear. *It's gon' follow me. Not you. Go.*

She let go of Asha's wrist and pushed her, hard, toward the far end of the miners' station, where a side trench went off into the dark with a strip of black funeral cloth tied around the lamp bracket at its mouth. Isola had seen Rae's cloth at a dozen turns in a dozen trenches growing up and never once followed it. She'd never needed to.

Asha went.

She stumbled, and looked back, and went, the long coat dragging in the water, the tote forgotten on the floor by the lockers. She went down the cloth route into the dark, and the dark took her, and Isola stood in the miners' station alone with the Shroud.

It hadn't moved. It didn't need to. It stood there in the water, looking at her, and the cold in her leg climbed another inch.

Isola turned and ran.

---

She didn't know she was going to Halflight until she was on the stairs.

The trench came up under Sixth Ward at the old shaft head behind the ward hall, and she came up the ladder there with her leg gone stiff and cold from the ankle to the knee, and she came out into a lane full of gold. The Synod lamps were still moving door to door at the east end. The Shrouds in the lanes stood where they'd stood all morning, each one in its circle of no-sound. Nobody looked at her. Nobody ever looked at anything a Shroud might want.

Behind her, she didn't have to look to know it, the one from the trench came up the ladder and into the lane.

She didn't run toward home. She couldn't bring this home. She ran the other way, down through the back of the ward, past the shrine at the second landing, toward the only door she could think of that went anywhere else.

The half staircase down to Halflight was steep and narrow and old, and her right leg wouldn't bend at the knee anymore. She took the stairs one at a time with her hand on the wall and her left foot doing all the work, and with every step, the cold climbed. She could feel it behind her knee now. She could feel the one going toward the bone like a second spine.

The Ashvein gate was shut.

She'd known it would be. Dante's map, if Dante's map could see anything, would've shown her what she already knew, that the Synod had sealed the whole ward, every way in and out. The gate stood at the bottom of the stairs in its iron frame, closed, and across the bars, laid over the iron like a sheet of frost, there was writing. White writing, glowing faintly in the dim. A prayer. *The Veil protects.* Over and over, in a hand so small and close it looked like lace.

Isola put her hand on the bars anyway. She pulled. The prayer brightened under her fingers and went hot, and somewhere far away inside the station, something rang, a long, flat alarm like a bell with a crack in it.

Through the bars, down the long low hall of Halflight, past the dim tile and the dull brass line in the floor where yesterday morning she'd sat at a little table with her knees an inch from his, she could see the other gate. The Shadowmere gate. Shut. Dark.

Nobody there.

Behind her, at the top of the half staircase, the Silence came down the steps.

Her phone buzzed against her heart.

She almost didn't feel it. She was standing at a sealed gate with a Shroud on the stairs above her and black climbing her leg and her cousin's name in her fist, and her phone buzzed, once, under her coat, against the tag.

She took it out.

*Tell me you good.*

It sat there on the screen under her own last message, the one she didn't remember sending. *Can I ask you.* Half a question. She'd fallen asleep in the middle of it last night with her thumb on the glass, and he'd let it sit there all night and all morning without answering, and now here he was, right under it, asking her a whole one.

She didn't think *he's the only one left*.

She didn't think anything that sensible. She didn't think about Dante Mercer's map or the Synod seal or what it would cost a Shadowmere man to open an Ashvein gate. She didn't think about the fact that she'd known him three days. She thought one thing, standing at the bottom of the stairs with the Silence coming down behind her, and it was in his voice, low and unbothered, across a table on the Ironcross line.

*Use the same route.*

She pressed black three to the back of the phone, and she called him.

It rang once. Not even all the way.

"Silas," she said.

She heard herself say it. She was still outside the Silence, just barely, and she heard her own voice come out of her raw and too loud, his name, and she had time to think, distantly, that she'd never said it to him before. Not once. Not to his face. She'd said it to her ceiling in the dark and to Micah at the sink and across a table at Halflight to watch it land, but she'd never said it *to* him, like this, like his name was a door.

She heard his breath go out on the other end.

"Where are you," he said.

"Halflight." Her voice cracked. She held on to the bars with one hand and the phone with the other and the strip against her heart under her coat. "*Open Halflight.*"

"I'm coming." He said it fast and low and flat, like a man already moving. "Isola. Hold on. I'm—"

The Silence reached her.

It took his voice first. She was looking at the phone, at his name on the screen and the seconds counting up under it, and his voice was in her ear, and then it wasn't. The call didn't drop. The seconds kept counting. He was still there, still talking, she knew he was still talking, and she couldn't hear a word of it. Then it took the sound of her own breathing, and the hum of the prayer on the bars, and the cracked bell still ringing somewhere deep inside the station, and the world went soft and grey and empty as the inside of a veil.

She turned around.

The Shroud was on the bottom step. It stood there looking at her. All those pale strips shifting behind the cloth where its face should've been.

Isola put her back against the gate. She slid down it, because her right leg wouldn't hold her up anymore, until she was sitting on the cold stone with the bars against her spine and the white prayer glowing over her shoulder. She took the strip out of her coat and held it in both fists in her lap where she could see it. Black to the middle of the I.

She started saying her cousin's name again.

She couldn't hear it. It didn't matter. She'd said it to a wall with her hands under a table, and she'd said it to a Shroud in a trench, and she was going to say it here, on the floor, as many times as it took, for as long as she had. *Asha. Noelle. Wraithmoor.* The cadence Omari had taught them. Each part set down carefully before the next.

The cold climbed past her knee.

She held on.

She thought about Selah humming at the stove. She thought about Micah on the couch with his hands hanging between his knees, and Tevin's palms flat on the window, and her father's hand on the wall with his eyes closed. She thought about Asha going down the cloth route into the dark with her coat dragging in the water, and she hoped her cousin was running, and she hoped she'd still be angry enough at Isola tomorrow to come home and say so.

The Shroud came off the step toward her.

She thought, very clearly, the way you think things when the world has gone quiet: if this was it, his was the last voice.

*Hold on. I'm—*

She hadn't heard the end of it. She found she didn't need to.

She held on.
