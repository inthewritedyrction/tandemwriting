# CHAPTER 16: SILAS

For nineteen minutes, Silas did exactly what his father told him.

"You will not touch that gate."

Dante said it into the phone while Silas was still on the Mercer stairs, taking them two at a time with his bad arm clamped against his ribs and his good hand on the rail. He said it again while Silas was on the tram, standing because he couldn't sit, watching the lamps of Shadowmere go by too slow through the rain-streaked glass. He said it a third time when Silas came off the tram at the old transfer stop and ran the last two streets to the Halflight stairs, and his voice hadn't changed at all, not once, low and even and very close, like he was standing right behind Silas's shoulder.

"You will go to the Shadowmere door, and you will stand there, and you will not touch it. That is a Synod seal on a Synod gate on an Ironcross station in a ward under audit. You put one finger on that iron, and it's not a suspension, son. It's High Heresy. It's the square." A breath. "I'm calling Ironcross. I'm calling everybody I've got. You stand there and you let me work."

"She's—"

"I heard what she said. I heard her say your name before she said anything else." Something moved under his father's voice, very deep, and was gone. "Stand there. Let me work."

So Silas stood there.

The Shadowmere door to Halflight was at the top of a narrow stone stair that came up out of the transfer stop, an iron gate in an iron frame, and across the bars, laid over the metal like frost, there was writing. The same writing Dante's map had shown him as a single red thread. White. Faint. A prayer in a hand so small and close it looked like lace. *The Veil protects. The Veil protects. The Veil protects.* It went on and on, crossing and recrossing the iron, and when Silas came up the stairs and stopped in front of it, close enough to feel the cold coming off it, it brightened a little, like it knew he was there.

The Ironcross booth beside the gate was empty. The clerk's stool was pushed back. His door-sized ledger lay open on the counter, a pen across it, like he'd gotten up in the middle of a word and walked away.

Silas looked through the bars.

Halflight ran away from him, long and low and dim, the curved grey tile of the ceiling, the pale scar in the floor where the tracks had been. Halfway down, the brass line. The little table sat on it where he'd left it yesterday morning, two chairs, one on each side, pushed back a little from when they'd stood up. At the far end, the Ashvein gate. Shut. Glowing faintly white across its bars the same as this one.

And on the other side of it, on the floor, sitting with her back against the iron, Isola.

He could see her clearly enough. That was the worst of it. The station was long, but it was straight, and the lamps were still burning, and he could see her sitting there on the bottom step of the Ashvein stairs with her back against the gate and her head bowed over something she was holding in her lap. Her right leg was out in front of her on the stone, stiff, wrong. Her coat had fallen open.

He couldn't hear anything from that end. He knew why. He could see it, the way you could see heat coming off a road. The far end of the station had a stillness to it that didn't belong to any room, a weight in the air, and inside it, past the bars, standing on the step above her, was the Shroud.

It wasn't doing anything. It was just standing there, looking down at her.

Silas took out his phone. The call was still open. The seconds were still counting up under her name. He put it to his ear.

Nothing. Not her breathing. Not the alarm he'd heard ringing in the background when she said *Open Halflight*. Not anything. The Silence had taken all of it, and the line was still open into nothing, and he stood there holding it to his ear anyway.

"Dante."

He'd called him back without deciding to. His father picked up on the first ring.

"I'm on with Ironcross."

"She's right there. I can see her."

"I know."

"There's a Shroud on her."

"I *know*, Silas." His father's voice cracked, just a little, just once, and came back together. "Ironcross says the seal's Synod. They can't lift it without a writ. I've got Lorraine on the writ office. I've got a man in the ward hall I can call. Give me time."

"How much time."

"Give me time."

He gave him time.

---

He counted it. He couldn't help it. He'd been counting things since he was a boy, rounds and breaths and steps and the time between a man's tells, and he stood at the Shadowmere gate with his good hand flat against his thigh so it wouldn't go to the iron, and he counted.

One minute. She hadn't moved. Her head was still bowed over whatever was in her lap.

Two. He called Rhea. It rang and rang and went to nothing. He'd known it would. A year, and the only thing she'd ever answered was *You alive?*, and only on fight nights.

Three. He called Jude. Jude picked up and said "Si" and then didn't say anything else, and Silas could hear him breathing hard, like he'd been running, and then he said "I'm on Letter Row, I can't—the box went cold, I'm—" and Silas said "Stay there," and hung up.

Four. Five. He looked at her leg.

He hadn't wanted to look at it. He'd been looking at her face, at her bowed head, at her hands. He made himself look now, because looking was the only thing he could do, and he'd been raised to know what he was looking at.

Her skirt had ridden up when she slid down the gate. Her right leg was bare from the boot to above the knee, and the skin of it was wrong. Even from this far, even through two sets of bars and a long dim hall, he could see it. Black lines. Thin and branching, like cracks in glass, like veins drawn in ink, coming up out of the top of her boot and climbing.

Silas knew what it was. Camille had shown him one when he was eleven, on a body laid out on her board in the back room, a man the Synod had wanted gone and had decided to take slow. She'd made him look at it for a long time. She'd made him name every part of it.

A death tether. Deathwork. A Shroud's claim, laid into living flesh and climbing toward whatever part of a body it needed to reach to finish.

He made himself read it, the way she'd taught him. Three branches above the ankle, running up the outside of the leg. One of them had turned and climbed the back of her calf and was sitting now just behind the knee. And one, the thickest, wasn't climbing at all. It was going in. Sinking. Toward the bone.

*When it reaches the hip,* Camille had said, her hand flat on the cold man's thigh, *that's it. That's the door. Everything after that is just waiting.*

Six. Seven. The one behind her knee moved. He watched it move.

He knew three ways to stop it. He'd known them since he was eleven. He stood at the gate with his hand against his leg and went through them again, the way he'd go through a fight he was about to lose, because going through them was better than standing there doing nothing.

You could take the leg. Above the black, before it reached the hip. Cut it off and burn the stump and hope you were faster than the claim. People lived through it. Some of them.

You could kill the Shroud. Put a blade through whatever was under that veil and hope its work died with it. Sometimes it did. Sometimes the tether kept climbing on its own, like a fire that didn't need the hand that lit it anymore.

Or you could open.

Eight. Nine.

He didn't let himself think about the third one. He thought about it anyway.

He knew what Graveshand was. He'd known since before he could read. Camille had bound the names into him herself, one at a time, on the inside of his left wrist and up his forearm and across his back, small and dark and close, the marks that people in Gutterlight took for decoration. *One for the family,* she'd said, with the needle in her hand and his arm across her knee. *One for the route. One for everything that came through and needed putting back.* They were death names. They were doors.

And she'd taught him the rule before she taught him to open a single one of them. He'd been six. She'd sat him down at the kitchen table across from Rhea and Jude and put her hands flat on the wood and said it to all three of them at once, slow, so they'd never forget it.

*Never call the dead unless you're prepared to answer what comes.*

Opening Graveshand didn't just let you touch death. It gave death a road back to you. Once you opened, every dead thing anywhere near knew exactly where you stood. And if you did what he'd be doing, if you put your hand on somebody else's deathwork and took it apart, that wasn't calling. That was shouting. Severance called louder than anything.

And you didn't know what happened when two death marks met inside a living body. Nobody knew. Camille had told him that too. You might pull the claim out. You might pull her out with it.

Ten. Eleven. Twelve.

"Dante."

"Writ office won't move." His father's voice was flat. "Lorraine's still on them. The man in the ward hall's not answering."

"The black's past her knee."

Silence on the line.

"Silas. If you touch that gate—"

"I know."

"You don't know. You think you know. You've never seen a man Silenced. I have." Something in Dante's voice went very low and very tired. "You open that gate, and it's not just you. It's Rhea and Jude, because they're your blood, and it's your mother, because it's her name on those marks, and it's me. They'll come for all of us. You hear me? *All* of us."

Thirteen. Fourteen.

On the floor on the other side of the far gate, Isola lifted her head.

She wasn't looking at him. She couldn't have seen him, not from that far, not through two gates and the Silence. She was looking down at what she held in her lap, both fists closed around it, and her lips were moving.

Silas watched her mouth. He couldn't hear a sound. He didn't need to. He could see the shape of it, slow and careful and the same, over and over. Three parts. A name. Each part set down before the next, like somebody laying stones.

She was saying somebody's name.

He didn't know whose. It wasn't his. It was longer than his. He could see the shape of it on her mouth, and he could see the thing in her fists, narrow and pale, like a strip of the paper the Synod wrote names on. She was holding it the way you'd hold a rope with somebody on the other end. She was sitting on the floor of an Ironcross station with the black past her knee and a Shroud on the step above her, and she was holding on to somebody.

Fifteen. Sixteen.

The black moved again. It was above the knee now, on the inside of her thigh, climbing. The one going toward the bone had stopped going down. It had started going up.

Seventeen.

He thought about Rae saying *godbinding rules apply*. He thought about Micah at the fence, yelling louder than anybody. He thought about Selah Wraithmoor, who he'd never met, and Omari Wraithmoor, who he'd never met, and a wall full of names in a house in Sixth Ward with one strip on it that was going to go cold if he stood here. He thought about Dante's strips on the shelf in the map room, Micah's warming. He thought about what it would mean, if the goddaughter of Mother Rae and the sister of Dante Mercer's godson died on an Ironcross floor while a Graves stood watching through the bars, and what Rae would do about it, and what the Wraithmoors would do about it, and what kind of war that would be.

That was the reason. He told himself that was the reason. Her dying was a bigger problem than her living. It was simple arithmetic. Anybody would see it.

Eighteen.

He thought about her saying his name before she said anything else.

Nineteen.

"I'm sorry," Silas said into the phone.

"Silas—"

He hung up.

---

He took his knife out with his left hand, because his right was busy.

He'd been holding it closed for three days, the burn Melena had left across his palm in the alley. Camille had wrapped it, and rewrapped it, and it had started to close under the wrap, a hard, half-healed seam across the middle of his hand. He unwound the bandage with his teeth. He put the point of the knife into the seam and opened it, one long clean cut, and the blood came up dark and fast.

He put his palm flat against the Synod gate.

The prayer screamed. There was no other word for it. The white writing under his hand flared so bright it hurt his eyes, and the cold of it went up his arm like a needle, and somewhere in the station, the cracked alarm started ringing again, louder, wrong.

Silas dragged his hand down the bars. Slowly. From as high as he could reach to the bottom of the frame, leaving a wide dark stripe of his own blood over all that white lace.

"Welcome me home," he said.

His mother had taught him the words. He'd never said them before.

Halflight went away.

It didn't vanish. It just stopped being the only thing there. The grey tile and the long hall and the brass line and the little table, all of it was still there, but it was thin now, like a picture painted on glass, and underneath it, through it, he could see what was really under the station.

Roads.

Old ones. Older than Halflight. Old transfer paths cut into the rock when the tiers were young, and narrower ones beneath those, rough and low, that he knew without being told were execution roads, the ways they used to walk people down to Gravebend when the Synod wanted them buried where nobody would find them. Funeral passages, sealed and forgotten and still there, running off into the dark in every direction like roots.

And the dead.

They'd been waiting. He understood that as soon as he saw them. They'd been down here in the roots of Halflight for a hundred years, the ones who'd died trying to climb. People who'd come up those stairs from below with no one to claim them and been turned back at the gate, and turned back again, and died in the transfer paths trying to find another way. People who'd been walked down the execution roads and never walked up. They were grey and thin and many, so many, and they turned toward him all at once the second his blood touched the iron, the way a crowd turns toward a door opening.

They reached for him.

He felt them on the marks. On every name Camille had ever bound into his skin, all at once, hands. Not grabbing. Hooking. Little cold fingers sliding in under each mark like you'd slide a finger under a loose stitch.

He had time for one clear thought before they got hold of him, and it was his mother's voice.

*Prepared to answer what comes.*

And then, from somewhere very close, closer than the dead, close as his own pulse:

*Si?*

Jude. In his blood. In the place under his breastbone where nothing should've been but bone.

*Silas, what did you do?*

And under that, fainter, farther, a voice he hadn't heard say anything but *You alive?* in a year:

*Oh, you stupid—*

Rhea.

He didn't have time for either of them. He went.

---

He crossed the dead roads under Halflight with the dead hanging off him like wet coats. It wasn't walking. It wasn't anything he had a word for. One second he was standing at the Shadowmere gate with his hand on the iron, and the next he was moving through the thin grey underneath of the station toward the far end, and the brass line passed under him like a seam in the dark, and then he was there.

The Shroud was standing over her.

Up close, under the veil, he could see what it was made of. Strips. Name strips, hundreds of them, layered one over the other where a face should have been, bone-white and close-written, every one of them a name the Synod had taken out of somebody's mouth. They shifted as it moved, rustling without sound.

And down from its hand, into her leg, the tether ran. He could see it now, really see it, the way he'd never been able to see anything before. A black line, thin as thread, running from the Shroud's grey fingers into the black lacework climbing up her thigh. Everything it had laid into her was on the end of that line.

He reached out with his bleeding hand and took hold of it.

It was cold. Colder than the gate. Colder than the body on Camille's board. He wrapped it once around his palm, then again, then again, the way you'd wrap a rope you didn't mean to let go of, so the black thread lay right in the open cut. His blood ran into it. It ran into his blood.

He read it. He made himself. Three branches above the ankle. One behind the knee, gone above it now. One into the bone. He knew what he was holding. He knew what it was doing. He knew where it was going.

The Shroud turned its head toward him.

Then he saw the mark.

It was on the strip across the forehead, the top one, the one that sat where a brow would be. A mark cut into the name written there. A Graves mark. He knew it like he knew his own hand, because he'd seen it on every old threshold his family had ever owned, cut into the stone above doors he'd walked through his whole life.

The short lower hook.

The one Dante wore. The one Dante cut, himself, into every old family door.

Silas stood there in the dead roads under Halflight with the Shroud's tether wrapped around his bleeding hand and his father's mark looking back at him from a face made of stolen names, and for one second, he couldn't think at all.

Then the dead pulled harder on his marks, and the black on her thigh moved another inch toward her hip, and he thought again.

"Mine," Silas said.

He said it to the Shroud. He said it to the dead hooked into him. He said it to the station, and the gate, and the prayer, and to whatever had cut his father's hook into that strip.

"She came through my gate. Her severance is mine."

Everything stopped.

The dead stopped pulling. He felt them go still on his marks, every one of them, a hundred cold hands holding very still under his skin. The Shroud stopped. Its head was half-turned toward him, and it didn't finish turning. The tether in his hand went taut and stayed taut.

It was Graves law. He'd known it since he could talk. *Mine* meant *mine until burial*. It meant no other hand collected this body. It meant whatever was trying to take her had to go through him first, and he didn't let it.

He pulled his hand back, the tether still wrapped around it, and drove his bleeding palm flat into the Graves mark on the Shroud's forehead.

The Shroud came apart.

It didn't fall. It came undone, all at once, every strip at once, the way a knot comes undone when you pull the right end. Hundreds of name strips, bone-white, flying loose into the dead roads like leaves off a tree in a storm. And every one of them was talking.

He heard them. Every name. Hundreds of voices, all at once, every person that thing had ever taken, saying their own names out loud for the first time in who knew how long. Some of them were screaming. Some of them were laughing. One of them, very close to his ear, was just saying *thank you, thank you, thank you* in a small, cracked voice, over and over.

The tether snapped.

He felt it go in his hand like a bone breaking, a sharp, clean crack, and the cold went out of it all at once, and the black thread lying in his palm turned to ash and blew away.

His blood was everywhere. It had run down his arm and off his fingers, and it hadn't stopped at the floor. It was on the gate. The Ashvein gate. It had soaked into the white writing and crawled through the lace of the prayer in thin dark lines, from one bar to the next to the next, and wherever it went, the prayer went dark.

The lock opened.

Then the next one. Then all of them. He heard them go, one after another, every lock in the gate and every lock in the station, a long rolling clatter of iron giving way, all the way back down the hall to the Shadowmere door where his handprint was still drying on the bars.

The Ashvein gate swung open.

And Silas stepped through it, out of Halflight, into Ashvein.

Over his head, the white writing on the gate frame rearranged itself. He watched it happen. The little close lace of *The Veil protects* came undone and wrote itself back in letters as tall as his hand, burning white against the dark stone of the stairwell.

UNAUTHORIZED TRANSFER.

And under it, larger:

HIGH HERESY.

---

She was on the floor at his feet.

He went down on his knees beside her. His knees hit the stone. He didn't feel it. He didn't feel much of anything, now that he thought about it. His right hand was very cold, colder than it should've been, and when he looked down at it, there was something wrong with the color of his fingers, a darkness under the skin of the first two knuckles that hadn't been there a minute ago. He didn't look at it long.

The Silence was gone. He could hear again. He could hear the alarm still ringing back in the station, flat and cracked, and somewhere above them up the half staircase, voices, shouting, boots. He could hear the dead, still hooked into his marks, murmuring now instead of pulling. He could hear Jude, very faint, under his breastbone, saying his name over and over.

He could hear her breathing. Barely. Thin and quick and shallow.

Her head had fallen back against the bars. Her eyes were closed. Her face was grey under the brown, and her lips were still moving, very slightly, still shaping the same three parts of the same name. She hadn't stopped. Even now. She was holding the strip against her chest with both fists, and he looked at it, and he could read it from here.

ASHA NOELLE WRAITHMOOR.

Black to the middle of the I. Not one letter more.

He looked at her leg.

The claim was gone. He could feel that it was gone the way you could feel a door had closed. Nothing was pulling on her anymore. Nothing was taking her. But the black was still there, all of it, the three branches and the one behind the knee and the one in the bone, dark and dead in her skin like the lines in burnt wood. It wasn't climbing. It wasn't doing anything. It was just there.

He'd removed the claim. Not the damage.

He thought he'd known that would happen. He'd known it the way you knew things before you saw them, which was not the same as knowing them at all.

"Isola," he said.

Her lips kept moving. Asha. Noelle. Wraithmoor.

"Isola. Can I—"

He stopped.

He'd been about to ask her. He'd been about to ask her if he could lift her, if he could touch her, if he could put his hands on her and carry her somewhere, because she'd sat across a table from him yesterday and closed her fist over a coin and said *Ask*, and he'd asked, and he'd meant to keep asking.

She couldn't answer. She was past answering. She was past hearing him at all. She was sitting on the floor of the Halflight stairs with her head against the bars, saying her cousin's name with the last of what she had, and the boots on the stairs above them were getting closer.

He didn't finish the question.

He got his good arm under her knees and his bad one under her back, and his shoulder screamed, and he lifted her anyway.
