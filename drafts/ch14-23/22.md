# CHAPTER 22: SILAS

The burial rail came up under Sixth Ward in a cinder pit.

Silas hadn't known that. He'd known the service line ran up into Ashvein. He'd known the funeral cars went up there empty and came back down full, and that Camille had used it more than once for things she never explained. He hadn't known where it stopped. When the car's doors opened onto a long, low cave of black slag and old ash, with a stone platform along one side and iron rungs set into the far wall going up into the dark, he lay on his trolley and looked at it and didn't understand where he was.

Isola did.

He felt her know. Her hand tightened on his where it held his palm against her ribs, and her heart under it jumped, and she turned her head for the first time since the car started moving and looked up at the rungs in the wall.

"That's ours," she said.

"Yours what?"

"Our pit. The cinder pit. Under the row." Her voice was very strange. "That's the ash chute. Up there. That's Daddy's stove."

Rhea had already climbed out. She stood on the platform looking up at the rungs with her inked hands on her hips. "It goes up into a house?"

"It goes up into *my* house."

Nobody said anything for a moment. Over their heads, through the rock, the seal bell kept tolling, slow and flat. Melena stood in the car doorway with Remy behind her, both of them looking up into the dark.

"Course it does," Rhea said finally, very quietly. "Course it does."

---

They couldn't get the trolleys up the rungs.

Remy climbed first. He went up the iron into the dark and was gone for a long time, and Silas lay there with Isola's hand on his and listened to the seal bell and counted. Then there was a scrape, and a clang, and a square of dim yellow light opened in the ceiling of the pit, and a man's voice came down through it, low and very hard.

"Who's down there."

Isola's whole body went tight under Silas's hand.

"Daddy," she said. Not loud. She didn't need to be loud. "Daddy, it's me."

The light in the ceiling went still.

What happened after that, Silas only got in pieces. He was flat on Camille's board on a funeral trolley at the bottom of a cinder pit with a dead hand and a stranger's breath tied to his, and everything happened above him or around him or too fast. A rope came down. Then another man came down it, a big man in work clothes with keys on his belt that rang when he moved, and he went straight past Silas like Silas wasn't there and crouched beside Isola's trolley and put his hands on her face.

"Baby girl."

"Daddy."

"What happened. What—" He'd seen the leg. Silas watched him see it. Watched a man's face go from fear to something else in less than a second, something with no bottom to it. "Who did this."

"A Shroud," Isola said. "In the trench. I was—Asha was—Daddy, she's at Godmother's, she's safe, I sent her—"

"*Who did this.*"

He wasn't looking at the leg anymore. He was looking at her hand. At where she was holding it, across the gap between the two trolleys. At the hand under hers, flat on her ribs. At the wrist that hand was attached to.

At the five black letters on the inside of it.

Omari Wraithmoor didn't need anybody to read him a name. He'd been reading names on walls since before Silas was born. He looked at his daughter's name, wet and black and shining on the wrist of a stranger from Shadowmere, and Silas watched him understand it. Not all of it. He didn't need all of it. He understood enough.

He looked up from the wrist, for the first time, at Silas's face.

"Graves," he said.

It wasn't a question.

---

They got them up the chute somehow.

Silas would never remember exactly how. Rope and Remy and Rhea's terms and a lot of cussing from a voice he didn't know yet, a young man's, loud, coming down through the hole from the kitchen above. They took Isola up first, strapped to a board, and Silas came up after her, hauled on another, so close behind that the rope that held hers brushed his face, because if the gap between them opened more than a foot, they both stopped breathing, and everybody had figured that out by now. He came up out of the ash chute behind a big iron stove into a kitchen he'd never seen, small and warm and smelling of smoke and roots and something sharp, like incense, with dried bundles of green hanging over the sink and little packets of salt wedged into the corners of every window.

They put Isola on the dining table. They put him on the floor beside it, on Camille's board.

He lay there. His right hand lay black on the floorboards beside him. His left arm was up on the edge of the table where Isola could reach it, and she'd reached it, and her fingers were around his, two of them, the way she'd held them on the stairs.

He looked up.

There were faces all around him. A tall young man with his locs retwisted neat in the front, standing in the kitchen doorway with his whole body leaning forward like a dog on a short rope. That was the cussing voice. Behind him, smaller, thinner, a kid Silas knew. He'd know that face anywhere, now that he saw it close, now that he wasn't trying to pick it out of a crowd at a fence. Micah, staring at him with his mouth open.

"*Silas?*"

And a woman, coming in from the hall. Older. Round face and heavy arms and watchful eyes, her locs wrapped in dark cloth, a long cream house dress, a charm at the base of her throat. She stopped in the door. Her eyes went to the table. To her daughter's leg. Silas felt the room go cold, really cold, the way it had gone cold in Dante's office when the seals answered, and he saw frost come up on the brass edge of the doorframe where her hand was resting.

Then Omari Wraithmoor came out of the ash chute behind the stove, and walked over to where Silas lay on the floor, and picked him up by the front of his shirt.

---

He didn't fight it. That was the first thing he decided, and he decided it before Omari's fist landed, and he held on to it the whole time.

He didn't fight when Omari hauled him up off the board, half up, enough to get at him, the gap between him and the table opening no more than six inches because Omari wasn't a stupid man and had seen what happened when it opened further. He didn't fight when the first one landed on the side of his face, on the swelling Jerrick had left, so hard his head went back against the leg of the table. He didn't fight the second one, or the third.

He'd been hit a lot in his life. He'd been hit by men who were trying to kill him and men who were trying to win money and men who just didn't like his face. He'd never been hit like this. Omari Wraithmoor didn't hit like a fighter. He hit like a father. Every punch had a whole life behind it, a baby girl and a first word and a scraped knee and a hundred nights waiting up for her to come home, and every one of them landed on Silas with all of that behind it, and every one of them was right.

He'd put his name on her. Without asking. Without a word. He'd brought her home on a funeral board with her leg ruined and her name on his skin. Any man would've done this. Silas would've done it.

"Daddy—" That was Micah. "Daddy, stop, that's *Silas*, that's my godbrother, he—"

"Then he should've known *better*."

Another one. Silas tasted blood. His lip, the old split, opening again. He let it.

"Omari." The woman in the doorway. Selah. Her voice was very low. "Omari, the tie. Look at his chest. Every time you hit him, look at her."

Silas didn't look. He knew what she meant. He could feel it. Every time Omari's fist landed, his breath went, and on the table, a half count later, he heard Isola's go too. A short hard catch. Like somebody had hit her.

Omari didn't stop.

He got Silas by the collar with one hand and drew back the other, and Silas's body did what it had been trained to do since he was fifteen years old in a practice ring under Gutterlight.

He didn't decide it. He'd decided not to fight, and he'd meant it, and his body didn't care what he'd decided. Omari's fist came in, and Silas's good left hand came up and caught the wrist and turned it, the way he'd turned Jerrick's, the way he'd turned the burned runner's under the rail bridge. He twisted. He came up off the floor on one knee and put Omari's arm up behind his back, high, at exactly the angle where one more inch of pressure would snap something, and he held it there.

The kitchen went silent.

Omari didn't move. He was a strong man and a proud one, and Silas could feel in the arm he was holding that Omari knew exactly what Silas could do with it. He'd felt it go to the edge. He knew how much was left.

Silas looked at the table.

Isola was looking at him.

She'd come up on one elbow. Her face was grey and her leg was dead on the cloth and her other hand was still holding the strip with her cousin's name against her chest, and she was looking straight at him, at his hand on her father's arm, at the angle, at the inch.

She'd seen this before. She'd seen it from the top of a rail bridge, from behind an iron upright, with her breath held. She'd watched him take a boy's arm right to this exact place and stop. She'd sat across a table from him the next morning and told him so. *I watched you not break a man's arm yesterday. You ain't gone break mine.*

She was the only one in this room who'd seen it. She was the only one who knew he could do it. She was the only one who knew what it cost him not to.

He let go.

He didn't push Omari away. He didn't step back. He just opened his hand and let the arm go, slow, and went back down on the floor beside the table, on Camille's board, and sat there with blood running into his beard and his black hand in his lap, and waited for whatever was coming next.

Omari stood over him, breathing hard, rubbing his wrist.

"Daddy," Isola said. "Enough."

She didn't say it loud. She didn't need to. Omari stopped. His whole body stopped, like somebody had laid a hand on his chest. He turned around and looked at his daughter on the table, and whatever was on her face, Silas couldn't see it from the floor.

Omari stood there a long time. Then he went to the sink and put both his hands flat on the counter and bowed his head and didn't move.

Isola lay back down.

She didn't look at Silas. He waited for her to. He sat there on the floor with her fingers still loose around his on the edge of the table, because she hadn't let go, she'd held on the whole time, and he waited for her to turn her head. She didn't. She looked at the ceiling. She looked at the window. She looked at her mother in the doorway. She looked at everything in that kitchen but him.

He noticed. He noticed every second of it.

---

Selah came to the table.

She walked past her husband at the sink without touching him. She walked past her sons in the doorway, Micah with his hand clamped on the tall one's arm, holding him back. She walked past Rhea, standing by the stove with her inked hands crossed over her chest, and past Remy at the window, and she didn't look at any of them. She came to the edge of the table where her daughter lay, and she looked down at Isola's leg for a long time.

The cold came off her in waves. Silas could feel it from the floor. It wasn't like the cold of the Halflight gate or the cold of the funeral road. It was living cold. A cold that had a pulse in it.

"Mama," Isola said.

"Hush, baby."

Selah put one hand flat on the table beside the leg, not touching it. Frost spread out from under her palm across the wood in a slow pale fan.

Then she turned her head and looked down at Silas.

She looked at him longer than Omari had. She looked at his face, swelling and bleeding. She looked at his right hand, black to the knuckle. She looked at the marks on his arms, all the old names Camille had bound into him, and Silas watched her eyes move over them, one at a time, and stop. She didn't reach for them. She didn't touch them. She drew her hand back from the edge of the table, closer to herself, like they were hot.

"I'm not touching any of that," Selah said quietly. "That's his mother's work. I don't touch another woman's work."

"Ma'am."

"I'm looking for the part of you that's still living." She was still looking at him. At his chest. At his breath, going in and out half a count behind her daughter's. "There's not much of it. But it's there." She looked up at Rhea. "You. You're his sister."

"Yes ma'am."

"You can hold that black in her leg where it is? Not push it. Not close it. Just hold it, so it doesn't climb while I work?"

Rhea uncrossed her arms. She looked at her own hands, the ink cracked and dull from last night. "I can hold it."

"Then come here and hold it."

Rhea came. She put both hands flat on Isola's thigh above the highest line, the way she had at 3C, and she started to speak, low and fast. Under her palms, the black went still.

Selah reached down and took Silas's left hand off the edge of the table.

Isola's fingers slid off his. Selah didn't let the gap open. She held his hand and laid it flat on the table, palm up, right beside her daughter's hip, so close the edge of his palm touched the cloth of Isola's skirt. Then she laid her own hand on top of his. Her palm on his palm. Cold as a cistern in winter.

With her other hand, she reached up and touched her daughter's ankle, right where the black went in.

Isola hissed.

Selah closed her eyes.

She started to hum.

Six notes. Low. The same six, over and over, steady as breathing. Silas didn't know them. He'd never heard them before in his life. But he felt them. He felt them come down Selah's arm and into her palm and through it, into his. He felt them go into his hand and up his wrist, past the five black letters, up into his arm, his chest, his ribs, cold and slow, moving with every beat of his heart. Like frost moving through the cracks in a window.

He looked down. On the table, from under Selah's palm on his, thin pale lines were spreading out across the wood. White. Branching. Running beside the black in Isola's leg, following every line of it, the three branches and the one behind the knee and the one going in. Not touching it. Not joining it. Just running alongside it, close, like somebody walking beside you in the dark with a lamp.

The hum went on.

His heart, slow and far away inside his chest, answered on the fourth note.

He felt it. He felt it catch, and kick, and come a half beat closer. Then the hum went around again, and on the sixth note, his lungs pulled. On their own. Not after hers. With hers.

Selah's eyes stayed closed. Frost was climbing her arms now. He could see it, a pale lace spreading up from her wrists toward her elbows, over the brown skin, white and fine and very cold.

From the doorway, a sound.

Silas looked up.

Melena had come in. She'd been on the stairs from the cinder pit with Remy, and she'd come up last, and she'd been standing behind the tall cousin and Micah in the hall where nobody could see her. Now she'd stepped into the kitchen doorway. She'd pushed her hood back. Her hair was coming down out of it, red, all of it, over her shoulders and the front of the borrowed sweater, bright as a lamp in that warm, close room.

She was staring at Selah's hands.

"That's the Elders' Prayer," Melena said.

Selah's eyes opened.

The hum didn't stop. It didn't even falter. Selah kept it going under her breath, the same six notes, steady, her hand flat on Silas's and her other hand on her daughter's ankle. But her eyes came up from the table and went to the doorway. To the girl standing in it with the red hair coming down.

Silas watched Selah Wraithmoor's face.

He watched it go still. The way Camille's face had gone still at the Gutterlight house that first night, looking at Melena across a bowl of stew. The way his father's face had gone soft and then closed over a green stone on a desk. A face seeing something it hadn't seen in a long, long time and had stopped expecting to see again.

The six notes kept going. Selah's mouth kept shaping them.

And between the fifth note and the sixth, so soft it was almost part of the hum, she said a name.

"Lucia?"
