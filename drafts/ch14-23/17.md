# CHAPTER 17: SILAS

The boots on the stairs were Ironcross. He could tell by the sound. Ironcross issued its door men the same heavy-soled boots it had been issuing for a hundred years, and they rang on stone the way nothing else did, flat and loud, like someone dropping coins down a well. Four sets. Maybe five. Coming down the half staircase from Ashvein fast, and above them, faint but getting closer, a sound Silas didn't want to name, a sound like wind with no wind in it.

More Silence. More Shrouds. The Synod didn't send one when it could send three.

He stood at the bottom of the Halflight stairs with Isola in his arms and the gate standing open behind him, its iron still dark with his blood, the words UNAUTHORIZED TRANSFER and HIGH HERESY burning white over his head. He couldn't go up. He couldn't go back through the station. Every lock in Halflight was open, and every clerk in Ironcross would know whose blood had opened them by nightfall.

He had nowhere to go. He stood there anyway, because he couldn't think of anything else to do with his feet.

*Down.*

It came from under his breastbone. The same place Jude's voice had come from, but not Jude.

*Down, Silas. You're standing on top of it. You opened it, you idiot, you're standing in the door. Go down.*

"Rhea," he said out loud.

*Don't talk. They can hear you. Go down.*

He looked down. At his feet, where the stone of the stair met the stone of the floor, the world was thin again, the way it had been inside the station. Halflight's floor like a picture painted on glass, and under it, through it, the roads. The old transfer paths. The narrower ways below those. The dead were still there, still hooked into his marks, murmuring, and as he looked down at them, they turned toward him again, all at once, like they'd been waiting for him to remember.

He stepped down into them.

---

The funeral road was cold, and it was quiet, and it was very, very long.

He'd heard about it his whole life without ever once walking it. Every Graves child did. It was the road under the roads, the one the dead used, the one his mother's work had been built to keep closed. Thresholds and grave nails and funeral thread. All of it, every stitch Camille had ever sewn, was a door shut on this road, so that whatever walked it stayed walking it and didn't come up into somebody's kitchen.

He was walking it with his own blood and his own marks and a girl in his arms, and every door his mother had ever shut was somewhere above his head.

It didn't look like anything. That was the strange part. He'd expected darkness, and it wasn't dark exactly. It was grey, all of it, a long grey passage with no walls he could touch and no ceiling he could see, and the floor was stone, worn smooth in a groove down the middle by a long time of feet. Things opened off it on both sides. Other passages. Other roads. He didn't look at them.

The dead walked with him. They didn't touch Isola. He noticed that, and held on to it. They were hooked into him, into every mark on his skin, and they walked along at his shoulders and his back like a crowd following a procession, murmuring, but they didn't reach for her once. She was his. He'd said so. They'd heard.

*Left,* Rhea said.

He went left. A passage opened that hadn't been there.

*Keep walking. Don't stop at the cross-roads. Don't look down the ones that slope.*

"Why not?"

*Because I said so. And don't talk.*

He didn't talk. He walked. The cold had gotten into his right hand, deep, all the way into the bone, and the hand had stopped hurting a while ago and he didn't like that. He could feel Isola's weight in his arms and her head against his shoulder and her breath, still thin, still quick, against his collar. He couldn't feel his own fingers under her knees.

He looked down at them once. The black had come up past the second knuckle on his first two fingers. Not lines, like on her leg. Just black, like the hand had been dipped in ink and left to dry. When he tried to close it, nothing happened.

He didn't look again.

*How long has it been,* he thought, and didn't say.

*Since what?* Rhea's voice came back right away.

*Since you helped me with anything.*

There was a pause. In it, he could feel her. Not hear her, feel her, the way he could feel the dead on his marks. Rhea, somewhere above him in the world, standing very still with her hand pressed flat against something.

*A year,* she said. *About.*

*You remember why?*

*I remember.* Another pause. *Keep walking.*

He kept walking.

---

Jude came and went.

He'd be there, under Silas's breastbone, steady, *I'm here, Si, I'm here,* and then he'd go quiet, and then he'd be back. Silas didn't understand it at first. Then he did. It was the same way he felt Rhea, the same way he felt the dead. A road. A thread. Three of them, running from his chest to theirs, like the threads under the glass of Dante's map.

He'd never known it was there. He'd been carrying it his whole life without knowing.

*Under the ward-clay,* Rhea said, as if he'd asked. Maybe he had. *All three of us. Since we were small.*

*Who put them there?*

*Don't ask me. I don't know. I never felt mine till today either.* A breath. *Ma might know. She won't say. You go ask her when you're done being this stupid.*

*Thanks.*

*Keep walking.*

Jude came back. *Si.* His voice was strange, strained, like he was talking while doing something else. *Si, the Ironcross alarm went to the Graves board. The whole board. Ma's board. You know why?*

*No.*

*Because Ironcross used an old funeral lock on that gate. An old one. Graves work. Somebody in our family built that lock a hundred years ago and Ironcross has been using it ever since, and you just bled into it, and now every Graves board in Shadowmere is lit up like—*

Jude stopped.

Silas felt it. Not words. A shift, under his breastbone, like somebody standing up too fast.

*Jude?*

Nothing. Then, very faint, not in Jude's voice, coming through Jude the way a sound comes through a wall:

*Who are you talking to?*

Dante.

Silas stopped walking.

*Keep walking,* Rhea said, sharp. *Silas. Keep walking.*

*That's Daddy.*

*I know who it is.*

He felt Jude go very still. He felt something in his little brother hold its breath. And then, faint, from the other side of the wall, Dante again, closer now, his voice gone low and careful in a way Silas had never heard it go:

*Jude. Who are you listening to?*

The thread went dead.

It didn't fade. It didn't go quiet the way it had when Jude was busy. It went dead, all at once, cut clean, like Jude had reached up into his own chest and pulled it out by the root. Silas stood in the grey of the funeral road with Isola in his arms and the dead at his shoulders and felt the place under his breastbone where his brother had been, and there was nothing there now. Just bone.

*He cut it,* Rhea said quietly.

*Why?*

*So Daddy couldn't follow it.* A pause. *Keep walking, Silas. Please.*

She'd never said please to him in her life.

He kept walking.

---

The road came up through the wash crypt behind South Bridge.

He knew it as soon as he saw it. Everybody in Shadowmere knew the wash crypt, at least from the outside. It sat under the south end of the bridge, where the river ran black under the old stone arches, and it was where the Quiet Staff from every Ring and every ward brought their dead to be washed and wrapped before they went down to Gravebend. Silas had stood outside it a dozen times waiting on a body. He'd never been inside. Nobody went inside but the Quiet Staff and the dead.

The funeral road ran right up through the middle of its floor.

He came up out of the grey into a long, low room of wet white stone, lit by lamps burning blue in iron cages along the walls. Stone tables stood in rows down the middle of it, each one with a drain carved into its surface, each one empty. The air smelled like salt and cold water and something sweet underneath, like the inside of Blue Circle. Somewhere, water was running.

Nobody was there. At this hour, the Quiet Staff had gone home. Silas stood between the washing tables with Isola in his arms and the dead crowding up out of the floor behind him, and for one second, he just breathed.

*There's a stair,* Rhea said. *Back corner. Behind the shroud racks. It goes up.*

He found it. A narrow iron door he'd never have seen if she hadn't told him where to look, set into the back wall behind long racks of folded white cloth. It wasn't locked. It opened onto a spiral stair, iron, turning up into the dark.

*Up where?*

*You'll see.*

He went up.

The stair was very narrow, and very steep, and it turned and turned and turned. He took it one step at a time with Isola held tight against his chest and his bad shoulder grinding against the wall on every turn and his right hand dead under her knees. He couldn't tell how far he'd climbed. He counted steps and lost count. He counted again.

Somewhere around the second hundred, she moved.

He felt it before he understood it. A shift in her weight, her head lifting off his shoulder, just a little. Then her hand, which had been holding the strip against her chest the whole way, loosened, and moved, and came up and caught a fistful of his coat.

"You," Isola said.

Her voice was nothing. A thread. Hoarse and slurred and so quiet he had to stop climbing to hear it.

"Yeah," he said. "It's me."

"You holding me wrong."

He looked down at her. Her eyes were barely open. She was grey under the brown, and her lips had gone pale, and she was frowning up at him, actually frowning, like he'd done something particularly stupid in front of company.

"How?"

"Your arm." She swallowed. "Under my knee. It's pulling. On the leg. Move it up. Under my thigh. Higher."

He moved it up. He did it slowly, as carefully as he'd ever done anything, shifting her weight against his chest and sliding his good arm up under her thigh, above the knee, above where the black stopped. She made a small sound in her throat, not quite pain.

"Like that?"

"Higher."

He moved it higher.

"And your other—" She stopped. Breathed. "Your other arm. Not my back. My shoulders. You got me folded."

He shifted his bad arm up behind her shoulders and felt his own shoulder grind and catch and held on. She came straighter against him. Her head settled into the place between his neck and his shoulder like it had been made to go there.

"Better?"

She didn't answer right away. He felt her breathe, a little deeper than she had been. A little steadier.

"Yeah," she said. "Better."

Then she turned her face into his neck, and her hand stayed fisted in his coat, and she was asleep.

Silas stood on the iron stair in the dark with her breath warm against his throat, and he didn't move for a while.

He'd done exactly what she'd told him. Every word. She'd been half dead and barely awake and had looked up at him like he was a fool, and she'd told him how to hold her, and he'd done it, and she'd decided it was good enough, and she'd gone back to sleep. She hadn't asked him. She'd told him.

He found he didn't mind that at all.

He kept climbing.

---

The stair came out under his own building.

He didn't understand it at first. He came up the last turn and found an old iron door at the top, and a landing, and beyond the landing a low cellar he'd never been in, full of broken chairs and crates and the smell of damp brick. Then he saw the boiler, the old one that ran the radiators, the one that knocked against his wall every time a tram passed. And he knew where he was.

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane.

He'd lived over a door to the funeral road for years. He'd come home and gone to bed and woken up and made tea four floors above the road the dead walked, and he'd never once known it.

*Ma knows every door in this building,* Rhea said, before he could ask. *She stitched them.*

He didn't have anything for that.

There was a door at the far end of the cellar, the one that led up into the building's stairwell. He went toward it. Behind him, the dead came up out of the iron stair and into the cellar, a grey crowd of them, murmuring, still hooked into his marks. He could feel them pressing at the walls. At the thresholds his mother had stitched into this building, the living wards she checked every morning on her way up to tell him what he was doing wrong.

The wards weren't there.

He felt the place where they should've been. Every threshold in this building had hummed under his feet for years, Camille's thread, deep and steady. Now there was nothing. Just door frames. Just wood and brick.

*Remy took them down,* Rhea said. *I called him. Told him how. Living wards don't let the dead through, and you're carrying a hundred of them on your back. If he hadn't stripped them, you'd have hit that door like a wall.*

*Remy knew how to strip Ma's wards?*

*Remy knows a lot of things.* A pause. *Ask him sometime.*

*Melena—*

*Upstairs. Second floor. Remy's with her. She's fine. She's scared.* Another pause. *Who is she, Silas?*

*Later.*

*That's what Daddy always says.*

He stopped at the cellar door.

---

Rhea was standing on the other side of it.

She'd opened it before he could. She stood at the bottom of the stairwell in the doorway with one hand still on the knob and the other pressed flat against her own breastbone, right where he felt her, and she was looking at him.

She looked like their mother. She always had. She had Camille's mouth and Camille's way of standing, square, like the ground had better hold her or else. She had Dante's eyes, which she'd hated since she was twelve. Her locs were wrapped up high in a dark cloth the way she'd worn them since she started doing funeral work, and there was salt on her sleeves and Underchant marks inked across the backs of both her hands, fresh, the ink still shining. She was thinner than she'd been a year ago. She was older. She was his sister.

She looked at him for a long time without saying anything. At his face. At the black creeping up his right hand. At the dead behind him, crowding the cellar, hooked into his marks.

Then she said it. Out loud, for the first time in a year. The thing she'd sent him every fight night, two words and a question mark, the only thing she'd said to him in all that time.

"You alive?"

Silas looked at her.

"It's good to see you, Rhea," he said.

She didn't answer that. Her eyes went down, from his face to his arms, to what he was carrying. To Isola, asleep against his neck, her hand fisted in his coat. To the way he was holding her, high under the thigh, shoulders straight, exactly the way he'd been told.

Rhea looked at that for a long time.

She didn't say anything. Not a word. She just looked, and her face didn't change at all, and somehow that was so much worse than anything she could've said.

Then she stepped back out of the doorway to let him through.

"Third floor," she said. "Your table. It's the only flat thing in this building long enough to lay her on." She turned and started up the stairs ahead of him. "And Silas."

He stopped.

She didn't turn around. "Whatever you did at that gate. Whatever you said. Don't say it again in this house."

She went up.

He stood at the bottom of the stairwell of 3 Shadowmere Lane with Isola asleep in his arms and the dead at his back, and he looked up the stairs his mother had stitched for years, bare now, every threshold stripped. Above him, on the second floor, a door opened, and he heard Remy's voice, low, and under it, Melena's, frightened.

He started to climb.
