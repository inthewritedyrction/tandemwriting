# CHAPTER 8: SILAS

Silas had walked Gutterlight at every hour there was, bleeding and sober and both at once, and he'd never walked it next to his mother in her funeral shoes.

She'd put on the real ones for this. Black, low heel, the pair she saved for standing at the front of a service while everybody else cried behind her. Gold clasp in her locs. Spool of thread in her coat pocket. She walked down the lane toward the noodle counter like the whole tier owed her money and she'd come to collect it in person, and people on the street got out of her way before they got out of his. That had never happened before either. He didn't love it.

Melena walked on his left in the clothes Remy had brought that afternoon, work pants and a grey sweater and a brown coat to her knees, a dark wrap pulled down tight over her hair. She'd kept Remy's mint. She'd been holding it all day, turning it over between two fingers like a rosary, and the wrapper was worn soft at the folds. Remy walked on her other side, close enough to catch her and far enough not to, and every few steps his eyes went to her face and came back.

*Oh, hell no.*

Silas had known Remy since they were six. He'd watched him fall for a girl who ran numbers for the east-gate crew, a singer at a Lamp Street bar, and, for one very bad week, a Bookkeeper twice his age. He'd never seen him look at anybody the way he'd been looking at Melena since two o'clock this morning, like she was a lamp somebody had handed him in a windstorm and told him not to drop.

The girl was on fire. Literally. The Synod was hunting her, Dante was lying about her, and Rae was hiding something big enough to make his mother put on shoes. And Remy was out here falling in love.

Remy caught him looking. "What?"

Silas kept his eyes on the lane. "Nothing."

"That's a whole lot of face for nothing, Si." Remy leaned around Melena to get a better look at it.

Silas jerked his chin at the street ahead. "Watch the street."

Remy grinned and didn't watch the street. "You ask Ma for her name yet?"

Silas didn't need to ask whose name. He'd been not asking about it all day. "Ask me that one more time and I'm putting you in the canal."

Remy spread his hands. "There's no canal in Gutterlight."

"I'll dig one," Silas said.

Beside him, Camille made a small sound that wasn't a laugh, because she was still too mad to laugh, but was close enough to one that Silas wanted to put himself in the canal too.

He wasn't thinking about silver locs. He wasn't thinking about a hand jammed in a skirt pocket or a chin coming up in the rain like she'd been waiting all her life for somebody to give her a reason. He had a burned palm, a girl the Synod wanted, a father who hadn't answered his own wife's ultimatum, and a godmother walking him into a Hush den. He had plenty to think about.

Rae's goddaughter. Of all the people in Shadowmere to knock salt out of.

Rae was waiting at the corner by the noodle counter, black from throat to ankle, hands folded, bells silent on her wrist. She didn't look at Camille's shoes. She didn't say good evening. She turned around and walked into the alley behind the counter, and they followed her through a gap in the fence and down a short flight of stairs to a plain wooden door in a plain brick wall, no lantern, no route marks in the frame.

Silas stopped on the bottom step.

He'd grown up with his elbows on Dante's map-room glass. He knew every Mercer passage in this tier, every safehouse and drop and service route behind the tailor rows, every door his family owned and most of the ones they were trying to. He could walk Shadowmere blind and tell you whose threshold he was standing on by the feel of it under his boots.

He had never seen this door in his life.

Rae didn't touch it. She looked at her son.

Remy stepped past her, laid his palm flat on the wood, and said something under his breath that Silas didn't catch. The door caught it fine. It swung open onto a stairwell full of blue light and a low, steady sound like a room full of people crying through a wall.

Silas looked at Remy. Remy didn't look back.

*Since when, motherfucker?*

He didn't say it. He'd say it later, somewhere without his mother and Rae and a burning girl to watch, and Remy was going to answer it. He went down the stairs.

The den had been a bathhouse back when Shadowmere had hot springs, and somebody had drained the pools and left them like open graves. Three of them, tiled blue and white gone grey, the two shallow ones full of cushions and blankets and people. Twenty, maybe thirty. Eyes open, every one of them smiling the same small smile, like they'd remembered something sweet and couldn't remember what. Blue lamps hung off the pipes. Steam came up through the floor grates. And every inch of wall was sewn with thread, grey line over grey line, crossed and recrossed until the tile looked woven, and it hummed.

He felt it in his back teeth the second his boot hit the floor. So did his mother. She stopped dead at the bottom of the stairs and looked at the nearest wall the way she'd looked at Jude's arm the day he broke it, already doing the math on what it would cost to fix and who was going to pay.

"Lament work," Camille said. "Every line. Three hundred people walked out of here lighter and don't remember why."

"They paid for it," Rae said.

"I know they paid for it." Camille didn't take her eyes off the wall. "I'm wondering who kept the change."

Miss Ora sat at the bottom of the deep pool in a velvet chair somebody had planted on the drain grate, thin as a broom handle, head wrapped in blue silk, a pipe between two fingers putting out blue smoke. Ora had the softest voice in Shadowmere and the hardest ledger, and everybody who'd ever owed her knew which one they'd hear from. A clerk stood behind a counter at the top of the pool steps with rows of glass vials on the shelf behind him. Two runners with blue cords knotted at their wrists leaned on the tile by the far stairs, and their eyes went to Silas first and to the wrap on Melena's head second.

The taller one held it a beat too long. Silas looked at him until he stopped.

He'd let him keep the eyes. Tonight.

"Renae." Ora's voice was a lullaby. "You brought me the whole family."

"I brought you a girl," Rae said. "The rest of them followed."

Ora laughed low and let her eyes go over Camille, the shoes, the gold clasp. "Mrs. Graves. In my house."

Camille didn't look at her. "Don't get used to it."

"Wouldn't dream of it, baby." Ora's eyes moved to Silas and stayed long enough to be rude. "Dante's boy. The one who fights." The pipe tipped toward his wrapped hand. "Already been in a fight tonight, looks like."

"Last night," Silas said. "I'm pacing myself."

Ora smiled at that like he'd said something sweet, and looked past him to Melena, and the smile went somewhere else. She looked at Melena a long time. Long enough that Silas shifted half a step to the left without deciding to.

"One drop," Rae said. "You measure it. In the walls."

"I always measure it." Ora lifted her pipe toward the clerk without turning her head. "Make it clean."

The clerk did it in front of them. He was a kid, barely older than Teo, nervous hands and a blue cord wrapped three times around his wrist, and he thumbed the cap off a vial and let one clear drop fall into a cup of tea that didn't change color. He handed it to Remy. Remy handed it to Melena.

She held it in both hands and looked at Silas over the rim, like she was waiting on permission.

He didn't want to give it. He wanted to take the cup out of her hands and pour it down the drain grate under Ora's chair and walk her right back up those stairs to a dark house that couldn't hold her. Every rule he'd ever been taught about Hush said no.

"You don't have to," he said.

"I know." She drank it.

Silas stood two steps above her with his good hand on his knife and watched her face like it was a fight he had money on.

Her shoulders came down first. Slow, like somebody setting down a box they'd carried for miles. Her hands loosened on the cup. Her breath went long without anybody telling her to count it. She blinked twice and looked around the den like she'd only just walked in, and the walls changed pitch, the hum dropping lower in his teeth, steadier, like something big had finally sat down to eat.

"Oh," Melena said softly.

Remy sat down on the step beside her, not touching, close. "How you feel?"

"Quiet." She tried the word out like it belonged to somebody else. She laid one hand flat on her chest, over the letter under her sweater, and held the other up in front of her face. Clean nails. Brown skin. Nothing climbing. "I can think about her, and it doesn't come."

Remy smiled at her, and it was the realest thing Silas had seen on his best friend's face in a year.

*Fuck.* Silas let out a breath he'd been holding since Dante's office. It was working. Rae's ugly, dangerous, bathhouse-full-of-strangers' grief idea was working, and he hated that it was working, and he'd take it.

Camille hadn't moved from the wall. She was reading the thread the way she read her crossword, lips moving, and she didn't look relieved at all.

A girl came down the far stairs while Ora was talking.

Silas wouldn't have looked twice except she didn't look at anything. Everybody looked at the pools when they came in a den. She looked at the counter. Long dark coat, fitted at the waist, a cut nobody in Gutterlight could afford, and a stiff bag on her shoulder. She set a wardcloth package tied with blue cord on the counter, about the size of a brick, and the clerk punched a black strip from a stack beside him and handed it to her without a word. She was back up the stairs in under a minute, weight back, head turned just enough to count the turns.

Somebody taught her to walk like that. Somebody she was scared of.

Not his business. Silas let his eyes come off the stairs. The clerk set the package on the shelf between the vials.

"Remington." Rae said it from the edge of the deep pool, where she'd gone down two steps to stand beside Ora's chair. "Come here."

Remy looked at Melena.

"I'm good," Melena told him, and for the first time since Dante's office, she sounded it.

Remy went down into the pool. Rae put her hand on his arm, Ora leaned forward in her chair, and the three of them started talking so low the walls ate it. His mother had her back to the room, reading her thread. And the tall runner by the far stairs had stopped leaning, because the wrap had slipped at the back of Melena's head, and there was red showing under the blue lamps, the brightest thing in the whole damn den.

Silas went up two steps and stood where that boy would have to look through him to see her, and he let the boy see exactly what that would cost.

It took fifteen seconds.

He turned around, and Melena wasn't on the step.

She was at the counter.

He saw it all at once, the way you saw a punch you'd already lost the chance to stop. The clerk had a vial in his hand, full, capped, clear to the top. Melena was holding something out to him across the counter, dark, catching the lamplight, and it took Silas half a heartbeat to understand it was one of the stones off her mourning collar. She'd worn the dress under Remy's borrowed clothes. He could see the thread hanging loose where she'd ripped the stone free with her own fingers.

The clerk wasn't looking at the stone. He was looking at her face with his mouth hanging open, like he was listening to music from three streets over.

He took the stone. He gave her the vial.

Silas was already moving. "*Melena.*"

She thumbed the cap off the way she'd watched him do it and drank the whole thing before Silas hit the bottom step.

Remy's head came up in the pool. His face went the color of ash.

Melena turned around with the empty vial held up in two fingers like a prize, and she was smiling. The den smile. The same small, sweet, empty one on every face in the shallow pools.

"It's so quiet, Silas." She laughed, light. "It's so *quiet*."

Her hands were clean. Her breath was slow and even. No smoke, no black, no heat on his skin. Every warning she'd ever given him, in Dante's office, on Lamp Street, at his mother's kitchen table, was gone, and he knew before it happened that gone was the worst thing it could be.

The air went white.

There was no knock on the door this time. It came out of all of her at once, heat rolling off her body like a foundry door kicked open, and the cup on the counter shattered, and the vials behind the clerk burst one after another down the shelf like knuckles cracking. The clerk screamed and slapped at his wrist where the blue cord had caught.

The walls lit.

The first line of grey thread went up beside the counter, and the fire ran along the grout, orange and fast, hit the next line where they crossed, and took it, and took the next. Three hundred lines of somebody else's sorrow went up like a fuse, and the hum in the walls went from a crowd crying to a crowd screaming. Silas felt it in his skull.

The package on the shelf blew.

It went with a crack he felt in his ribs. Silver sprayed out of the wardcloth and caught and burned blue-white, and something small and hot hit him under the eye. He tore it off his cheek. Half a coin, melted, stuck to his skin. He threw it down.

The people in the shallow pools didn't move.

Fire was running the walls over their heads, the steam pipes were starting to shriek, and they lay there on their cushions smiling up at it like somebody had lit it for them special.

"*Up.*" Silas grabbed the nearest one by the collar, a man old enough to be Dante, and hauled him off his blanket and threw him at the stairs. The man stumbled, caught himself, kept walking, still smiling. Silas grabbed the next. His burned palm split on somebody's coat and he felt it go and didn't stop.

Across the room, his mother had kicked her funeral shoes off and walked straight into the other shallow pool in her stockings. She had a grown woman under each arm and she was marching them up the steps like she was walking them into church, and every time one of them tried to sit back down, Camille said "*No ma'am*" in the voice she used on her children, and the woman stood up straight.

At the bottom of the deep pool, Ora was on her feet, the lullaby gone out of her voice. She jabbed her pipe at the counter like a knife. "*Who sold her that?*"

"She got in my head!" The clerk was backing up the far stairs with his wrist smoking. "Ora, I swear on my mama, she got in my *head*—"

Remy had gotten to Melena. She was standing where she'd been, by the counter, in the middle of the fire, with the hem of the borrowed sweater smoking and her face turned up to the burning walls like she was watching a sunrise. Remy stood in front of her as close as the heat would let him, which wasn't close enough, and his voice was shaking.

"Melena. Look at me. What color's my coat?"

She looked at him. The smile didn't move. "Ugly green."

"Can I pick you up? I gotta carry you out of here. Can I?"

She thought about it. In the middle of a burning room, with the roof starting to groan over all of them, she stood there and actually thought about it, and Silas, dragging a boy out of a pool by the back of his shirt, wanted to scream at both of them.

"Yes," she said.

Remy picked her up. He did it fast and he did it right, one arm under her knees, one behind her back, and the heat coming off her had to be like holding a stove against his chest, because his jaw locked and his eyes watered and he didn't let go.

"Not the front," Silas said.

Remy was already turning. "*Our* door."

*Our.* Silas filed that too.

He shoved the boy up the stairs and turned around once. The clerk was gone. The counter was burning. And sitting on the wood in the middle of the flames was a dark stone with a loose black thread hanging off it.

He grabbed it. It seared straight into his good hand, and he closed his fist on it anyway and put it in his coat.

He looked for Rae.

The deep pool was empty except for Ora's chair, and Ora's chair was on fire. Ora was going up the far side with a runner pulling at each arm. There wasn't a black dress anywhere in the room.

Gone. Rae had walked them into this room, and she'd walked out of it before it burned, and Silas was going to find out exactly how long she'd known she'd need to.

He ran.

The plain door was standing open at the top of the stairwell. Remy went through with Melena against his chest, Camille went through in her stockings with her funeral shoes in one hand and her coat over her mouth, and Silas went last, and behind him Blue Circle came down. The tile roof gave with a groan he felt in his feet and a crack like a rib, and a fist of hot air came up the stairwell and slammed him into the wall. Smoke boiled out through the gap in the fence. Somewhere on the street side, bells started ringing.

He pulled the door shut behind him and looked back from the top step. There wasn't a door in the brick. There was just brick.

*Since when, motherfucker.*

They came out behind the noodle counter into the rain. The street in front of the bathhouse was chaos, people spilling out of the front gate burned and smoking and some of them still smiling, runners with blue cords scattering in every direction, Blue Circle's lantern lying shattered in the gutter and still burning. In the middle of the road stood one runner with his cord burned black down to the knot, screaming at nobody.

"The package. The package was *in* there. Somebody gonna pay for that package."

His eyes landed on the four of them coming out of the alley. On the steam coming off Remy's arms. On the girl he was carrying. The runner turned and ran toward the rail line.

Silas marked the direction. He'd see him later.

"The dark house," Remy said, breathing hard. Melena was limp against him now, eyes half closed, still smiling a little, steam rising off the both of them into the rain.

"Dark house won't hold her." Camille was putting her shoes back on one at a time, standing on one foot in the middle of the street like it was her own kitchen floor. "Half that den saw her face. And whatever's in her tonight, my thread won't keep it till morning."

"So where?" Remy's voice cracked on it.

Camille finished with her shoe and straightened up and looked at Silas, and he knew what she was going to say before she said it, and he didn't like it, and he wasn't going to argue.

"There's one door in Shadowmere I sewed with your blood in the line," she said. "It don't answer to Dante. It don't answer to Renae. It answers to you."

He paid for the whole back car of the last south tram. The tram man took one look at the smoke climbing over Gutterlight and the burned girl in Remy's arms and decided he'd never seen any of them. At every stop the doors opened on empty platforms and shut again. Camille sat beside Silas with her hands folded in her lap and her funeral shoes wet, and halfway down the line she said, without looking at him, "Renae was gone before the roof came down."

Silas kept his eyes on the window. "I saw."

"She brought that child into those walls and she was gone before they burned."

He took the stone out of his coat and turned it in the tram light. Smooth, dark, heavy for its size, and something moved deep inside it when he tipped it, like smoke caught in glass. The skin across his good hand had bubbled where he'd grabbed it. Two hands, both burned, in two days, by the same girl.

Across the car, Melena opened her eyes and looked up at Remy holding her. She didn't pull away.

"You smell like smoke," she said.

"So do you." Remy's voice came out wrecked.

Her eyes slid shut again. "I wanted it quiet. Just for a little while."

Remy didn't say anything. He looked out the window at the rain going by with his jaw locked, holding a girl who'd just burned down a city block, and Silas watched his best friend try not to cry on a tram and knew there wasn't a damn thing in Shadowmere that was going to talk him out of her.

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane was dark when they got there. Silas went up the steps first and laid his ruined palm flat on the outer door, and the threshold knew him, the way it always had, his mother's knots in every corner of the frame and his own blood in the line, a hum under his hand that went deeper when he leaned his weight into it.

"She's with me," he told it.

The door opened. Remy carried Melena across, and Silas held his breath and waited for the ward to fight her the way Camille's thread had in the dark house.

It didn't. The hum went tight under his palm. It held. It settled.

The second floor had been empty since spring. Bare mattress, a table, a radiator under the window that knocked every time a tram ran under the street. A real window, looking out over the lane. Remy laid Melena on the mattress and she curled onto her side and was asleep before he'd gotten his arms back.

Silas looked at that window a second longer than he needed to.

Camille sat down on the floor with her back against the wall and her spool of thread in her lap. "I'll sit with her," she said. "Remington, go home and put something on that arm."

"I'm staying." Remy didn't look up from the mattress.

Camille looked at him for a long time. Something in her face gave. "All right, baby."

Silas was already at the door.

"Si." Remy's head came up. "Where you going?"

"Runner from the den went toward the rail line, crying about a package." Silas checked the knife at his hip with the hand that still closed. "Somebody sold her a full vial for a rock, and somebody's package went up with Ora's walls. I want to know whose before the sun comes up."

Remy's eyes went to his hands. "With those?"

"I've done worse with less," Silas said. "Lock it behind me."

His phone buzzed on the stairs. He took it out under the hall lamp.

*Dante: Where is she.*

Two days. Two burned hands, one dead route seller, a godmother who walked out of a fire she'd started, a best friend who knew a door Silas didn't, and a girl from Ashvein whose name he still didn't have.

And now his father wanted to know where she was.

Silas put the phone back in his coat without answering and went out into the rain.
