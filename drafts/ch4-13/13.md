# CHAPTER 13: ISOLA

Asha didn't leave that night either.

Isola had gone home from Halflight braced for it. She'd come through the front door with black three warm against her heart and her face still doing something she didn't want anybody in this house to see, and she'd gone straight down the hall to Asha's room, ready for empty hooks and a bare mattress and the row of bags gone from the wall. The door had been shut. She'd opened it without knocking.

Asha had been sitting on her bed doing her nails.

She'd looked up, and she'd looked at Isola standing in the doorway in her coat, and she'd gone back to her nails. "You need something?"

The bags were still there. All of them. Packed and buckled in their row against the wall.

"No," Isola had said, and shut the door, and stood in the hall for a long time.

So now it was night, and the house was dark, and Asha was still on the other side of the wall, and Isola was sitting at the kitchen table at an hour when nobody should've been sitting anywhere, with a cup of tea she hadn't touched and her phone face down in front of her, and black three lying on top of it.

She'd taken it out of her pocket four times since dinner. She'd put it back three.

It wasn't about the coin. That was the problem. She had nothing to tell him about the coin. She hadn't found out who was carrying for Blue Six, or at least, she hadn't found out anything she was willing to say. Nobody had come to the house asking about a package that burned. She'd sat across a table from him that morning and taken his route, and she'd asked him what if she needed something that wasn't about the coin, and he'd said use the same route, and she'd walked away feeling like she'd won something.

Now she was sitting in her mother's kitchen at midnight with no reason in the world to use it, and she was going to use it anyway.

She picked up the tag and held it against the back of her phone. The screen lit. It opened onto a thread she'd never seen before, blank and dark, with a single route mark at the top where a name should've been. Three short silver lines, stacked.

She looked at it for a long time. Then she typed.

Isola: You make it home?

She sent it before she could think about it. Then she put the phone face down on the table and pushed it away from her with one finger like it might bite.

It buzzed in under a minute.

She looked at it. She made herself count to ten. Then she turned it over.

Silas: Yeah. You?

Isola: I'm sitting in my mama's kitchen.

Silas: That's home.

Isola: That's a kitchen.

Silas: In Ashvein that might be the same thing.

She almost laughed. She pressed her lips together instead and looked at the ceiling, at the old water stain over the stove that had been there her whole life.

Isola: You don't know nothing about Ashvein.

Silas: I know Micah.

Isola: That's not Ashvein. That's a disease.

There was a pause. She watched the screen. Then:

Silas: He told Jude you threw a towel at him.

Isola sat up straight.

Isola: When?

Silas: Couple days ago. I don't know. Jude tells me things.

Isola: Micah doesn't have a phone.

Silas: Micah's resourceful.

She put the phone down on the table and looked at it, and then she picked it back up, because she was going to have to have a conversation with her baby brother tomorrow, and she wanted the facts first.

Isola: What else did he tell Jude?

Silas: Nothing.

Isola: You hesitated.

Silas: It's text. You can't tell if I hesitated.

Isola: I can tell.

The screen sat there. Then the little dots came up, and went away, and came up again.

Silas: He said you looked at me long enough at Pell's to know who wrapped my hand.

Isola's face went so hot she had to put her cup against it.

Isola: He's lying.

Silas: Okay.

Isola: He lies all the time. He's not even good at it.

Silas: Okay.

Isola: Stop saying okay.

Silas: What you want me to say?

She didn't know what she wanted him to say. She sat there with the cup against her cheek and the phone in her other hand and the house dark around her, and she knew she should put it down and go to bed, and she didn't.

Isola: Who did wrap it?

There was a longer pause this time.

Silas: My mother.

Isola looked at that for a while. She thought about a bandage, white gone grey at the edges, tied so neat nobody could've done it one-handed. She'd known somebody who loved him had tied it. She hadn't thought it would be his mama.

Isola: She did a good job.

Silas: She'd want you to tell her that.

Isola: I'm not telling your mama nothing.

Silas: Smart.

---

It went on like that.

She didn't mean for it to. She'd meant to send one message, to see whether the route worked, to see whether he'd answer. That was all. But he'd answered, and she'd answered him back, and somewhere in there, one line had turned into two and two had turned into a conversation, and she was still sitting at the kitchen table with the tea gone cold, and she hadn't once thought about getting up.

She asked him where he lived, and he wouldn't tell her the street, but he told her something else.

Silas: My building's got a number painted over on the front. Somebody did it twice. You can still see the three under it.

Isola: Why'd they paint over it?

Silas: Don't know. It was like that when I got there.

Isola: Why didn't they do it right?

Silas: I don't think they wanted to.

She thought about that. A number somebody had tried to cover twice and couldn't, or wouldn't, so that it kept showing through no matter what color they put over it. She didn't know why it made her chest hurt. It was a building. It was paint.

Isola: That's the saddest thing anybody's ever told me about a building.

Silas: It's not sad. It's still there.

She didn't have anything for that. She sat with it a while.

Then, because he'd given her something true, and she'd been raised to pay what she owed, she gave him something back.

Isola: My cousin keeps her bags packed. Has for two years. All of them, buckled, lined up against her wall like she's waiting on a train. I quit asking her what for a long time ago.

She looked at it after she sent it. She didn't know why she'd said it. She hadn't said it to anybody, not Selah, not Tevin, not Micah, not even herself, not in words.

Silas: You want to know now?

Isola: Yeah.

Silas: That's not the same as quitting.

She set the phone down. She sat there in the dark with her hands flat on the table, the way Selah sat sometimes when the cold was in her and she was waiting for it to pass, and she didn't pick it up again for a while.

When she did, there was another message waiting.

Silas: You good, Wraithmoor?

She stared at it.

Isola: Don't call me that.

Silas: That's your name.

Isola: That's my block name. That's what the Synod calls me when they're reading off a list.

Silas: What do your people call you?

She thought about it. Her people called her a lot of things. Isola. Mama's baby, when Selah was in a mood. *Girl*, when Omari was out of patience. Rae called her Renae. Tevin, when he wanted something, called her by a name he'd made up when they were five and that she'd sooner die than tell a stranger.

Isola: Isola.

Silas: I know.

Isola: Then use it.

The dots came up and went away. Came up again.

Silas: Goodnight, Wraithmoor.

She glared at the phone. She thought about telling him to stop. She had it all typed out, the whole thing, and her thumb was right over the button.

She deleted it. She didn't tell him to stop.

---

"Who you talking to?"

Isola nearly dropped the phone.

Selah was standing in the kitchen doorway in her house dress and her wrap with a hand on the frame. She'd come down the hall so quiet Isola hadn't heard her, which Selah could do when she wanted to and rarely bothered. She looked tired. She'd been rubbing her temples again. There was a frost-pale patch at her wrist where she'd held it against something cold.

"Nobody," Isola said.

"Mmm." Selah came in and filled the kettle at the sink. "Nobody got you smiling at the table at this hour."

"I'm not smiling."

"I didn't say you were smiling now." Selah set the kettle on the stove and lit it and stood there with her back to Isola, humming under her breath. The same six notes. Over and over. Low enough to be almost nothing.

Isola turned the phone over on the table so the screen was face down.

"Somebody from the Market," she said, after a while. She didn't know why she was saying it. "Somebody I met. At Pell's."

The humming went on.

"He was walking some girl through the lane," Isola said. "When I met him. That's how I met him, really, he was getting her out of the way of a runner and he backed into me. And she was—" She stopped. She thought about the hood pressed against the freight wall, and the curl slipping loose at the edge of it, and the bandaged hand reaching over on Bath Row to tuck it back in. "Mama, you ever seen anybody with red hair? Not dyed. Not rust. Red. Like the inside of a coal."

Selah stopped humming.

She didn't turn around. She stood at the stove with her back to the table and the kettle starting to tick on the burner, and she didn't move at all. The silence went on so long that Isola heard the grounding wires in the walls, and the radiator, and her own heart.

"Where?" Selah said.

"At Pell's. And on Bath Row, yesterday. With him."

Selah turned the burner off. The kettle hadn't boiled. She stood there with her hand on the knob.

"Mama?"

"Go to bed, Isola."

"Mama, what—"

"I said go to bed." Selah turned around. Her face was perfectly calm. Her eyes weren't. "It's late. You got to be up at first bell, and so do I. Take that phone with you, and go to bed, and leave the light off on your way."

Isola went.

She lay on top of her blanket in the dark with her clothes on and the phone on her chest, and she listened to her mother not humming in the kitchen. Selah didn't come down the hall after her. Selah didn't go back to bed. Isola heard her sitting at the table, the chair creaking every so often, for a long time.

She picked up the phone. The thread was still open, the last line still sitting there. *Goodnight, Wraithmoor.*

She started to type. She didn't know what. She was tired, and her mother's face was in her head, and her cousin was on the other side of the wall with her bags packed, and she wanted to ask him something, and she didn't know what it was.

Isola: Can I ask you

She was asleep before she finished it. Her thumb slid on the screen as her hand went loose, and somewhere in the dark, the message went, half a question with nothing at the end of it, and sat on his screen all night under *Goodnight, Wraithmoor*, waiting for her to come back.

---

There was no first bell.

Isola woke up to silence and didn't understand it. She lay there in the grey dark with the phone still on her chest and listened, and something was wrong, and it took her a long time to know what.

Ashvein had rung first bell every morning of her life. It woke the miners and the women who packed their food, and it set the grounding wires humming in every wall on every street, and it was so much a part of the morning that she'd never once thought about it, the way you never thought about your own breathing. It was the first sound of every day.

Today there wasn't one.

She got up. Her feet found her boots without looking. She went down the hall and into the front room, and Omari was standing at the front window in his work clothes with his watch on and his keys on his belt, very still, looking through the whisperglass and the security mesh at the lane.

"Daddy."

He didn't turn around. "Get your mama."

"What—"

"Get your mama. Get your cousins up. Get your brother. Don't put on any lights."

She looked past him through the window.

The lane was full of gold.

That was the first thing she saw. Lamps, carried high on poles, not the coded lanterns over the doors but Synod lamps, the kind she'd only ever seen in the square on execution days, burning a clean, cold gold that made everything they touched look like it had been carved out of metal. There were a dozen of them at least, moving slow down the length of the lane. Under them walked men in Ironcross grey with ledgers under their arms, and others in plain Synod black, and at every door they stopped, and knocked once, and waited.

Behind them, not walking with them, not walking anywhere, stood the Shrouds.

Isola counted three before she made herself stop. They stood at the far end of the lane and at the corner and in the middle of the street, veiled from crown to collar in grey that drank the lamplight, pale marks showing through the cloth where their faces should've been. They didn't move. They didn't do anything. They just stood there, and around each one of them, in a circle maybe ten feet wide, there was no sound at all. Isola could see a neighbor's dog in the lane barking at one of them, its mouth wide, its whole body jerking with it, and no bark came out.

That was why there'd been no first bell. The Silence had taken it.

"It's a search," Omari said quietly. "Whole ward. They started at the east gate before dawn. They'll get to us." He finally turned his head and looked at her. His face was very calm. "Get everybody up, baby girl. And whatever you got, whatever anybody in this house got that they shouldn't, it needs to not be in this house in about four minutes."

Isola went.

She didn't remember most of the next four minutes afterward. She remembered pieces. Selah already up, already dressed, already in the kitchen with her hand flat on the counter and frost spreading out from under her palm in a slow pale fan across the metal trim. Micah coming out of his room in a shirt inside out, saying *what, what*, and Tevin behind him, saying nothing at all, his face gone hard and flat the way it did right before he did something stupid, and Isola stopping in front of him in the hall and putting her hand flat against his chest and saying *no* and watching him hear her. She remembered the grounding wires humming wrong, high and thin, like they were trying to ring the bell themselves.

She remembered her own hand going into her skirt.

The coin was there. It had been there for three days, against her hip, riding the seam. She took it out and looked at it in the grey light, the Veiled One's face and the prayer around the edge and the quicksilver that only caught one corner, and she knew exactly where it was going to go, because she'd known since she was nine years old where Omari put anything he didn't want found.

The stove in the kitchen sat on an iron base with an ash chute behind it, a narrow metal throat that ran down through the floor into the old cinder pit under the house, where nobody had cleaned in forty years because there was no reason to. Isola went into the kitchen past her mother at the counter, and crouched behind the stove, and pulled open the little iron door at the back of the chute.

It breathed up at her, cold and grey and smelling like a century of burned coal.

She dropped the coin in. She heard it go. A little ring against the iron, and then another, farther down, and then nothing.

She shut the door. When she stood up, Asha was standing in the kitchen doorway.

She was dressed. Coat on, boots laced, the stiff tote over her shoulder. She'd heard everything. She'd been listening from the hall. She looked at the stove, and at the little iron door, and then she looked at Isola, and something in her face went very still.

Isola reached into her coat.

She didn't think about it. Her hand just went there, the way it had gone to her skirt, to the inner pocket over her heart where black three was warm against the cord with Rae's token. She didn't know what she was going to say. *They're here.* *There's Shrouds.* *There's gold in the street.* She just knew he'd answered in under a minute last night, and that he'd said *use the same route*, and that he was the only person she knew in all of the Underside who might know what to do with a lane full of Silence.

"Don't," Asha said.

Isola stopped.

Asha came into the kitchen. She came all the way to the stove and stood right in front of Isola, close, so close Isola could see the sleep still in the corners of her eyes and the fear underneath it. She put her hand over Isola's hand, over the pocket, over the tag.

"You call anybody," Asha said, very quietly, "anybody, Isola, and I'm gone. I'll go out the vent. You know I can. I'll go out the vent and down the trench, and you won't find me, not this time, not ever. You'll never see me again."

"Asha—"

"I mean it." Her fingers pressed down. "You want to keep me in this house, you keep your hand out of that coat."

Isola stood there with her cousin's hand over hers.

She thought about the bags. She thought about the name going slack. She thought about Asha on the cliff landing with her hands over her face, standing very still, deciding something.

She took her hand out of her coat.

Asha let go of her. She stepped back. Something went out of her face, fast, too fast to name. Then she turned and went into the front room to stand with the others, and Isola stood alone at the stove for a second with her empty hand at her side, and then she followed.

They knocked four minutes later. Once.

---

The Synod reader was a thin man with a thin voice and a ledger bound in white.

He came in first, with two Ironcross clerks behind him and a woman in Synod black with a writ in her hands, and the gold lamps stayed outside in the lane, so that the light came through the whisperglass and the mesh in long cold bars across the floor. The Shrouds stayed outside too. Isola could see one through the window, standing in the middle of the street, not facing the house, not facing anything. Inside its circle, the rain fell without a sound.

The woman in black read the writ.

It was long. It began with a prayer, the one stamped around the edge of every Veilmark Isola had ever touched, *The Veil protects*, and then went on in language that sounded like it had been written in Crownlight by somebody who'd never been within six tiers of Ashvein. A household audit. Sixth Ward. By order of the Synod, in light of irregular circulation of currency recorded at an establishment in Shadowmere known as Blue Circle, and in light of a courier entering that establishment who was recorded as belonging to this ward. Every household to present its registry. Every name to be read against its record. Any irregularity to be noted and answered.

Omari stood in the middle of the front room with his hands at his sides and listened to all of it, and didn't move. He didn't touch the walls. He didn't reach for the panels he'd built years ago, the ones that could seal this house shut in the time it took to blink. Isola watched him not reach for them. You didn't seal against the Synod. You didn't do anything against the Synod but stand there and let them read.

"Registry," the reader said.

Omari gestured at the wall.

The reader went to it. He looked at the name chains for a long moment, four generations of Wraithmoors in thread and ink and pressed metal, and Isola thought she saw something cross his thin face, something that might have been surprise, or might have been contempt. Then he opened his white ledger, and set it on the arm of the couch, and began to read.

He read each name aloud. Then he laid the end of a long, pale stylus against the strip on the wall that held it, and waited, and watched.

"Omari Kade Wraithmoor."

Under the stylus, the strip held. Isola could see it from across the room, the funeral thread drawing taut through her father's name, the faint warmth of it coming off the metal.

"Selah Maris Wraithmoor."

It held.

"Isola Amara Renae Wraithmoor."

Isola felt it. She'd never felt it before, not from this side, not from somebody else reading it. A small tug, low in her chest, like somebody had put a finger on her heart and pressed. Then it was gone. The reader made a mark in his ledger.

"Micah Amari Wraithmoor."

Micah, standing beside her, flinched.

The reader made a mark. He kept making it, longer than he'd made any of the others. Then he turned and looked at one of the Ironcross clerks, and the clerk opened a smaller ledger of his own and ran his finger down a column and nodded.

"Micah Amari Wraithmoor," the reader said again, not to the wall this time, but to the room. "An Ironcross sponsorship packet was filed in this name yesterday at noon. Sponsor of record, Danika Wraithmoor, Ironcross." He looked at Micah. "Sponsorships are not processed for households under audit."

The clerk took out a stamp. He opened a page in his ledger and set the stamp down on it with a sound like a door closing.

"Void," the clerk said.

Micah didn't make a sound. Isola didn't look at him. She couldn't. She felt him go still beside her, the whole thirty days going out of him all at once, twenty-nine and a morning, three work chits and two letters from his foreman and fourteen Veilmarks to the noon courier, and she reached over without looking and took his hand, and held it, hard, and he let her.

"Tevin Malachi Wraithmoor."

It held.

"Asha Noelle Wraithmoor."

Isola felt it go.

She felt it from across the room, the way she'd felt it under her fingertips in the dark two nights ago. The funeral thread through Asha's name, going slack. Not breaking. Just letting go, soft and terrible, like a rope somebody had let fall at the other end. The reader's stylus rested against the strip, and the strip lay there under it doing nothing at all, and in about one more breath, he was going to see it.

Isola didn't think.

She let go of Micah's hand and put both of hers under the edge of the table beside her, out of sight, flat against the underside of the wood. And she said her cousin's name.

She said it the way Omari had taught them, so low it was almost nothing, so low it was barely breath, with her lips hardly moving and her eyes on the floor. Each part of it set down carefully before the next. *Asha. Noelle. Wraithmoor.* The cadence her father used for the wall and nowhere else. The words weren't anything. Saying them right was everything.

The thread caught.

She felt it catch. She felt it tighten, slow and stubborn, like hauling a full bucket up out of a cistern with your bare hands. It didn't want to. Whatever had let go at the other end of that name was still letting go, and Isola was the only thing holding on, and she held on. She said it again, under her breath. *Asha Noelle Wraithmoor.* She felt the warmth come up into the strip on the wall from across the room, thin and faint and fragile as frost on glass. She held it.

The reader looked at the strip for a long moment. He looked at Asha, standing by the window in her coat. He looked back at the strip.

It held.

He made a mark in his ledger and moved on.

Isola didn't let go. Not right away. She kept her hands flat under the table and kept the cadence going under her breath, over and over, all through the rest of the reading, all through the last few names on the wall that belonged to people long dead and couldn't be read for anything. Her fingers had gone cold. Not chilly. Cold, deep in the bones, the kind of cold that came off her mother's hands when Selah was having one of her days. A headache had started behind her eyes, a slow, pressing ache that built and built until she could feel her pulse in it. She held on anyway.

She was so angry she could hardly see.

At Asha, standing there by the window in a coat she couldn't afford, with her bags packed in the next room and her name trying to leave this wall in front of a Synod reader. At Asha, who'd put her hand over the pocket and said *I'll go out the vent*, and meant it, and had made Isola choose between this house and a man who'd answered in under a minute. At Asha, who'd done this to all of them, to Micah and his thirty days, to Selah with frost at her wrist, to Omari standing in the middle of his own front room not reaching for his own walls.

She held her cousin's name on the wall the whole time she was hating her for it.

The reader closed his white ledger.

"Asha Noelle Wraithmoor," he said, and Isola felt it in her chest, and held. "The courier recorded entering the establishment known as Blue Circle is recorded as belonging to this household. You will present yourself at the ward hall at third bell today and explain your business there." He looked at Asha. "Do you understand?"

"I understand," Asha said.

Her voice was perfectly calm.

The reader nodded. The woman in black rolled up her writ. The Ironcross clerks closed their ledgers. And the three of them went out the way they'd come, into the gold, and down the lane to the next door, where they knocked once, and waited.

Nobody in the Wraithmoor front room moved. Isola let go.

It went out of her all at once, like setting down something too heavy, and her hands came out from under the table and lay in her lap, shaking, white at the fingertips with cold. The headache throbbed behind her eyes. Across the room, the strip with Asha's name on it went quiet on the wall, and Isola didn't look at it, because she didn't want to know whether it was still holding without her.

She looked at Asha instead.

Her cousin was standing at the front window with her back to the room, looking out through the mesh at the lane. The gold lamps were moving on, door by door. The Shroud in the middle of the street hadn't moved at all. The rain fell around it without a sound.

Asha wasn't looking at any of that. She was looking down and to the left, at the narrow grating set into the wall beside the front step where the vent from the kitchen came out into the lane. It was the oldest vent in the house. It led down through the wall and under the street and into the trench routes that ran under all of Sixth Ward, and every child in this family had been told a hundred times never to go near it.

Asha looked at it for a long time.

And she smiled.
