# CHAPTER 4: ISOLA

The noon courier took fourteen Veilmarks, two more than Isola had meant to pay. The route inspectors were out. She'd known they might be, and she'd brought the fourteen, and it still felt like being robbed by somebody with manners.

He was a thin man with an Ironcross stamp on the back of his hand, and he checked the seal on Micah's packet twice before sliding it into the flat case at his hip. Isola counted the coins into his palm one at a time. She'd never listened to money before this morning. Now each one rang against the one beneath it on the way down, low and full, and she didn't let go of a single coin until she'd heard it.

Micah stood in the doorway behind her with his hands in his jacket, as if he could make the packet travel faster by staring at it. When the courier stepped back into the lane, Micah let his shoulders down for the first time since first bell.

"That's it?"

"That's it." Isola shut the door. "Now you just gotta not do nothing stupid until Danika write back."

"I been not doing nothing stupid for thirty days."

"Twenty-nine and a morning." His eyes went to his phone on the table, and Isola picked it up before he could. "And you still ain't getting this."

He sucked his teeth and dropped onto the couch, which meant he'd given up for now. With Micah, *for now* usually lasted until he got hungry. Isola looked up at the clock over the name wall. The minute hand had slipped past twelve while the courier counted.

Noon had come. Asha hadn't.

That was the deal Isola had made with herself in Asha's room that morning, standing over a torn bag with a counterfeit in her hand. If Asha came home before the courier, Isola would ask her to her face. If she didn't, Isola would take the salt run into Shadowmere and see what she could find out down there. She'd been hoping for the first one. The clock didn't care.

Asha's door was still open down the hall, the way Isola had left it. She went in and shut it behind her.

The bag stood where she'd put it against the wall, torn pocket turned toward the plaster. She'd folded the strap under it before she walked out, and the strap was still folded. Nobody had touched it. Isola worked two fingers through the tear and felt along the lining until the coin was there, cold and a little too light, and drew it out. She didn't look at it again. She'd already looked at it enough.

She stood there with it closed in her fist and made herself think it through one more time, because she only got to decide this once. If she left it here, it stayed under Omari's roof with every name on that wall standing beside it. If she took it with her, the only person it could tie itself to was the one who'd chosen to pick it up. And if Asha came home and went feeling for it, she'd find the lining empty and know somebody had been in her things.

Isola wanted her to know that.

She slid the coin into the hidden pocket of her skirt, behind the seam where she kept the money, and stood the bag back up so the tear wouldn't show from the doorway. The rest of Asha's bags waited in their row beside it, packed and buckled, the way they'd been waiting for two years. Isola had quit asking what they were for a long time ago. Standing in the middle of the room with High Heresy against her hip, she wished she'd been the kind of cousin who kept asking.

In the kitchen, Selah was packing roots into a jar. The humming had started back up sometime after breakfast, the same six notes, and it hadn't let up long enough for Isola to decide whether that was better or worse.

"I'm going for the salt."

Selah wiped her hands on her apron and drew the order slip out of her pocket. She didn't hand it over right away. She held it a second and looked at Isola the same way she'd looked at her over the dishes, long and level and not asking anything out loud, then laid it in her palm.

"Pell," Selah said. "Not nobody else."

"I know."

"Funeral grade."

"Mama. I know."

Selah went back to the jar. "Then you ain't got nothing to worry about."

Selah didn't repeat herself. When she did, she wasn't talking about the thing she was saying. Isola put the slip in her front pocket, where she'd see it every time she reached for money, and set Micah's phone face down on the counter. Selah looked at the phone, then at her, and moved the jar so it sat right on top of it. It was as good as a lock.

Tevin's door was shut. Isola didn't knock, because knocking would only tell him she was leaving. She checked the front room on her way out instead. His route coat was still hanging off the chair where he'd dropped it. Good.

The token Mother Rae had given her for runs hung on a cord under her shirt. It was a flat brass piece, worn soft at the edges, with marks on its face Rae had never taught her to read. Isola had never asked. Rae answered questions when Rae felt like it, and that wasn't often enough to waste one on a piece of brass that worked fine without being understood. She pulled the cord out in the lane and kept the token in her fist all the way down the cliff stairs, past the shrine at the second landing, where somebody had left a cup of tea for the dead that was already grey with dust, and past the women hauling water up from the vent cisterns. One of them asked after Selah without breaking stride. Isola told her Selah was fine without breaking hers. The stairs cost nothing and saved her the lift fare. She'd pay for the lift coming back if the salt was heavy.

The chasm bridge was loud with people trying to get across before the mist came up. It had come up twice already today, according to the boy selling grounding charms at the head of the span, and the second time, three people had stood frozen in the middle for ten minutes because they couldn't see the rail. Isola could see the rail. She could see the whole bridge, the black stone arching over the vent, the violet glow breathing up out of the dark beneath it, and the Shadowmere guard post at the far end with its lamp and its line.

The line moved slowly. The guard was a big man in a mercenary's coat with half the buttons gone, and he had a route scanner on a chain and a metal plate set into the counter in front of him. The woman ahead of Isola paid at the plate. She pricked her thumb on the needle beside it without being told and pressed it down, and the guard watched the metal drink until it had enough. The boy behind her either didn't have blood to spare or didn't want to give it. He gave a name instead, a crew name Isola recognized from the east gate, and swore to run for them whenever they called. The guard wrote it in his book without looking up.

Isola knew that crew. She watched the boy walk out onto the bridge like he'd paid with something he could earn back, and she hoped somebody at home loved him enough to be mad about it.

She'd bled on that plate herself before Mother Rae gave her the cord.

She stepped up and opened her fist. The guard looked at the token before he passed the scanner over it. It made the short flat sound she'd been waiting for, and he lifted his chin toward Shadowmere without asking where she was going or what she had in her skirt. She kept her free hand on her bag strap the whole time, nowhere near the pocket. She didn't know whether a scanner that read tokens could read anything else. She wasn't about to find out by touching her own hip in front of it.

Isola didn't like Shadowmere.

She'd never had to explain that to anybody at home, because nobody at home liked it either. In Ashvein, a crew wore its colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were on before you stood on it, and if you didn't, somebody's auntie would tell you from a window and then tell your mama you'd needed telling. Shadowmere didn't have aunties in windows. It had neon and violet mist and rain falling out of solid rock, and people who smiled at you while they figured out what you were worth. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

*Running errands or something*, Selah had said about Asha this morning. Isola had a counterfeit in her skirt that said otherwise. If Asha's errands ran anywhere, they ran through here.

That was why she went to the assayer before she went to Pell. If she was going to walk around Shadowmere looking for her cousin, she needed to know exactly what her cousin had left behind in that bag.

The woman kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain didn't reach. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Mother Rae and watching the woman weigh it on a little brass scale with weights no bigger than teeth. She could weigh anything. People brought her scrapings, filings, and coins that had been through fire, and she put on her loupes and told them what it was worth. Isola had never once heard her ask where any of it came from.

She was weighing something now for a man in a dock coat who was arguing with the result. Isola waited until he lost and left, then stepped up, set her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over.

The coin lay in the middle of her palm. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody passing could see more than a corner.

The assayer looked at it. Then she put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it. "I don't weigh High Heresy."

The man at the next counter, selling charm wire, stopped talking to his customer, and the customer stopped listening to him.

"Get it off my counter."

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

She walked away without hurrying, because hurrying in the Back Market was the same as asking to be followed. She'd known what the coin was since Asha's room. Knowing it by herself had been one thing. Hearing a woman who never asked questions call it by its name out loud, in front of strangers, was something else, and the words followed her out from under the arch.

High Heresy meant the square. It meant the woman from the mining district on her knees while the Synod took her breath, and her fingers scraping at the stone long after there was any point to it. Isola had been a girl when she watched that, standing with her mother's hand clamped on her shoulder so she couldn't turn away. For one second, coming out into the rain, the fingers on that stone were Asha's. Isola had to stop walking.

She let the rain hit her face until the picture went somewhere else. Then she went looking for Pell, because the salt was the reason she was down here, and it was the only thing on her list today she still knew how to do right.

His stall stood in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods and the counters took up half the walking room. Pell sat under his heat lamp the way he always did, three braids in his beard resting on his belly, rings on every finger catching the amber light whenever he lifted one to point at somebody's purse. He had a scale nobody else was allowed to touch, a drawer he kept locked, and a mouth that never stopped running, which was usually the only annoying thing about him. Mother Rae bought her funeral grade from him because he kept it dry and didn't cut it. Selah had made that very clear the first time she sent Isola down here alone, and she'd made it clearer when Isola came home and admitted what Pell had charged. Pell had a price for Shadowmere and a price for Ashvein, and he'd never once been ashamed of either.

Usually, by the time Isola got to the counter, he'd asked after Selah, complained about his knees, and tried to sell her something she hadn't come for.

"Rae's girl," Pell said. He was already reaching. "What she need?"

He hadn't asked about Selah. Isola set the slip down. He read it, nodded, and scooped from the open barrel at his elbow into a square of brown paper.

She watched the scoop go in and come out. The salt was grey and coarse, with dark grit caught between the grains, and it clumped where the damp had gotten into it. Isola had been packing salt into Selah's windows since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them. She'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name: fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it was trying to get away from you. Pell kept it in the locked drawer under the counter, ground fresh every morning, and every time Isola came for Rae, he'd unlocked that drawer without being asked.

He was folding the brown paper.

"That ain't funeral grade."

Pell didn't look up. "It's what I got today."

"That's quarry salt."

"It's salt."

"It's quarry salt, and it's wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop." She put her hand flat on the slip. "Rae's slip say funeral grade."

"Rae can come get it herself."

He'd never said anything like that in front of her. Pell talked too much, sure, but he talked the way people did when they wanted to keep you happy, not the way they did when they wanted you gone. Isola looked at his hands. They were folding the paper too carefully, the corners squared up over and over, the way people kept their hands busy so their faces didn't have to do anything. She pulled the cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he'd have to look at it.

"She sent me," Isola said. "Same as last time. Same as the time before that. Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today."

Pell stopped folding.

He looked past her. Isola knew better than to turn around. She'd grown up in a house where you learned what was behind you by watching the face in front of you, and Pell's face had just gone blank as a wall.

Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end, and the other stood right beside her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word to Pell or to her. In Ashvein, she'd have known whose men they were by their sleeves before they ever reached the counter. These two wore plain dark coats and the kind of patience a person only had if they'd never once been told no at a counter.

Pell reached under the counter, farther back than the drawer. He came out with an envelope and set it down. The man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking at it. The man at the end of the counter looked at Isola's hand instead, the one still holding Rae's token out in the open, and he kept looking long enough that she felt it all the way up her arm. She closed her fist over the brass. He followed the first man out of the stall, and the two of them went up the lane toward the bend without hurrying.

Pell didn't unlock the drawer until they'd been gone a good while, and when he finally did, his hands were shaking hard enough that the key took two tries.

Isola dropped the cord back under her shirt while she waited. *Nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something.* Pell owed it something. She'd just watched him pay.

He took out the morning cut in its grey paper and weighed it out, and when he went to fold the packet, a thin stream of white ran off the edge of the scale and onto the counter. Pell watched it go and didn't say a word.

Isola had never seen him not say anything about anything.

"Who was that?"

"Who?"

She waited.

"Rae account," Pell said. His letters wandered across the line as he logged it in his book. "Eighteen. Tell her I said hey."

Eighteen was robbery, and the both of them knew it. On any other day she'd have stood there and argued him down to fourteen just to watch him sweat. Today she looked at the grit still sitting in the brown paper he'd tried to hand her, and she saw it all the way through. Pell hadn't been cheating Rae. He'd been trying to get Rae's girl gone before those two walked up and found her at his counter with Rae's token around her neck. *Rae can come get it herself* had been the only warning he could afford to give.

She couldn't prove a bit of it. She took the packet anyway, and she didn't argue the eighteen. That was the only thanks Pell was getting.

"You want me to tell her who was here when I came for her salt?"

He didn't answer that. His eyes went up the lane, toward where the men had gone, and then came back down and found something on his scale that needed looking at.

Isola followed the look up the lane.

Asha was standing at the bend.

Isola knew her by how she stood before she could see her face: weight back, head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her, the way you stood when somebody had told you exactly where to be and you were scared of getting it wrong. Isola had never seen Asha stand like that anywhere she was supposed to be. The coat was dark, fitted close at the waist, falling to her knees. A structured tote hung off one shoulder, stiff-sided and new, the kind of bag that held its shape whether anything was in it or not. Isola had never seen either one in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Bags like that too. She thought about the row of packed bags against Asha's wall, and the coin against her own hip, and she didn't like how easily it all fit.

The bend sat a hair past Gutterlight's back wall, close enough that the crowd noise came through the stone in waves. Asha turned her head toward it. She was about to go.

Isola stepped away from the counter with the packet in her hand.

"Move!"

A boy came down the lane at a dead run, elbows out, a route coat flapping open behind him, and in front of her, a man stepped back from the freight wall to get out of his way and put his whole back straight into her.

He didn't knock her down. She'd been knocked down by bigger, and her feet stayed where they were. But the packet left her hand on the hit, and she watched it go, turning once in the air, and burst against the wet stone at her feet. White salt went everywhere. It hit the puddles and went grey on contact, sinking into the black water like it had never been anything at all.

One bumps. One runs. You find out what's gone when you get home. Every story Isola had ever heard about the Back Market went like that, and her hand was already in her skirt before she'd finished thinking it, fingers closed over the coin, holding it so hard the edge bit.

"Damn, my bad," the boy called over his shoulder, already past the bend.

Isola looked down at the salt. Then she looked up at the man. And up.

He was tall. Not regular tall. She had to tip her head back to get all of him, and she was too mad to be embarrassed about it. Somebody had hit his face recently. There was a swelling high under one eye, a split in his bottom lip, and a bandage wrapped across the knuckles of his right hand, white gone grey at the edges. Rain had soaked his locs and kept coming off them, down past his ear and into his beard, and he didn't wipe it.

"You've got to be fucking kidding me," Isola said, staring at the puddle where the salt had already gone dark. "That's eighteen Veils dissolving in that puddle."

"Eighteen?" The man glanced past her at Pell. His voice was low and unbothered, like he had all the time in the world and hadn't just cost her a whole afternoon. "You robbing folks in daylight now?"

Pell lifted both hands, rings flashing. "Price is the price, Graves. Ashvein orders come with a fee."

*Graves.* Isola filed it. It wasn't the kind of name people got from their mamas.

Behind the man, something shifted against the freight wall. A hooded figure, small, pressed flat to the brick. Isola only got a corner of it before he moved. The hood was down low, but the lamp from the next stall caught one curl that had slipped loose at the edge, and the curl was red. Not dyed red. Not rust. Red, like the inside of a coal.

"Pell," he said, without turning around. "Bag her another one. Put it on Camille."

Pell's eyebrows climbed his whole forehead. "Camille know she paying for this?"

Isola did not know who Camille was. Either Camille was this man's woman and the girl at the wall was something else entirely, or the girl at the wall was Camille, or he was a whole lot more trouble than his face already said he was. None of the possibilities improved her afternoon.

He dug into his coat with his good hand and came out with four Veilmarks. He set them on Pell's counter one at a time, so the quicksilver caught the lamp. "Bag her another one."

Pell looked at the coins. Then he looked at the man's face, long enough to place it, and whatever he found there shifted something under his beard. "Don't got another morning cut."

"Then give her the evening cut early."

Pell sighed like he'd been asked to carry the whole tier up the cliff stairs on his back, and reached beneath the counter.

Four Veilmarks. Pell had put her down for eighteen on Rae's account not five minutes ago, for the same salt, and he'd just taken four off this man without so much as arguing the difference. Isola stared at the coins sitting on the counter and decided she was going to have words with Pell about it someday, and that today wasn't that day, and that she hated it.

She'd crouched to look at the salt without remembering doing it. She stood back up, hand still in her skirt, and looked at him properly this time. She took her time about it. She didn't see why she shouldn't. He'd taken her whole packet; she could take a minute. The swollen eye. The lip. The wrapped hand, which somebody who knew what they were doing had tied, because nobody got a bandage that neat with one hand. He was fine. She noticed that the way you noticed weather, without deciding to, and she didn't appreciate it any more than she appreciated the rain. He had the kind of face that would've been easy to look at even without somebody having already tried to rearrange it, and he was standing there letting her look like he knew exactly what she was doing and didn't mind.

She looked past him again for the red curl.

He shifted half a step left, easy as anything, and put his shoulder right in her line of sight.

Isola knew a block when she saw one. She'd grown up with Tevin. Whatever that was at the wall, he didn't want her looking at it, and he wasn't going to say so.

She went back to his face, and her chin came up. "I ain't ask you to pay for it. I ain't trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere. Everyone knows how y'all get down."

He almost smiled. She saw it start in the corner of his mouth, saw the split lip stop it, and saw him decide it was worth the pain anyway for about half a second. "I knocked it out your hand. Debt's mine."

He said it the way people said things that were already settled, not like he was offering. Isola had a whole answer ready for a man trying to make her owe him. She didn't have one for a man who'd just taken the owing for himself and left her standing there with nothing to argue.

Pell slapped a fresh packet on the counter, wrapped twice in waxed paper. Isola took it with her free hand. The other one stayed in her skirt. She wasn't letting go of that coin for anybody, and certainly not for a man named after a cemetery.

"Next time," she said, "watch where you backing up."

"Next time, stand somewhere else."

Oh, she had something for that. She had several things for that. She could feel all of them lined up behind her teeth, and she looked at him and decided he hadn't earned a single one, and she could see that he knew it, and that he was enjoying it. That was the worst part.

She tucked the packet under her arm, stepped around the dark patch on the stone, and went up the lane toward the bend.

She didn't look back. She wanted to. She could feel the exact spot between her shoulders where she'd be able to feel him watching if he was watching, and she wasn't going to give him the satisfaction of finding out whether he was. She had a cousin to catch.

The bend was empty.

Two runners went across it with a box between them, arguing about which door they'd been paid to use. Behind them there was nothing but the lane curving away toward the noise, and the noise. Isola stood exactly where Asha had been standing and turned in a slow circle, and nobody in a fitted coat was anywhere.

Ten steps. Maybe fifteen. That was how close she'd been.

She checked the crossing and the doorways on both sides and the mouth of the next alley, where the drainage ran ankle-deep and stank of dye. She walked the length of Gutterlight's back wall and looked into every doorway that would let a body stand out of the rain. She kept at it until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her back teeth, and by then the crowd at the bend had doubled, and Asha could have been anywhere in it or nowhere near it.

Isola stood in the rain with the packet under her arm and her hand still in her skirt, and she was so angry she couldn't decide who to aim it at.

Asha first. Asha for the coat and the bag and the coin and the two years of packed luggage. Pell second, for eighteen. The two men in the dark coats third, for whatever they were. Then the boy with his elbows out. Then the man.

*Graves.*

He hadn't known what he'd cost her. She knew that. He'd backed into her getting out of a running boy's way, and he'd paid for the salt without making a show of it, and he'd kept his body between her and whatever was at that wall like it was the most natural thing in the world. None of that made her feel any better about the fact that she'd been fifteen steps from her cousin and she'd spent them looking at a man's face.

It was a good face. That wasn't the point.

She took the lift home. The salt wasn't heavy, but she paid the fare anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might've decided to follow a girl with Rae's token and a coin nobody would weigh.

The lift groaned up the shaft with eleven people pressed into a cage built for eight. Somebody's elbow sat in her ribs the whole way. Isola kept the packet flat against her chest and her hand over her skirt, right where the seam bunched around the coin, and the operator called out the levels without looking up from his lever. Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.

She spent the whole ride irritated by six seconds of him.

Six seconds was about how long she'd stood there looking at him like she had the right, and about how long he'd stood there letting her. She went back over *Debt's mine* and couldn't find anything wrong with it, which was its own problem. She went back over *Next time, stand somewhere else* and found plenty wrong with that, and found herself almost smiling at it, and quit. She thought about the way he'd shifted half a step without anybody asking him to. She thought about the rain going into his beard.

*Graves.* It wasn't a name. It was a warning people had decided to call somebody.

Somewhere past Service Shaft, she caught her thumb working one of her rings around and around her finger. She made herself stop. By Ashvein West, she was doing it again.

By the time the gate opened, the rain on her coat had started drying into dust. Coal smoke hung low over the lane, and she'd missed at least one bell while she was gone. The boys from across the street had disappeared from under their lantern. Isola trusted the empty spot less than she'd trusted the boys standing in it. Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge, and she looked at that little pile longer than a pile deserved.

The lantern above the door still burned amber.

She stood under it a moment longer than she meant to. Then she knocked the dust off her boots against the frame and let herself in. Micah was asleep on the couch with one arm hanging off the cushion and his shoes still on. He'd given up on the day, but not on the shoes. Tevin's coat hadn't moved from the chair, and his door was still shut. She listened at it on her way past and heard him breathing on the other side, slow and even, the way he breathed when he was pretending not to be awake. Good. He could pretend all evening.

Asha's door was open. Isola didn't go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she'd left it. If Asha had come home, she'd come and gone without touching it.

In the kitchen, Selah was at the stove. She turned when Isola came in and held out both hands for the salt before Isola reached the counter. "Pell?"

"Pell."

Selah turned the packet over. "This ain't the morning cut."

"Evening cut." Isola set her bag down. "He tried me with quarry salt first."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Her mother untied the waxed paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. It ran the way it was supposed to. She looked at the double wrap a while longer than she'd looked at the salt, then nodded once and folded it closed again.

"You find your cousin?"

Selah had told her this morning not to go looking. Now she hadn't asked whether Isola had looked. She'd asked whether she'd found her. Isola reached for a rag that didn't need reaching for. "Saw her. Lost her in the Market."

"Lost her how?"

A coat Isola couldn't have bought with three months of courier fees. A bag to go with it. A coin the assayer wouldn't touch. Two men who never said a word, and Pell, quiet. And a man called Graves who'd backed into her at the worst possible second and then stood there like he'd been glad to.

"Crowd," Isola said.

It was the second time in one day she'd handed one of her parents a word that was only mostly true.

Selah held her stare long enough that Isola wiped the same spot on the counter twice. Then her mother turned back to the stove, and after a moment, the humming started again.

"Wash up," Selah said over it. "Food almost ready."

Isola went to the sink and turned on the water. She held her hands under it and watched the Shadowmere rain go down the drain with the red dust from the stairs, and she prayed that whatever Asha had gotten herself into, it wouldn't end with her back in Shadowmere.
