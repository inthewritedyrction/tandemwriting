# CHAPTER 7: SILAS

Silas woke up on the floor with his back against the door and no memory of deciding to sleep.

His neck had locked at an angle God had not intended. His shoulder had set overnight into something closer to stone than muscle, and his right hand, inside Camille's wrap, had moved past burning into a deep, steady throb that kept time with his pulse. He sat still for a moment and took inventory the way he would've after a fight. Stomach steady. Hands, one and a half. Nothing broken that hadn't already been broken yesterday.

The house was quiet. The lamp had burned down to almost nothing. Across the room, the new line of thread under the window still held, dark and whole, and the bedroom door still hung crooked off its one good hinge.

Melena was asleep at the table.

She'd folded her arms on the wood and put her head down on them, and at some point in the night she'd pulled Dante's coat off the chair and dragged it over her shoulders like a blanket. The letter was under her hand. Even asleep, she had her fingers spread over it. Her hair had come all the way loose and lay across her arms and the table in a heavy red sheet, and in the low light, it was the only color in the room.

He'd sent Remy the address around the time the lamp started to go. He'd sat there with the phone in his good hand for a long time first, thinking about Dante's *she ain't yours* and Camille's *a week*, and about how he was a man with one working arm guarding a door against a girl who could walk through his mother's thread, and in the end he'd typed the street and the house and two more words, and sent it before he could talk himself out of it.

Silas: Bring food.

Remy hadn't answered. Remy hadn't needed to.

He knocked at first bell.

Silas knew it was him by the knock, three short and one long, the same knock he'd been using on every door between them since they were fourteen. He got himself up off the floor in stages, one hand on the frame, and by the time he got the door open, Melena had jerked awake at the table and was sitting bolt upright with her hand flat over the letter and her eyes wide and hot.

"It's all right," Silas told her. "He's mine."

Remy stood on the step under the awning with a paper sack in one hand and a lidded jar in the other, rain beading on the shoulders of his green coat. His locs were tied back off his face, and the silver cuff on his ear caught what little light the street had. He had his messenger bag across his chest like always, and he looked at Silas the way he always looked at him the morning after anything, top to bottom, cataloguing.

"You look terrible," Remy said.

"You say that every time."

"Every time it's true." Remy's eyes went to the white mitt of Silas's hand and stopped there. "And that's new."

"Work."

"Camille wrap that?"

"Who else."

Remy's mouth went sideways. Remy's mama and Camille had known each other since before either of their sons could walk, and Remy had grown up getting fussed over and fed and occasionally smacked by Camille Graves the same as if he'd been hers. He knew her wraps. He knew what it meant when one went all the way to the wrist. He didn't say anything else about it, which meant he was saving it.

He looked past Silas into the room, and Silas watched him find the window, and the burned line under it, and the bedroom door, and finally the girl at the table with the coat around her shoulders and her hair down and her eyes still too bright. Remy's face did something Silas had seen it do a few times in his life and never gotten tired of watching. It got gentle. Not soft, not stupid. Just gentle, all at once, like a light somebody had dimmed so you wouldn't have to squint.

"Morning," Remy said to her. He didn't step in. He didn't offer his hand. He just held up the paper sack. "I brought bread. It's from Odette's by the Third Lane tram, so it's real bread, not that sawdust they sell by the Ring. And there's tea, but I put honey in it, so if you don't like honey, I'm sorry, that's on me."

Melena looked at him like he'd spoken a language she used to know.

"This is Remy," Silas said. "Remy, Melena."

"Melena," Remy said, like he was setting it down somewhere careful. "Can I come in?"

It took Silas a second to understand that Remy wasn't asking him.

Melena's hand moved on the letter. She looked at Remy, at the bread, at the coat on his shoulders and the bag across his chest and his hands, both of which he was holding where she could see them without anybody having told him to. Then she nodded.

Remy came in. He set the sack and the jar on the counter by the cold stove, not on the table, and started unpacking it with his back half turned to her, which Silas understood after a moment was on purpose. He talked the whole time. He talked about the line at Odette's and the old man who'd tried to cut it and the woman who'd stopped him with one look. He talked about the rain, which he said had been coming down sideways on the Third Lane platform like it had somewhere better to be. He found three cups in the cabinet and rinsed them and poured the tea, and the room filled up with the smell of honey and something smoky, and somewhere in all that talking, Melena's shoulders came down from around her ears.

Silas leaned against the doorframe and let him do it. He'd known Remy his whole life. He'd watched him talk a crying runner off a ledge and a Bookkeeper out of a beating and Silas himself out of more bad decisions than either of them would ever say out loud. He just hadn't watched him do it to a stranger before, and he found he didn't have a word for what it was like to stand there and see it work.

"You want bread first," Remy asked her, "or you want tea first, or you want me to stop talking first? Because I can do all three. The third one's the hardest, but I can do it."

Something happened at the corner of Melena's mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile would've been if she'd let it.

"Tea," she said.

---

It went well for a while. Long enough that Silas started to believe it would keep going well, which was always his first mistake.

Remy brought the tea to the table and set it down and then sat down across from her, not beside her, with the bread between them. Silas stayed in the doorway with his own cup and his bad arm hanging, close enough to move, far enough not to crowd. Melena drank half the tea and ate a piece of the bread, slowly, pulling it apart with her fingers like she was trying to remember whether she was hungry. Remy didn't watch her eat. He ate his own and looked at the window and told her what each of the stitches in Camille's thread line meant, which was a lie, because Remy didn't know what the stitches meant any more than Silas did, but he told it so well that Silas almost believed it.

Then Melena moved her hand and looked down at the letter.

She'd forgotten it was under her palm. Silas saw the exact second she remembered. Her fingers spread across the page, and her face changed, and he knew the shape of what was coming before it came because he'd watched it come twice already.

The tea in her cup started to steam harder.

"Hey," Remy said.

The heat came up off the table in a wave. It pushed against Silas's face across the room. The lamp flickered. The paper under Melena's hand began to curl at its corners, and black crept out from under her nails and up the ends of her fingers like ink in water, and she looked down at it and made a small sound that Silas would remember for a long time afterward, a sound like somebody watching their own house burn from across the street.

Silas pushed off the doorframe.

"Don't," Remy said, without looking at him. Not loud. He said it the way you'd say it to somebody about to step on a loose stair.

Silas stopped.

Remy didn't get up. He didn't reach for her. He slid his chair back a little, and then he slid off it and crouched beside the table, low, below her eye line, a good arm's length away. He dug into his coat pocket and came out with one of the mints he'd been handing out to everybody in Shadowmere since they were boys, and he unwrapped it, slow, and held up the empty wrapper between two fingers so it caught the light.

"Melena," he said. "What color is this?"

She was breathing too fast to answer. The heat climbed another notch. Silas felt it in his teeth.

"Just the color," Remy said. "You don't have to do anything else. What color?"

Her eyes went to the wrapper. It took her a moment.

"Silver."

"Silver. That's right." Remy set the wrapper down on the floor between them like an offering. "What color's my coat?"

She looked at him. "Green."

"Green. Good. Now him." Remy tipped his head toward the door without taking his eyes off her. "What's he got on?"

Melena's gaze came across the room to Silas, unfocused, burning. Silas didn't move. He didn't do anything. He just stood there in his shirt from yesterday and let her look.

"Black," she said. "His shirt's black."

"It's always black. He thinks it makes him look dangerous." Remy didn't smile when he said it, but something in his voice did. "Where are you?"

"A house."

"Whose house?"

Her breath hitched. "His mother's."

"That's right. Who's in the room?"

"You. Him."

"Who else?"

She looked around. The heat wavered. "Nobody."

"Nobody," Remy agreed. "Nobody else in this room but us, and the bread, and that thread line his mama's gon' kill him over. Put your feet flat. Both of them. Push your heels down. You feel the floor?"

She did it. Silas watched her do it, watched her plant Rhea's old boots on the boards and press down like she was trying to push through them.

"Good," Remy said. "Now just listen."

He put his hand flat on the floorboards beside the silver wrapper and tapped. Six beats, but not even ones. Two close together, then a pause, then one, then three that came quick and uneven like somebody stumbling and catching themselves. Then he did it again. Same six. Same stumble.

Silas didn't know where Remy had learned it. He'd never seen him do it before in his life. But he watched Melena's eyes go to Remy's hand on the floor, and he watched her breathing slow down to try to match it, and he watched the black start to draw back from the ends of her fingers like a tide going out.

The third time through, the heat in the room broke. It didn't vanish. It just let go, all at once, the way a fist lets go, and the lamp steadied, and the steam coming off her tea thinned back down to what it was supposed to be.

Melena sat there with both hands flat on the table and her heels pressed into the floor, breathing.

"There you go," Remy said softly. He picked up the silver wrapper and folded it in half and set it on the table in front of her, next to her cup. "That's yours. Keep that."

She looked at it. Then she picked it up and held it in her fist.

Silas stood in the doorway and didn't know what he felt.

He'd caught her in Dante's office and she'd told him not to touch her. He'd covered her in the alley and burned his hand to the bone and she'd told him not to ask her to say thank you. He'd done everything he knew how to do, and he knew how to do a lot, and every bit of it had been him getting between her and something. He'd never once thought to ask her what color his shirt was.

Remy had walked in with bread and done it in five minutes, kneeling on the floor.

Silas didn't resent him for it. He didn't think he did. He was relieved, mostly, the way you were relieved when somebody else got the stuck door open after you'd been throwing your shoulder at it for an hour. But there was something else in there too, something that sat lower and didn't have a clean edge, and he didn't feel like looking at it in front of either of them. He drank his tea, which had gone cold, and kept his face where it was.

"What was that?" he asked Remy finally. "The tapping."

Remy stood up, one knee popping, and sat back down in his chair. He didn't answer right away. He reached for his own cup and turned it around in his hands, and his other hand went to the ward-thread bracelet on his wrist and rubbed at it with his thumb, the way it always did when he was thinking about something he didn't want to think about.

"Something my mama used to do," he said. "When we were little. Storm nights." He shrugged. "Seemed like the kind of thing that might work."

Silas didn't ask anything else. He knew which *we* Remy meant, and he knew better than to make him say it.

---

Melena ate the rest of the bread after that. She ate it like she'd just found out she was allowed to, and Remy kept talking, and at some point in the next half hour, while Remy was explaining with great seriousness that the old man at Odette's had tried to cut the line because he claimed his wife was dying and then had been seen not ten minutes later buying a second loaf for her, Melena laughed.

It wasn't much. It was short, and it surprised her as much as it surprised either of them, and she put her hand over her mouth afterward like she'd broken something. But Silas heard it from the doorway, and he saw Remy hear it, and he saw what happened to Remy's face. He'd known Remy his whole life. He'd never once seen him look at anybody like that.

Silas looked at the window instead.

"Can I ask you something," Melena said, when it was quiet again.

"You can ask me anything," Remy said. "I might lie."

She turned the silver wrapper over in her fingers. "Is there anything down here that makes it stop? Not the fire. The rest of it." She didn't look at either of them. "I would give a great deal for one hour where she isn't—" She stopped. She didn't finish. "One hour where somebody being dead doesn't touch everything I put my hands on."

Remy didn't answer right away. He sat back in his chair. His thumb found the bracelet again.

"Yeah," he said, quieter. "I know what that is."

Silas looked at the floor.

"There's stuff," Remy said. "Down here there's always stuff. But the stuff that gives you that hour, it steals it. That's what it does. It takes the hour and it gives it to you and it feels like a gift, and then later it comes back and charges you for it. With interest. Every time." He looked at her steadily. "I've watched people pay that bill. It's not a good bill."

Melena didn't say anything. She looked down at her hands.

The black came back under her nails while Silas watched, not much, just a thin line at the quick. Her shoulders went tight. She turned her palms up on the table and looked at them, and then, very fast, her hands came up off the wood together and moved into a shape. One palm turned out, the fingers spreading wide, the other drawing back toward her chest, the way you might draw back to throw something, or pull something out of the air. It had a set to it, a practiced line, like a move she'd been made to do so many times her body went to it on its own.

She stopped herself halfway through. She shoved both hands down under her thighs and sat on them, and her face went white, and the heat in the room jumped and then fell.

"What was that?" Remy asked.

"Nothing."

"Didn't look like nothing."

"It was nothing." Her voice was flat and final. "Something my father taught me. It doesn't matter."

It mattered. Silas could see it mattered in the way she wouldn't look up from the table. But it wasn't his to ask, and Remy didn't ask either. He just nodded, slow, and let it lie on the table between them with the crumbs, and got up to find more tea.

He opened the wrong cabinet.

Silas heard the grate rattle before he saw it. Remy had pulled open the high cabinet beside the stove looking for the honey, and instead of honey he'd found a short row of small dark glass bottles behind a locked iron grate, each one with a white label and a black stamp. Remy stood there with the cabinet door open and his other hand still raised.

"Well," Remy said. "That's not honey."

"Camille's," Silas said. "For emergencies."

Melena had turned in her chair. "What are they?"

Remy looked at Silas. Silas looked back at him and didn't say anything, because it wasn't his to say and they both knew it. Remy shut the cabinet door, gently, and sat back down.

"Dimming," Remy said. "That's what they call it up top. What the Synod calls it, when they're doing it to you on purpose. Down here, you buy it in a den, it's Hush." He spread his hands flat on the table. "That's the stuff I was telling you about."

"Would it stop the fire?"

"Those?" Remy jerked his head at the cabinet. "Those would stop everything. That's emergency stock. One of those and we'd be carrying you up the stairs, and you'd sleep through tomorrow and wake up not knowing your own name."

"I didn't ask about those." Melena sat forward. She'd taken her hands out from under her thighs. They were steady now, mostly, and she laid them flat on the table and looked at Remy across the bread crumbs. "I asked whether it would stop the fire."

Remy opened his mouth and closed it again.

"Remy," Silas said.

"I'm thinking."

"Think faster."

"You don't get to rush me, you burned your whole hand off." But Remy's eyes had gone far away, and his thumb was on the bracelet again, and Silas recognized that look too. It was the look Remy got right before he talked them both into something.

"Hush doesn't put the fire out," Remy said slowly. "Not normally. Normally there's no fire. But it takes the edge off whatever's underneath. That's the whole point of it. It takes the grief, the noise, the—" He gestured at her, at the room. "If whatever's lighting you up is coming from the same place the grief is, maybe. Maybe it'd quiet it."

"Maybe," Silas said.

"Maybe," Remy agreed. "But not those. Not some emergency bottle in a cabinet. If you wanted to know, actually know, you'd want somebody who measures it. One drop. One. Under somebody's eye who knows what they're looking at, so if it goes wrong, it goes wrong small."

"And who's that?" Silas asked, though he already knew.

"Ora." Remy met his eyes. "Blue Circle. She owes me."

"Everybody owes you."

"Ora owes me *specifically*."

Silas pushed off the doorframe and came all the way into the room for the first time since Remy had knocked. He sat down at the end of the table, between them, with his bad arm in his lap and his good hand flat on the wood, and he looked at Melena.

"Dante would kill me," he said.

"Dante would kill you," Remy agreed.

"Camille would kill me before Dante got the chance."

"Camille would kill you and then she'd kill me for coming up with it."

Melena looked between the two of them. "Then why are you still talking about it?"

Because his mother had crouched by that window last night with her hand a half inch off the burned thread and said *a week*, and Camille didn't round down. Because he had a bell he didn't have and a door that didn't latch and a girl who could walk through grave nails. Because Silas had been between her and something since noon yesterday and he was tired, and he was hurt, and he'd watched Remy do in five minutes with a mint wrapper what he couldn't have done with a whole night and a knife, and he didn't have any better ideas.

"One drop," Silas said.

Remy sat up.

"One. I hold it, not her, not you. We see what it does. If it does anything I don't like, anything, we walk out, and we don't argue about it in the street." He looked at Melena. "And you don't buy anything. You don't touch anything. You don't let anybody in that den put anything in your hand. You hear me?"

"I hear you."

"I'm serious."

"I can see that you're serious." Melena looked at his hand, at Camille's wrap, and something moved across her face that wasn't quite an apology and wasn't quite not. "I'm serious too."

Remy was already standing up, already checking his bag, already halfway to the door with that look on his face, the one that meant he'd be talking the whole way there and wouldn't hear a word anybody said back. He stopped beside Melena's chair. He didn't touch her. He just nodded at the silver wrapper still folded in her fist.

"Bring that," he said.

She looked down at it. Then she slid it into the pocket of Dante's coat, carefully, like it was worth something.

Silas sat at the end of the table and watched her do it, and he thought it was the first time since Dante's office he'd seen her face look like it belonged to somebody her age. Easy, almost. Not happy. But easy, for a minute, in the morning light coming grey through Camille's window, with tea in her and bread in her and a boy in a green coat who'd known to get down on the floor.

He didn't know what he felt about it. He thought maybe it was fine not to know yet.

He got up, and got his coat, and went to find his knife.
