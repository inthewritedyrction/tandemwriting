# 3SL Voice Guide (working draft)

The test chapters are written to these rules. Mark anything that's wrong.

**1. The narration is the character talking.** Close third and past tense stay. But Silas and Isola get to react in the middle of things: a dry aside, a cuss, a judgment, a joke at their own expense. These are grown people, so the humor is drier than Violet's and slower to panic. *Fourth Wing:* "I'm so fucked." *Your Ch. 3:* "Kids like that were always getting into something in Shadowmere." *Test version:* "Fourteen. Two more than she'd budgeted, which meant two more than they had."

**2. Short lines are for voice, not drama.** A one-line paragraph is fine when it's a reaction, a joke or a hard turn. It isn't fine as a solemn fragment for effect ("Nobody had." "It held."). If the line wouldn't come out of the character's mouth, it gets folded back into the paragraph.

**3. Attraction lands in the body first.** They notice and react, maybe roast themselves, then get back to work. Nobody explains why they noticed. *Kingmaker:* "it's bad timing to notice this guy is gorgeous. And that he smells really good." *Test version:* Isola clocks that he's fine, is annoyed about it, and moves on because her cousin is getting away.

**4. Name feelings plainly.** "Her stomach dropped" is allowed. Being honest and fast beats being clever.

**5. Tell when the pressure earns it.** World facts come with attitude or a stake, or because somebody in the scene needs to hear them. The narration doesn't stop to give the reader a tour.

**6. People say the hard thing.** Family members fight out loud. Love and cruelty are both blunt.

**7. Scenes move.** Each chapter opens on stakes and ends on a turn. Things happen on the page instead of being remembered.

**8. No refrains.** No repeating a phrase across paragraphs as a style move ("He thought about… He thought about…").

**9. Blackness lives in the social fabric.** It shows in who checks whom, family shorthand, aunties, money, manners and the jokes. Dialogue carries the dialect, and narration stays mostly standard with occasional slides.
# CHAPTER 4: ISOLA

The noon courier wanted fourteen Veilmarks, and Isola was carrying High Heresy in her skirt pocket while he counted them.

Fourteen. Two more than she'd budgeted, which meant two more than they had. The route inspectors were out, he said, like that was her problem. She counted the coins into his palm one at a time anyway, and she listened to every single one ring against the last on the way down. She'd never listened to money before this morning. Now she couldn't stop. Low and full meant real. Anything flatter and she'd have snatched it back and eaten the shame.

They were all real. The courier checked the seal on Micah's packet twice, slid it into the flat case on his hip, and stepped back into the lane like he hadn't just walked off with the last of the house money.

Behind her, Micah let out a breath he'd been holding since first bell.

"That's it?"

"That's it." Isola shut the door. "Now all you gotta do is not do nothing stupid until Danika write back."

"I been not doing nothing stupid for thirty days."

"Twenty-nine and a morning." His eyes went to his phone on the table, and she picked it up before he could. "And you still ain't getting this."

He sucked his teeth and threw himself on the couch like she'd personally ruined his life, which meant he was done fighting about it until he got hungry. Isola looked at the clock over the name wall.

Twelve-oh-four. No Asha.

That had been the deal she'd made with herself in Asha's room that morning, standing over a torn bag with a fake coin in her hand. Asha comes home before the courier, Isola asks her straight. Asha doesn't, Isola goes down to Shadowmere and finds out for herself. She'd been praying for door number one.

The clock didn't care what she'd been praying for. Neither did Asha, apparently.

---

The bag was right where she'd left it, torn pocket turned to the wall, strap still folded under the way she'd folded it so she'd know if anybody touched it. Nobody had. Isola fished the coin out of the lining and didn't look at it. She'd done enough looking. It was a little too light and a little too cold and it was going to get somebody in this house Silenced if she wasn't careful.

She could leave it. Put it back, let Asha come home to it, pretend she'd never torn the pocket. Except then it'd be sitting under Omari's roof, two feet from a wall with four generations of their names on it, and if the Synod ever came knocking, they wouldn't care whose bag it was in. They'd read every name on that wall like a charge sheet.

If she carried it, the only person it could hang was her.

That math wasn't hard.

And when Asha came home and went digging for it and found the lining empty? Good. Let her sweat. Let her lie awake for once wondering who knew what.

Isola tucked the coin into the hidden pocket behind the seam where she kept money, stood the bag up so the tear wouldn't show from the door, and looked at the rest of them. Five bags. Packed and buckled and lined up against the wall like they were waiting on a train. Two years they'd been sitting there.

She'd stopped asking about them a long time ago because asking made Asha go quiet, and quiet Asha was worse than smart-mouthed Asha. Right now, with a counterfeit riding her hip, Isola would've given a lot to go back and be the kind of cousin who kept asking anyway.

---

Selah was in the kitchen packing roots into a jar and humming. Same six notes. She'd been humming them since breakfast, which was never a good sign, and she didn't stop when Isola came in, which was a worse one.

"I'm going for the salt."

Her mother wiped her hands on her apron, pulled the order slip out of her pocket, and held it. Didn't hand it over. Just held it and looked at Isola with the same look she'd given her over the dishes, the one that saw straight through your skin to whatever you'd shoved in your pockets.

Then she put the slip in Isola's hand. "Pell. Not nobody else."

"I know."

"Funeral grade."

"Mama. I *know*."

"Then you ain't got nothing to worry about." Selah went back to her jar.

Selah Wraithmoor did not repeat herself. If she was saying a thing twice, she was talking about something else entirely, and everybody in this house had learned that by age six. Isola put the slip in her front pocket where she'd see it every time she reached for money, and set Micah's phone face down on the counter.

Selah looked at the phone. Then at Isola. Then, without a word, she slid the jar of roots right on top of it.

Better than a lock. Micah wasn't brave enough to move Selah's jar.

Tevin's door was shut on her way out. She didn't knock. Knocking would just tell him she was leaving, and Tevin knowing she was gone was how Tevin ended up somewhere he shouldn't be. His route coat was still hanging off the chair in the front room where she'd made him drop it.

Good. Stay mad in the spare room, cousin. Mad in the spare room never hurt anybody.

---

Mother Rae's token hung on a cord under Isola's shirt. Brass, worn soft at the edges, with marks on its face Rae had never bothered to teach her to read. Isola had never bothered to ask. Rae answered questions when Rae felt like it, which was roughly never, and the token worked fine without Isola understanding it. Most things in her life did.

She held it in her fist all the way down the cliff stairs. Past the shrine on the second landing, where somebody had left a cup of tea for the dead that had gone grey with dust. Past the women hauling water up from the cisterns, one of whom asked after Selah without slowing down. Isola said Selah was fine without slowing down either. That was the whole conversation, and it was a full one. In Ashvein, *how's your mama* meant *I see you and I'll notice if you don't come back up*.

The stairs were free. The lift wasn't. She'd pay for the lift on the way home if the salt was heavy, and not one second before.

The chasm bridge was packed with people trying to get across before the mist came up. A boy at the head of the span selling grounding charms told everybody who'd listen that it had come up twice already today and three people had frozen dead in the middle because they couldn't find the rail. He told it like a sales pitch. Isola didn't buy a charm. She could see the rail fine. She could see the whole black stone arch of the bridge, the violet glow breathing up out of the vent beneath it, and the guard post at the Shadowmere end with its lamp and its line.

The line crawled. The guard was a big man in a mercenary coat with half his buttons gone and a route scanner on a chain around his neck. In front of him, set into the counter, sat the metal plate.

The woman ahead of Isola pricked her thumb on the needle beside it without being told and pressed it down. The guard watched the plate drink until it was satisfied. The boy behind her didn't have blood to spare, or didn't want to spare it, so he gave a crew name instead and swore to run for them whenever they called.

Isola knew that crew. East gate. She watched the boy walk out onto the bridge like he'd gotten a bargain, and she hoped somebody at home loved him enough to beat his behind for it.

She'd bled on that plate herself, once. Before Rae gave her the cord.

She stepped up and opened her fist. The guard looked at the token, ran the scanner over it, and got the short flat beep she'd been waiting on. He jerked his chin toward Shadowmere without asking where she was going or what was in her skirt.

She kept her free hand on her bag strap the whole time. Nowhere near the pocket. She had no idea whether a scanner that read tokens could read anything else, and she was not about to find out by patting her own hip in front of it like a fool.

---

Isola did not like Shadowmere.

Nobody at home did, so she'd never had to explain it, but if somebody had made her, she'd have said this: in Ashvein, crews wore their colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were standing on before your feet hit it, and if you didn't, somebody's auntie would lean out a window and tell you, and then tell your mama you'd needed telling.

Shadowmere didn't have aunties in windows. It had neon and violet mist and rain falling out of solid rock, which was ridiculous and showed off, and it had people who smiled at you while they figured out what you were worth. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

*Running errands or something,* Selah had said about Asha this morning.

Errands. Sure.

Isola had a fake coin in her skirt that said otherwise. Whatever Asha had been running, it ran through here.

So before she did one thing for Rae, she was going to find out exactly what her cousin had left lying in that bag.

---

The assayer kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain couldn't get at her scales. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Rae and watching the woman weigh it on a brass scale with weights the size of baby teeth. She could weigh anything. Scrapings, filings, coins that had been through a fire. She'd put on her loupes and tell you what it was worth, and in all the time Isola had watched, she had never once asked a single soul where anything came from.

That was the whole reason to come here.

The woman was busy telling a man in a dock coat that his silver was mostly lead. He argued. He lost. He left. Isola stepped up, laid her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over.

The coin sat in the middle of her palm. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody walking by could see more than a corner.

The assayer looked at it. She didn't pick it up. She didn't reach for her loupes. She just put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it.

"I don't weigh High Heresy."

The man at the next counter, selling charm wire, stopped talking mid-sentence. His customer stopped listening mid-word.

"Get it off my counter."

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

She walked away slow. Hurrying in the Back Market was the same as standing on a crate and yelling *follow me*. Her face was hot, and her heart was going like a work bell, and she kept her feet at a stroll.

She'd known what it was since Asha's room. She had. But knowing a thing alone in your cousin's bedroom and hearing a woman who never asked questions say it out loud in front of witnesses were two different animals.

High Heresy meant the square. It meant the woman from the mining district on her knees, and the Synod taking her breath slow so everybody could watch, and her fingers scraping at the stones long after scraping could help her. Isola had been eleven. Selah had clamped a hand on her shoulder so she couldn't turn away, because Selah believed you looked at the things the Synod did so you'd never be stupid enough to forget them.

For one second, stepping out into the rain, the fingers on those stones were Asha's.

Isola stopped walking. She stood there in the drip off the arch and let the rain hit her face until her stomach climbed back down out of her throat.

*Okay. Okay. Salt.* The salt was the reason she was down here. The salt was the only thing on her list today she knew how to do right.

She went to find Pell.

---

Pell's stall sat in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned out into thread sellers and funeral goods and the counters ate half the walking room. He sat under his heat lamp the way he always did, three braids in his beard resting on his belly, a ring on every finger catching the amber light whenever he pointed at somebody's purse. He had a scale nobody else touched, a drawer he kept locked, and a mouth that never shut, which was usually the only annoying thing about him.

Usually.

Rae bought her funeral grade from Pell because he kept it dry and didn't cut it. Selah had made that crystal clear the first time she sent Isola down alone, and even clearer the day Isola came home and admitted what he'd charged. Pell had a Shadowmere price and an Ashvein price, and he'd never once in his life been embarrassed about either.

By the time she reached his counter, he should have asked after Selah, complained about his knees, and tried to sell her something she hadn't come for.

"Rae's girl." He was already reaching. "What she need?"

He hadn't asked about Selah.

Isola put the slip down. He read it, nodded, and stuck his scoop into the open barrel at his elbow.

She watched it come up. Grey. Coarse. Clumped where the damp had gotten into it, with dark grit caught between the grains. She'd been packing salt into Selah's windowsills since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them, and she'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name. Fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it had somewhere better to be. Pell kept it in that locked drawer, ground fresh every morning, and every time Isola had ever come for Rae, he'd opened that drawer without being asked.

He was folding the grey stuff into brown paper.

"That ain't funeral grade."

Pell didn't look up. "It's what I got today."

"That's quarry salt."

"It's salt."

"It's quarry salt, and it's wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop." She put her hand flat on the slip. "Rae's slip say funeral grade."

"Rae can come get it herself."

Isola blinked.

Pell ran his mouth. Everybody knew Pell ran his mouth. But he ran it the way people did when they wanted you to stay and buy something else, not the way they did when they wanted you gone. And his hands were folding that paper too careful. Squaring the corners. Squaring them again. The way you kept your hands busy so your face didn't have to do anything.

*Oh, something's wrong.*

She pulled Rae's cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he'd have to look at it.

"She sent me. Same as last time. Same as the time before that. Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today."

Pell stopped folding.

He looked past her.

Isola didn't turn around. She'd grown up in Omari Wraithmoor's house, where you learned what was behind you by watching the face in front of you. Pell's face had just gone as blank as a wall.

Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end. The other stood right next to her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word.

In Ashvein, she'd have clocked their crew by their sleeves before they ever got to the counter. These two wore plain dark coats and the kind of patience you only had if nobody had ever told you no at a counter in your life.

Pell reached under the counter. Not to the drawer. Farther back. He came up with an envelope and set it down, and the man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking at it.

The one at the end of the counter looked at Isola's hand. The one still holding Rae's token out in the open like a fool.

He kept looking. Long enough that she felt it crawl all the way up her arm.

She closed her fist over the brass. He followed his friend out of the stall, and the two of them strolled up the lane toward the bend like they had all day, because they did.

Pell didn't unlock the drawer for a long time after that. When he finally did, his hands were shaking so bad the key took two tries.

Isola tucked the cord back under her shirt. *Nobody tells you who owns the street until you owe it something.* Well. Pell owed it something. She'd just watched him pay.

He weighed out the real stuff, the morning cut, fine and white in grey paper, and when he folded the packet, a thin stream of it slid off the scale onto the counter. He watched it go.

He didn't say a word about it.

Pell. Silent. About salt on his own counter. She'd have bet the house money on that never happening, and she'd have lost the house money, which, after the courier, was about two Veilmarks.

"Who was that?"

"Who?"

She waited.

"Rae account." His letters wandered all over the line as he wrote it in his book. "Eighteen. Tell her I said hey."

Eighteen was robbery, and they both knew it. Any other day, she'd have stood there and argued him down to fourteen just to watch him sweat. Today she looked at the grey grit still sitting in the brown paper he'd tried to pass her, and the whole thing came clear.

He hadn't been cheating Rae. He'd been trying to get Rae's girl gone before those two walked up and found her standing at his counter with Rae's token around her neck. *Rae can come get it herself* was as close to a warning as Pell could afford.

She couldn't prove one bit of it. She took the packet anyway and didn't argue the eighteen. That was all the thanks he was getting.

He looked up the lane toward where the men had gone, and then back down at his scale like it had done something interesting.

Isola followed his look up the lane.

Asha was standing at the bend.

---

Isola knew her by how she stood before she saw her face. Weight back. Head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her. The way you stood when somebody had told you exactly where to wait and you were scared to death of getting it wrong.

Isola had never once seen Asha stand like that anywhere she was supposed to be.

The coat was new. Dark, fitted close at the waist, falling past her knees. A tote hung off her shoulder, stiff-sided and expensive, the kind of bag that held its shape whether there was anything in it or not.

Isola had never seen either one in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Bags like that cost money. And money like that came from somewhere. Five packed bags against a bedroom wall, a fake coin against her hip, and her cousin dressed like Ironcross at the back of Gutterlight.

It all fit. She hated how easily it fit.

Asha turned her head toward the crowd noise coming through the stone. She was about to go.

Isola stepped away from the counter.

"*Move!*"

A boy came tearing down the lane, elbows out, route coat flapping, and right in front of her, a man stepped back off the freight wall to get out of his way and put his entire back into her.

He didn't knock her down. She'd been knocked into by bigger, and her feet stayed where they were. But the packet flew out of her hand. She watched it go, turning once in the air like it was taking its time, and burst on the wet stone at her feet.

Eighteen Veils of funeral grade hit the puddles and went grey.

*One bumps. One runs. You find out what's gone when you get home.* Every story she'd ever heard about the Back Market went exactly like that, and here was a man's back in her chest and a boy running past, and her hand was in her skirt before she finished thinking it, fingers locked around the coin so hard the edge bit.

"Damn, my bad," the boy called over his shoulder, already past the bend.

Isola looked down at the salt. Then she looked up at the man.

And up.

Lord. He was *tall*. Not regular tall. Tall enough that she had to tip her head back to get all of him, and she was too mad to be embarrassed about it. Somebody had gotten to his face recently. Swelling high under one eye, a split in his bottom lip, and a bandage wrapped across the knuckles of his right hand, white gone grey. Rain had soaked his locs and was still running off them, down past his ear and into his beard, and he wasn't even bothering to wipe it.

"You've got to be fucking kidding me." She stared at the puddle. "That's eighteen Veils dissolving in that puddle."

"Eighteen?" He glanced past her at Pell. His voice was low and unbothered, like he had all the time in the world and hadn't just cost her her whole afternoon. "You robbing folks in daylight now?"

Pell lifted both hands, rings flashing. "Price is the price, Graves. Ashvein orders come with a fee."

*Graves.*

Isola filed it. That was nobody's mama's name. That was what people called you when they'd decided what you were.

Something moved behind him against the freight wall. A hooded figure, small, pressed flat to the brick. She got one look before he shifted. The hood was down low, but the lamp from the next stall caught a single curl that had slipped out at the edge, and the curl was *red*. Not dyed. Not rust. Red like the inside of a coal.

"Pell," the man said, without turning around. "Bag her another one. Put it on Camille."

Pell's eyebrows went all the way up his forehead. "Camille know she paying for this?"

Who in the world was Camille?

Either Camille was this man's woman and the girl at the wall was something else, or the girl at the wall *was* Camille, or he was a whole lot more trouble than his face already said he was. Isola didn't love any of those options.

He dug into his coat and came out with four Veilmarks and set them on the counter one at a time so the quicksilver caught the lamp. "Bag her another one."

Pell looked at the coins. Then at the man's face, long enough to place it. Something shifted under his beard. "Don't got another morning cut."

"Then give her the evening cut early."

Pell sighed like he'd been asked to haul the whole tier up the cliff stairs on his back, and reached under the counter.

Four Veilmarks.

Isola stared at those four coins sitting on Pell's counter. Pell had put her down for eighteen on Rae's account not five minutes ago, and this man had just laid down four for the same salt, and Pell hadn't argued so much as a copper.

Oh, she and Pell were going to have *words*. Not today. But they were going to have them.

She'd crouched over the puddle without noticing she'd done it. She stood up, hand still in her skirt, and looked at him properly this time.

She took her time. Why not? He'd taken her salt; she could take a minute. The swollen eye. The lip. The wrapped hand, tied neat and tight, the kind of neat nobody managed one-handed, which meant somebody who loved him had done it.

And he was fine.

Just. Fine. She noticed it the way you noticed weather, without deciding to, and she appreciated it about as much as the rain. He had the kind of face that would've been easy to look at even without somebody having already tried to rearrange it, and he was standing there letting her look like he knew good and well what she was doing and didn't mind one bit.

*Absolutely not. You are looking for your cousin.*

She looked past him for the red curl.

He shifted half a step left, smooth as anything, and put his shoulder square in her line of sight.

Isola knew a block when she saw one. She'd grown up with Tevin. Whatever that was against the wall, this man didn't want her looking at it, and he wasn't going to say so.

Her chin came up. "I ain't ask you to pay for it. I ain't trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere. Everyone knows how y'all get down."

The corner of his mouth started to go. She watched the split lip stop it, and she watched him decide the smile was worth the sting anyway, for about half a second. "I knocked it out your hand. Debt's mine."

He said it like it was already settled. Not an offer. A fact.

She had an answer ready for a man trying to make her owe him. She'd had that answer ready since she was fourteen. She did not have one for a man who'd just taken the owing on himself and left her standing there with nothing to fight.

That was annoying. That was *deeply* annoying.

Pell slapped a fresh packet on the counter, wrapped twice in waxed paper. Isola took it with her free hand. The other one stayed in her skirt. She was not letting go of that coin for anybody, and she was definitely not letting go of it for a man named after a cemetery.

"Next time," she said, "watch where you backing up."

"Next time, stand somewhere else."

Oh, she had something for that. She had several somethings. She could feel every one of them lined up behind her teeth, ready to go. She looked at him and decided he hadn't earned a single one.

And she could tell he knew it. And she could tell he was *enjoying* it.

That was the worst part.

She tucked the packet under her arm, stepped around the grey puddle, and went up the lane toward the bend without looking back.

She wanted to look back. She could feel the exact spot between her shoulder blades where she'd know if he was watching, and she wasn't about to give him the satisfaction of finding out. She had a cousin to catch.

---

The bend was empty.

Two runners crossed it with a box between them, arguing about which door they'd been paid to use. Behind them, nothing. Just the lane curving off toward the noise of Gutterlight, and the noise.

Isola stood exactly where Asha had been standing and turned in a slow circle. Nobody in a fitted coat. Nobody anywhere.

Fifteen steps. That was how close she'd been. Fifteen steps, and she'd spent them looking at a man's face.

She checked the crossing. She checked the doorways on both sides and the mouth of the next alley, where the drainage ran ankle-deep and stank of dye. She walked the whole length of Gutterlight's back wall, ducking into every doorway that would let a body stand out of the rain, until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her back teeth.

By then the crowd at the bend had doubled. Asha could have been anywhere in it or nowhere near it.

Isola stood in the rain with the salt under her arm and her hand still locked on the coin, so mad she couldn't pick who to aim it at.

Asha first. Asha for the coat and the bag and the coin and two years of luggage. Pell second, for eighteen. The two men in dark coats third, for whatever they were. The boy with the elbows.

Then him.

He hadn't known what he cost her. She knew that. He'd backed up to get out of a running boy's way, and he'd paid for the salt without making a show of it, and he'd kept his body between her and whatever was at that wall like that was just a thing his body did.

None of which made her feel one bit better about the fact that she'd been fifteen steps from her cousin and spent them on his face.

It was a good face. That was not the point.

---

She paid for the lift home. The salt wasn't heavy. She paid anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might've decided to follow a girl with Rae's token and a coin nobody would weigh.

Eleven people in a cage built for eight. Somebody's elbow lived in her ribs the whole way up. Isola kept the packet flat against her chest and her hand over her skirt, and the operator called out the levels without looking up from his lever. Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.

She spent the entire ride irritated by six seconds of him.

Six seconds was about how long she'd stood there looking at him like she had the right, and about how long he'd stood there letting her. *Debt's mine.* She went over it and couldn't find a single thing wrong with it, which was its own problem. *Next time, stand somewhere else.* Plenty wrong with that one. She caught herself almost smiling about it and stopped.

The way he'd shifted half a step without anybody asking.

The rain going into his beard. Like he couldn't be bothered.

*Graves.* That wasn't a name. That was a warning somebody had decided to call a man.

Somewhere past Service Shaft, she caught her thumb working one of her rings around and around her finger. She made herself stop.

By Ashvein West, she was doing it again.

---

The rain on her coat had dried to dust by the time the gate opened. Coal smoke hung low over the lane. She'd missed at least one bell. The boys who'd been posted under the lantern across the street all morning were gone.

Isola trusted the empty spot less than she'd trusted the boys.

Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge. Nobody in her house left a job half done on that step. She looked at the little pile longer than a pile deserved, and then she looked up.

The lantern over the door still burned amber.

*For now.* She hated that she thought it. She went in anyway.

Micah was asleep on the couch, one arm hanging off the cushion, shoes still on. He'd given up on the day but not on the shoes. Tevin's coat hadn't moved from the chair. His door was still shut. She listened at it on her way by and heard him breathing on the other side, slow and even, the way he breathed when he was pretending to be asleep.

Good. Pretend all evening, cousin.

Asha's door was open. Isola didn't go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she'd left it. If Asha had come home, she'd come and gone without touching it.

Selah was at the stove. She turned around when Isola came in and held out both hands for the salt before Isola got anywhere near the counter. "Pell?"

"Pell."

Selah turned the packet over. Looked at the double waxed paper. "This ain't the morning cut."

"Evening cut. He tried me with quarry salt first."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Her mother untied the paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. It ran like it had somewhere better to be. She nodded once, but she looked at that double wrap a good while longer than she'd looked at the salt.

"You find your cousin?"

Selah had told her this morning *don't go looking*. And now she wasn't asking whether Isola had looked. She was asking whether she'd *found*.

Of course she was.

Isola reached for a rag that did not need reaching for. "Saw her. Lost her in the Market."

"Lost her how?"

A coat Isola couldn't have bought with three months of courier fees. A bag to go with it. A coin the assayer wouldn't touch. Two men who never said a word. Pell, quiet. And a man called Graves who'd backed into her at the worst possible second and then stood there like he'd been glad to.

"Crowd," Isola said.

That was the second word today she'd handed one of her parents that was only mostly true. She was going to have to start keeping count.

Selah held her eyes long enough that Isola wiped the same spot on the counter twice. Then her mother turned back to the stove, and after a minute, the humming started up again. Same six notes.

"Wash up," Selah said over it. "Food almost ready."

# CHAPTER 11: ISOLA

Asha came to breakfast like nothing had happened, and that was how Isola knew she was in real trouble.

Isola had lain awake half the night listening to her cousin pack on the other side of the wall. Folding. Rolling. Buckling. She'd waited for the front door to lift on its hinges, that soft double click Asha thought nobody heard, and it had never come. Sometime before dawn, the packing stopped.

And now here Asha was at the end of the table with her back to the wall, eating the plate Selah had kept warm two nights running, asking if there was more tea. Calm as a Sunday.

She didn't look at Isola once.

Isola sat down across from her and didn't touch her food. The coin sat in her skirt like a second heartbeat. Final bell had rung last night. Asha hadn't said a word. And Isola, who had looked her cousin dead in the face behind a lye vat on Bath Row and said *I'm putting this coin on the table at dinner my own self*, had stood outside Asha's bedroom door with her hand on her pocket and done absolutely nothing.

Because she'd heard the bags. And she'd understood, standing in that hall, that if the coin hit the table, Asha would be gone by morning. Not maybe. Gone. The bags were buckled. The name was already loose on the wall. The only thing still holding her cousin in this house was that nobody had said it out loud yet.

So Isola hadn't said it.

She'd let her own threat die in the hallway, and she was furious with herself, and now Selah was pouring tea and Micah was begging for the pepper sauce and Omari was reading the ward notices with his glasses down his nose, and the coin was still in her skirt where she'd sworn it wouldn't be.

"You going somewhere?" Selah asked. "Coat on at first bell?"

"Errand for Godmother."

Selah looked at her. Then at Asha. Then back at her. She didn't say a word, which in Selah's language was a whole paragraph.

At the end of the table, Asha finally looked up. Just a second. At Isola's coat, at Isola's hand resting on her skirt, and Isola watched her cousin figure out that the coin was still there and that Isola hadn't used it.

Something went across Asha's face. Relief, maybe. Or shame. Or that sour thing that came after both, when you got away with something you hadn't wanted to get away with.

Then she went back to her eggs.

Isola got up and left before she threw the teapot.

---

Halflight sat half a staircase below Ashvein and one locked door above Shadowmere, and it belonged to Ironcross, which meant it belonged to nobody who'd ever actually had to walk through it.

Isola had been through it once, years ago, holding Rae's hand. She remembered it bigger. It was an old transfer station from back when trains ran between tiers, one long low hall with a curved grey tile ceiling and a pale scar down the middle of the floor where somebody had pulled up the tracks and paved over them. A gate at each end. Ashvein behind her, Shadowmere ahead. Both locked. Both with an Ironcross clerk sitting in a little glass booth beside them, looking like they'd been sitting there since the last train left.

On the wall, under a hundred years of grime, hung the old route board. All nine tiers, painted top to bottom in fading white letters, the way every child in the Underside learned them before they learned to cross a street. Crownlight at the top, nearest the surface, where the Synod made laws for places its officials wouldn't be caught dead in. Dawnveil under it, keeping the people rich enough to enjoy those laws. Ironcross turning both into contracts. Then everything else, all the way down, supplying whatever the top three wanted.

Isola had always thought that board was more honest than the Synod meant it to be. It didn't pretend anybody was equal. It just listed them in order, like a ledger.

Going down was easy. That was the part nobody told you when you were small and memorizing the order. The gates only cared where you were headed. Going back *up* was the trick. Going up, you needed somebody important to claim you deserved it.

"Name," said the clerk at the Ashvein booth.

"Isola Wraithmoor."

"Entry tier."

"Ashvein."

"Exit tier."

"Ashvein."

He wrote it down in a ledger the size of a door. Didn't look up. "Weapons."

Isola hiked her skirt to the knee and showed him the knife strapped to her right thigh. The clerk looked at it like it was a pair of shoes. "Declared." He wrote that down too. "Ironcross records every declared weapon in Halflight. Undeclared weapons void your contract and blacken your crossing. Understand?"

"I understand."

"Table's at the line."

She'd known there'd be a line. She just hadn't thought about what it would look like.

It was a strip of brass set into the floor where the tracks used to run, worn dull in some spots and bright in others from a century of feet stepping very carefully around it. Light tile on one side, dark tile on the other. No rope. No wall. No guard. Just brass in a dirty floor, and everybody in the Underside knew the Ironcross line was a wall anyway, the same way everybody knew a black lantern meant somebody had died.

The table sat right on top of it.

It was *small*. Smaller than Selah's kitchen table. Smaller than the little round one in Rae's shop. Somebody had set it so the brass ran straight down the middle, one chair on the light side and one on the dark, and they'd set the chairs close because there was nowhere else for them to go.

The chair on the dark side was already taken.

He was sitting with his back to the Shadowmere gate, his wrapped hand resting on one knee, looking up at the route board. The bandage went all the way to his wrist now. The swelling under his eye had gone purple-black overnight. He looked like a man who'd been hit in the face two days running and had decided that was the face's problem.

He turned his head. She watched him see her.

It took him a second. His eyes went to her face, then to her hands, the two rings, then back up. And his whole face just stopped. He sat there in the Ironcross chair and looked at her like somebody had walked up and handed him a coin he'd thought he lost.

Then the corner of his mouth went up, split lip and all, and he didn't fight it this time.

"Salt," he said.

Isola walked the rest of the way to the table and sat down. Her knees, under the table, came within an inch of his. She moved them.

They ended up closer. That table was a crime.

"Graves," she said.

"So you got that much."

"Pell said it." She settled her coat. "I got more than that."

"Do you."

"Silas," she said, and watched it hit.

It hit. He sat back a little in his chair and looked at her a long moment, and whatever he was thinking went across his face too fast to read, but she'd gotten him. She'd gotten him clean. She felt it like a win, small and hot, and she didn't let herself enjoy it more than her face could hide.

"Micah," he said.

"How you figure?"

"Rae said my godson's sister. I only got one godson whose sister'd look at me like I owe her money." He tipped his head. "You got his eyes."

"I don't have *nothing* of his. He got mine. I'm older."

"That right."

"You know my brother?"

"Bought him noodles twice. Jude brings him to the fights." That almost-smile came back. "He yells louder than anybody at the fence. I couldn't pick him out of a crowd without Jude pointing, but I'd know that mouth anywhere."

"That's Micah," Isola said before she could stop herself, and he laughed.

It was short and low and came out of him like he hadn't planned it, and it changed his entire face. Purple eye. Split lip. Hand wrapped up like a parcel. None of it stopped him from looking good when he laughed, and that was just unfair. That was an injustice. Isola sat at the little table with two Ironcross clerks pretending not to watch and decided she'd file a complaint about it later with somebody in charge.

"So," he said. "Rae says you have something I want to see."

"Rae says a lot of things."

"Rae says almost nothing. That's why I came."

Okay. Fair.

Isola put her hand in her skirt. She took her time. She could feel him watching her hand, the same way he'd watched it at Pell's counter, where it had stayed buried in her pocket the whole time. He'd wondered. She knew he'd wondered. She let him wonder a little longer just because she could.

Then she drew the coin out and set it on her side of the brass, and took her hand away.

He looked at it. Didn't reach.

"Huh," he said.

"Huh what?"

He didn't answer. He reached into his own coat instead, slow, the way you'd reach for something in front of somebody who might not like what came out. A folded handkerchief. He laid it on his side of the line and opened it with his good hand.

Isola's stomach dropped.

"Blue Circle," he said. "From the package that burned. I picked up one. Somebody else scooped the rest, and I got three of them back off him at the rail bridge."

Isola kept her face real still.

He picked up one of his and held it out over the line. After a second, she picked up hers and did the same, and they turned them under the dim tile light at the same time, an inch apart. The quicksilver on both coins flashed bright at one corner of the Veiled One's face and died before it reached the other side.

"Same," Isola said.

"Same." He set his down. "Can I see yours closer?"

She handed it over. He turned it, rubbed his thumb across the face, looked at the grey that came up on his skin. Then he set it down on *her* side of the line, not his, and slid it back to her with one finger.

She picked it up. She put it down. A minute later, when he asked where the light had been when she found it, she picked it up and handed it across to him again.

It went back and forth like that more times than it needed to. She knew it. He knew it. Neither of them said one word about it.

"So what is it," Isola said finally. "Not what it's called. The assayer already told me what it's called, in front of a whole charm-wire man and his customer. What *is* it."

He told her.

It was good, that was the first thing. Not crew work. Crews made lead and lacquer. This was real quicksilver and a binding compound you couldn't buy, one somebody had to know how to cook. His brother had tested it last night and found the same compound holding a stolen gold token together, one somebody had used to sell a route with three kids on it. Somebody was dressing these coins up to look like fresh Crownlight issue.

"You don't do that to buy bread in the Back Market," he said. "It's built to climb. Up through Ironcross and past it. Somebody's pouring them down here and sending them up as high as anybody'll take them."

His eyes sharpened. "You heard about that."

"I heard some runners talking behind a lye vat on Bath Row."

"She's not mine."

"A lot of people walk with me. She's a job."

"That's what they said."

Isola hadn't expected that. She didn't know what to do with it, so she didn't do anything.

The man was waiting at the top of the Halflight stairs.

She'd signed out in the door-sized ledger and started up the half staircase, and she felt him before she saw him, the way she'd felt those two walk up to Pell's counter. Then she saw the coat. Plain. Dark. Cut long.

He was leaning on the wall where the corridor turned, like a man waiting on a friend, and he didn't look at her when she came up. That was how she knew. Everybody looked at you in a corridor like that. Only the ones who were supposed to be watching knew better than to.

She'd seen that coat at the end of Pell's counter, staring at Rae's token in her hand.

*Of course. Of course you're here.*

Isola didn't slow down. She didn't speed up. She kept her eyes on the steps and her hand off her pocket and climbed right past him like he was part of the wall. At the turn, where the corridor bent and the lamps thinned out and there was one breath of shadow between one light and the next, she let the Veil slip.

She'd been doing it since she was a girl, and she'd never once managed to explain it to anybody who couldn't already do it. It wasn't hiding. You couldn't hide in an empty corridor. It was more like going thin. Letting go of whatever part of you made people's eyes catch, the way you'd let go of a rope, so anybody looking would slide right off you and land on the next thing over.

She felt it go. She felt the man's attention come around the turn after her, reach for her, and slide.

She kept walking. Up the stairs. Through the door. Out into the coal smoke of Ashvein. By the time he came around that corner looking, there was nothing in that corridor but lamps and a dirty floor.

She held it until she was three streets from home.

When she let go, it came back into her all at once, like walking into a warm kitchen out of the cold. She stood on the corner by the shrine with the broken roof and breathed. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them and told them to quit it, and they mostly listened.

Then she put her hand inside her coat and touched black three, warm against her heart.

*Silas.* His face when she said it, like she'd slapped him with something nice. *Slower.* The way he'd taken his hand back off that table, slow, so she'd have room to reach.

And a man in a dark coat at the top of the Halflight stairs, waiting on Rae's goddaughter.

She took her hand out of her coat and walked the rest of the way home, and she didn't let herself smile until the front door was shut behind her. Then only for a second.

Then she went to find out what Asha was packing now.
