The first time he reaches for it, she closes her fist and makes him ask. The coin goes back and forth more than it needs to. He gives her black three. Before she leaves, she gives him half of what she wouldn't give him in the lane: her first name, without the Wraithmoor.

# CHAPTER 11: ISOLA

Mother Rae set the meeting for second bell, and Isola got to the Halflight stairs at a quarter past first because she wanted to see him walk in before he saw her.

Rae hadn't told her much, which was normal. *Graves boy wants to look at what you found. Halflight, second bell. Don't take nothing from him you can't give back.* She'd said it over the counter while she wrapped a candle for somebody else's funeral, the way she'd tell you which stall had the good peppers.

Isola had the coin in the pocket behind her skirt seam and one rule for herself. Asha's name stayed in her mouth. Whatever this man knew and whatever he offered, he wasn't getting her cousin.

She'd watched Blue Circle blow last night with Asha somewhere inside it. Asha had come home after midnight with smoke in her hair, walked straight past the name wall without looking at it, and gone to bed. She hadn't come out of her room since. Isola hadn't pushed. If she asked, Asha would lie, and Isola would have to decide what to do about the lie, and she wasn't ready to make that decision in a house with Omari in it. So she was making it down here instead.

Halflight sat half a staircase below Ashvein and one locked door above Shadowmere. Ironcross owned it, which meant Ironcross staffed it, which meant a clerk in a glass booth at the bottom of the stairs with a ledger as big as a door.

"Name."

"Isola Wraithmoor."

"Entry tier."

"Ashvein."

"Exit tier."

"Ashvein."

He wrote it down without looking up. "Weapons."

Isola pulled her skirt up to the knee and showed him the knife strapped to her thigh. The clerk looked at it the way you'd look at somebody's shoes. "Declared," he said, and wrote that down too. "Ironcross records every declared weapon in Halflight. Undeclared voids your crossing. Table's at the line."

The hall past the booth used to be a train platform, back when trains ran between tiers. Somebody had pulled up the track and paved the bed, and all that was left of it was a strip of brass set into the floor down the middle, worn dull where a hundred years of feet had stepped around it. Barred gates closed off both ends. Ashvein behind her, Shadowmere in front.

The old route board still hung over the Shadowmere gate, nine tiers painted in a column, Crownlight at the top and Nightroot at the bottom. Every kid in the Underside learned that order before they learned to tie their shoes. What nobody bothered to teach you was the part Isola had figured out on her own by the time she was twelve. Anybody could go down. The gates didn't care about that. Going up, you needed somebody important to claim you, and nobody important had ever claimed a Wraithmoor for anything.

The table sat right on top of the brass line, one chair on the Ashvein side and one on the Shadowmere side. It was smaller than Selah's kitchen table, smaller even than the little round one in Rae's back room where Isola did her homework when she was nine.

The Shadowmere chair was already taken. So much for watching him walk in.

He sat with his back to the barred gate and his wrapped hand resting on his knee, looking up at the route board like it owed him money. The swelling under his eye had gone dark purple at the edges, and the split in his lip had scabbed. Somebody had rewrapped the hand. His locs were dry this time, pulled back and tied, which showed off more of his face than she needed to see right now.

He turned his head and saw her. Isola had been ready for a lot of things. She'd been ready for him to stand up and try to loom, or to grin at her like he'd won something, or to look past her for whoever she might have brought. He just looked at her. His eyes went to her face, and her hands, and the two rings, and back up, and stayed. Something in his face eased. She couldn't name it and didn't trust it. The corner of his mouth went up, split lip and all.

"Salt," he said.

"Graves."

She sat down. Her knees, under the table, came within an inch of his. She moved hers to the side. He moved his to make room, and somehow they ended up closer.

Her stomach did something stupid. She ignored it.

"Rae said Micah's sister," he said.

"Rae said you wanted to see what I found."

"I do."

"Rae also said not to take nothing from you I can't give back." Isola settled her coat. "So I ain't here to take. I'm here to collect."

He tilted his head.

"You knocked eighteen Veils of funeral grade into a puddle," she said. "You stood right there at Pell's counter and told me the debt was yours. So it's yours. I'm calling it in."

"I paid Pell."

"You paid Pell. You ain't paid me."

He looked at her for a long second, and she watched him decide whether to argue. The smile came back instead, slower, like it hurt and he'd decided he didn't care.

"What's it cost?"

"Answers. I ask, you tell me the truth, and we're square."

"And if I ask you something back?"

"You can ask," Isola said. "Wasn't nothing in the debt about me answering."

He laughed. It came out low and short, like it had caught him off guard, and it changed his whole face. The bruise and the scabbed lip and the bandage didn't do a thing to stop him from looking good, and Isola sat there across from him with two Ironcross clerks pretending not to listen and thought, very clearly, *Girl, focus.*

She put her hand in her skirt and took out the coin. She set it on the table on her side of the brass and kept two fingers on it. He looked at it for a while without moving. Dark silver. Grey creeping in at the edges. The Veiled One's face on the front, smooth where somebody's thumb had been worrying it.

He reached for it. Isola closed her fist over the coin before his fingers got to the line.

His hand stopped right at the brass. He didn't pull it back, and he didn't push. Instead, he looked at her fist and up at her face, waiting to see what she'd do.

"Ask," she said.

He held her eyes. "Can I see it?"

She opened her hand and held it out across the line, palm up. He picked the coin up off her palm with two fingers, and his skin didn't touch hers. She would've bet the house money nobody that size could manage it.

He turned it over under the lamp and rubbed his thumb across the face, slow, and held his thumb up so she could see the grey it left behind.

"Jude tested one like this this morning," he said. "Not this one. A gold route token out of a dead man's locker. Somebody'd dressed it up so it'd pass for Crownlight issue. Same grey under the gold."

"Whose locker?"

"Man who sold a runner route with three kids on it." His voice stayed even. "One of them's still missing."

Isola looked at the coin in his hand. She'd thought about this coin as Asha's problem since the minute she found it in the bag lining, and Asha's problem was big enough to take up all the room she had. She hadn't once thought about whose problem it had been before it got to Asha.

He set the coin down on her side of the brass and slid it back with one finger. Isola picked it up. A minute later, when he asked whether it had been wrapped in anything when she found it, she held it out to him again so he could look at the edge. He gave it back. She asked him what the grey was, and he took it again to show her where it came up thickest. It went back and forth like that more than either of them needed. She knew it, and she could tell he knew it. Neither of them said anything.

"Okay," Isola said. "What is it?"

"You know what it's called."

"I know what the assayer called it, in front of a charm-wire man and his customer, right before she told me to get it off her counter. I'm asking what it *is*."

He told her. It wasn't crew work. Crews pressed lead and painted it. This had real quicksilver on the face and a binding under it that you couldn't buy anywhere he knew of, which meant somebody was cooking it. It was built to look new and Crownlight-clean, and you didn't go to that kind of trouble for coins you planned to spend on bread in the Back Market.

"It's built to climb," he said. "Somebody's making it down here and sending it up."

"Up how?"

He watched her a second before he answered. "There's a dead box on Letter Row. Blue Six. Things go in and things come out, and nobody sees who does either one."

"How you know that?"

"A runner told me last night."

"He just told you?"

"He was having a bad night." His eyes went back to the coin. "I asked nice."

Isola remembered the rail bridge. The man on his knees in the gravel, his arm bent up behind him, and Silas standing over him so calm he might've been waiting on a lift. Up on the span in the dark, her heart had been going like a drum. She'd watched him set that arm up to break, and she'd watched him not break it, and she still didn't know why.

"You knew somebody was on that bridge," she said.

He looked up.

"Rail bridge," Isola said. "You looked right at the top of the span."

"I knew somebody was."

"You didn't come up."

"Whoever it was could've come down."

"Whoever it was had good sense."

He let that sit between them for a moment, and she watched him put it together, her coat and her boots and the bridge. Surprise never showed up on his face. If anything, he looked like a man who'd been hoping.

"You were following me," he said.

"I was following somebody else. You were in the way." She lifted her chin. "Again."

The corner of his mouth went up. He leaned forward and put his elbows on his knees, which brought him into her space and put his face level with hers. Up close, she could smell soap and something under it like cedar.

"You know who carried this," he said quietly. "Whose bag it was in."

Isola didn't answer. She kept her eyes on his and her hand flat over the coin.

"I could find out," he said. "You know I could. I could pull that name out of you if I needed it."

It didn't come out like a threat. He said it the way he'd said *Debt's mine*, flat and certain, like he was telling her what time it was. Isola felt her pulse jump in her throat, and she kept her hands still on the table so he wouldn't see it.

"I watched you not break a man's arm," she said. "You ain't gone break mine."

He stayed exactly where he was. She watched his jaw work once. When he sat back, he took his elbows off his knees and his face out of her space and put both back on his own side of the brass.

"No," he said. "I'm not."

---

She asked him the rest of her questions after that. Who else knew about Blue Six? Jude, he said, and one other person he trusted. Was the missing runner a girl or a boy? A girl, eleven. Had anybody come around asking him about a package that burned at Blue Circle?

His eyes sharpened. "Why?"

Isola had planned to keep it. She'd planned it all the way down the stairs. He'd given her Blue Six and the dead man's locker and a missing eleven-year-old without trying to sell her any of it, and she'd been raised by Selah Wraithmoor, who'd told her a hundred times that you paid for what you got even when nobody was making you.

"After the den blew," she said, "I was behind a vat on Bath Row, waiting on some runners to clear out. They were talking about the package. Said it came in on Ora's name and it burned and they don't care how. Somebody's paying for it anyway." She watched his face. "They said somebody called Blackwood is collecting. And if it don't come out of the den, they're charging it to the girl you walked in with. The redhead."

He went very still.

"She's not mine," he said.

"She was with you."

"She's a job."

He said it flat. Isola could tell he believed it, and she could also tell from his face that he didn't like how it sounded, because he looked away from her and up at the route board and stayed there while his jaw worked.

"Blackwood," he said.

"That's what they said."

He brought his eyes back to her. "Thank you."

Isola hadn't expected that. She'd expected him to ask how she'd gotten behind a vat on Bath Row, or what she'd been doing at the den, or who she'd been following. He didn't ask any of it. He just sat there having thanked her, and she had to look down at the coin to keep her face from doing anything.

From inside his coat, he took out something small and black.

It was a route tag, the flat enameled kind runners clipped inside their collars in the Back Market, with three short silver lines cut into the face. He set it on the table on his side of the brass, right at the edge, and took his hand away.

"Black three," he said. "It's my route. Hold it to your phone and whatever you send comes straight to me. Nobody reads it on the way, and nobody logs it." He nodded at the coin. "If you find out where that came from, or somebody comes to your door asking about a package that burned, or you hear that name again."

Isola looked at the tag. She left her hands where they were.

"Why do you trust me?"

"I don't."

She looked up at him.

"I'm giving you a way to tell me when I should," Silas said.

She thought about it. He'd given her an answer that didn't flatter either one of them, and she could respect that. She looked at his hand, still resting on the table an inch from the tag, an inch from the line.

He moved it, drawing it back slow to set it on his knee and leaving the tag by itself on the table.

Isola reached across the brass and picked up black three. It was warm from his coat. She turned it over, looked at the three silver lines, and put it in the inside pocket of her coat with Rae's cord.

"What if I need something," she said, "that ain't about the coin?"

He didn't answer right away. She watched him think about it, and she watched him decide.

"Use the same route."

---

She stood up first. He stood up on his side, and the table was so small that standing put them closer than sitting had. She had to tilt her head back to look at him, same as in the lane.

"We square?" he asked.

"We're square."

She turned around and walked toward the Ashvein gate and got three steps before she stopped. Both clerks were watching her from their booths, and she could feel him watching her from the line, and she stood there with her back to all of them and argued with herself.

In the lane, he'd asked for her name with his eyes and she hadn't given it to him. He'd been calling her Salt ever since, like that was the only thing she was good for. Rae had given him Micah's sister. He probably had Wraithmoor by now, and he could get the rest of it from anybody in Sixth Ward who'd ever bought candles from her godmother.

She wanted him to get it from her.

Isola turned around. "Isola."

She didn't give him the rest. Wraithmoor belonged to her daddy and the wall and four generations of names, and that half could stay in Ashvein where it was safe. The first one was hers. Her mama had picked it before anybody else got a say.

He didn't say anything back, just stood there on his side of the brass with his wrapped hand at his side, and she watched him take her name and hold onto it like he was afraid to drop it.

She left before her face could give her away.

---

The man was waiting at the top of the stairs.

She'd signed out in the clerk's ledger and started up the half staircase, and she saw his coat before she saw his face. Plain, dark, cut long. He was leaning against the wall where the corridor turned, the way you'd lean if you were waiting on a friend, and he didn't look at her when she came up.

Everybody looked at you in a corridor that narrow. You looked so you'd know how to get past each other. The only people who didn't look were the ones who already knew who you were.

She'd seen that coat at the end of Pell's counter, staring at Rae's token in her hand.

Isola kept her pace. She kept her eyes on the steps and her hand off her pocket and walked past him like he was part of the wall. At the turn, where the lamps were spaced too far apart and there was one dark stretch between them, she let the Veil slip.

She'd been able to do it since she was small and had never once explained it to anybody who couldn't already do it, and she'd quit trying. All she knew was that she could make herself thin, and a man looking for her would look right past her to the next thing over.

She heard his coat scrape off the wall behind her and felt him come around the turn, and she kept walking, up the stairs and through the door at the top and out into the coal smoke of Ashvein. When he got to the top of those stairs, he'd find an empty corridor and a lamp that needed oil.

She held it for three streets. Letting go hit her the way a warm kitchen did after the cold, all at once, and she had to stop at the corner by the shrine with the broken roof and put her hand on the wall until her legs remembered her.

Her hands were shaking. She took out her phone and black three, held the tag against the back of the phone until the screen lit up with three silver lines, and typed *There was a man on the stairs. He was at Pell's.*

Her thumb hovered over it. He'd tell her to stay home, and he'd ask who the man was and why he'd been at Pell's and what he'd been looking at, and somewhere in the answer to that was Rae, and somewhere past Rae was Asha.

Isola deleted it one letter at a time, put the tag back in her coat, and walked home the long way, past every auntie's window on the block.
