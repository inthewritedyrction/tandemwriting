# CHAPTER 5: SILAS

Three hours on the step, and the only thing that had tried to kill Silas was the rain.

It came off the cavern ceiling in long, steady ropes, soaked through his collar, and found every place Jerrick had hit him. His left shoulder locked up a little more every hour. Gutterlight roared somewhere behind the rooftops whenever a match ended, and in between, the street in front of the dark house stayed empty enough that his hand kept drifting toward his knife without being told.

He'd counted everybody who walked past. A drunk Ring fan arguing with his own shadow. Two girls in work coats sharing an umbrella with a hole in it. A boy with a cart of empty bottles who looked at Silas's face, looked at the knife hand resting on his knee, and found a reason to cross the street. Not one of them had been worth getting up for.

His phone buzzed against his thigh.

Remy: You home?

Silas: Working.

Remy: That ain't a place.

Silas: It is tonight.

Remy: Dante?

Silas didn't answer that. Remy knew what no answer meant. Three dots showed up and went away twice before the next message came through.

Remy: Eat something. I'm bringing food in the morning.

Silas: You don't know where I am.

Remy: Lorraine do.

Naturally, Lorraine did. Silas put the phone away before he had to think about how many people in this city knew more about his night than he did.

Jude hadn't texted at all, which meant the token was still on his bench and Jude was still bent over it with his glasses on top of his head, ignoring everybody, including God. Rhea hadn't texted either. That was normal. She texted when she wanted to know if he was alive and went quiet when she didn't want him knowing she cared.

Behind him, the lock turned.

He was on his feet with the knife out before Camille's thread finished loosening. The door opened about four inches. Melena stood in the gap with the hood down and her hair out of its last pins, red as a warning lamp in the dark of the hall. She looked at the knife.

He put it away.

"You said you'd sit," she said.

"I was sitting."

"You've been sitting for three hours."

"That's what sitting is."

She didn't think that was funny. Rain blew in across the threshold and spotted the hem of the mourning dress, and she didn't step back from it. Down the street, the Ring went up in another roar, and her eyes cut toward the sound.

"What *is* that?"

"Gutterlight. Somebody's getting hit."

"All night?"

"Till somebody stops getting up."

She took that in without blinking. He was starting to notice that about her. Whatever you handed her, she held it and looked at it before she decided what to do with it, like she'd been raised in a house where you didn't get to drop things.

"I want to say something," she said. "Before I lose my nerve."

"Go ahead."

"In your father's office. You grabbed my wrist."

"You were falling."

"I'm aware of what I was doing."

"You'd rather I let you eat the floor?"

"I'd rather you asked." Her hand had found the edge of the door and was holding it tight. "I have one rule. Nobody touches me without asking. Not you. Not anyone."

Silas looked at her. "And if you're on fire?"

She thought about it longer than he expected. "Ask quickly."

He almost laughed. "There a two?"

"I'll let you know."

The door shut. The lock turned, and the thread drew tight against the frame. Silas stood in the rain and looked at that door for a second, the way you'd look at a dog that had just told you its name.

He sat back down.

His shoulder settled against the doorframe, and he closed his eyes for exactly one breath, and Pell's lane came back before he could stop it. The girl from Ashvein with her hand jammed in her skirt like she had a whole bank in there, looking up at him like he'd personally dumped her salt in the puddle on purpose. Silver down the lengths of her locs. A ring in her nose. Those two Ironcross rings, heavy enough to split a lip if she ever decided to swing.

*I ain't trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere.*

Most people who got knocked into by a Graves in Gutterlight said sorry first and figured out why later. She'd cussed him out and turned down his money.

He hadn't stopped thinking about it all night. That was a problem, and he didn't have time for problems with silver locs and a smart mouth. He had a girl from seven tiers up behind a door with rules, and a shoulder that wasn't going to be worth a damn by morning.

An hour later, the step was warm.

Silas sat up off the doorframe. It wasn't his body heat. He'd been sitting on cold stone for four hours, and it had stayed cold the whole time. Now it was warm under his thighs and getting warmer, and a thin skin of steam was lifting off the wet stone around his boots.

Behind him, Camille's thread snapped.

He felt it in his back teeth, a hard twang like a string on a bass going all at once. He was on his feet with the token out before he'd decided to stand. The lock plate took the brass. The ward checked him, slower than it should've, like something had already been through it and left it confused.

Smoke hit him in the face when the door opened.

The front room was empty. The cot was untouched. Down the hall, the back-room door hung open six inches, and where the lock used to be, there was a lump of brass the size of his thumb, still glowing at the edges and dripping down the wood in a bright little line.

"Melena."

Nothing.

He shoved the door open the rest of the way. The back room was exactly what Camille had built it to be: four walls, a bed, a lamp, and no windows. That was the point. No windows meant nobody could look in. It also meant the only other way out was the Mercer service door in the back wall, iron, with a Mercer lock and a double line of funeral thread sewn across the seam.

The thread was ash. The lock had run down the iron like candle wax. The door stood open to the back lane, and rain was blowing in across the floor and hissing where it hit.

Silas stood there for one second and let himself think it.

*Dante's gonna kill me.*

He could hear it already. *I gave you one job, son.* And Camille wouldn't say anything at all, which was worse.

He went out the back door.

---

She wasn't hard to follow. She wasn't trying to be.

Rain hit everything in Shadowmere and ran off it, but it didn't run off the places she'd touched. On the brick at the mouth of the back lane, two handprints steamed like somebody had pressed hot irons there. Ten steps on, a drainpipe had a black scorch shaped like a palm. Farther down, in a puddle, the water had gone warm enough to smoke.

She'd gone left at the dye vats, away from the Ring. Toward the market, toward the open lanes, toward the part of Gutterlight where the street widened and you could actually look up and see the cavern ceiling through the mist.

She was looking for sky. Same as in the alley outside Dante's.

"Damn it," Silas said, and ran.

His shoulder screamed every time his left foot hit the stone. He held his arm against his ribs and kept going, past the shuttered pawn counter, past the tailor row, through a gap between two buildings so narrow his coat dragged both walls. A woman leaning out a window above him yelled something about his mama. He didn't stop to answer.

He came out onto Lamp Street and the noise went away.

It didn't fade. It went all at once, like a door shutting. One second there was rain hitting tin and a tram grinding somewhere south and the Ring roaring behind him, and the next there was nothing. The rain kept falling. He could see it coming down in the lamplight and bouncing off the stone, and it didn't make a sound. His own boots landed silent. His breath went in and out of him without a single noise he could hear.

The lamps on Lamp Street dimmed to blue.

Everybody on the street knew what that meant, and everybody moved. A man selling skewers pulled his cart into a doorway. Two kids who'd been splashing in the gutter disappeared like somebody had erased them. A woman yanked her shutters closed without a sound. In ten seconds, the whole street was empty except for the rain.

And Melena.

She was standing in the middle of the street with her face turned up and her hood down, the rain running through her hair, and her hands were lit. She wasn't on fire yet, but the black under her nails had climbed to her knuckles, and a dull orange glow was coming through the skin of her palms like a lamp behind a curtain.

At the far end of Lamp Street, the Shrouds came around the corner.

There were two of them. Tall, taller than him, in layered grey veils that hung to the ground and didn't move right in the wind. Bone marks stitched down the front of them. Strips of paper hung from their heads and shoulders, hundreds of them, fluttering, and every strip had writing on it. Names. Silas had seen Shrouds before, the way everybody in Shadowmere had, from a distance, from behind a door, through a crack. He'd never once wanted to see one closer.

They moved down the street without walking. Their veils never touched the ground.

Melena hadn't seen them. She was still looking up.

Silas had about four seconds to think about her rule.

He couldn't ask. Nothing anybody said under Silence came out, and even if it did, the Shrouds would hear it before she did. He couldn't call her name. He couldn't do anything at all except what he was about to do, and she was going to hate him for it, and he was going to do it anyway.

He crossed the street in six silent strides and took her.

His right arm went around her waist. He pulled her backward off her feet and into the recessed doorway of a closed threshold shop, and she fought him the whole way. She was stronger than she looked and she didn't fight like a princess. She drove her elbow back at his ribs, his bad side, and found exactly the spot Jerrick had found, and his vision went white for a second. He held on.

Her hands were the problem. They were glowing brighter now, and the light was spilling out across the wet stone in front of the doorway like a sign that said *here*.

Silas grabbed both of her wrists in his left hand and pressed her palms flat against his own chest, under his coat, and closed the coat over them.

It burned.

It burned like grabbing a pot handle you forgot was on the stove, except you couldn't let go. The heat went through his shirt and into his skin and into his palm where it was clamped over the backs of her hands. He felt the skin on his palm go tight, then wet. He bit down on the inside of his cheek and didn't move.

Melena had gone still against him. Her back was pressed to his chest. Her pulse was going so hard he could feel it through her wrists, through his own burned hand.

The Shrouds passed the doorway.

Silas could see them over the top of her head. Up close, the name strips weren't paper. They were thinner than paper and they moved like they were breathing. One Shroud stopped. It turned its veiled head toward the threshold shop, slow, and Silas watched the name strips across its face lift and settle, lift and settle, like something under there was tasting the air.

Melena's hands got hotter.

He pressed them harder against his chest and put his mouth next to her ear even though she couldn't hear him, and breathed out long. Longer than he breathed in. Same as at Pell's. *Count it.*

He didn't know if she felt it. He did it again anyway.

On the third breath, the heat dropped. Not all the way. Just enough that the glow under the coat went from orange to red to almost nothing.

The Shroud turned its head away and moved on.

He held her there until both of them were gone around the far corner, and the lamps on Lamp Street went from blue back to amber, and the sound came back all at once like somebody had kicked a door open. Rain on tin. A tram. The Ring, roaring for somebody who'd just gotten hit in the face. Somewhere above them, a shutter opened and a woman said, "Lord have mercy," to nobody.

Silas let go.

Melena turned around and shoved him hard enough that his back hit the door behind him.

"I said *ask*."

"Couldn't."

"You absolutely could have—"

"Under Silence?" He held up his left hand. The palm was red and shining, a blister already lifting across the middle of it where the backs of her hands had been. "Nothing you say comes out under Silence. That's the whole point of it. Nobody hears you scream, and nobody hears you ask permission either."

She looked at his hand.

He watched the fight go out of her face, fast, and something worse come into it. She looked at the blister like she'd been the one holding the pot.

"I didn't—" she started.

"I know."

"I didn't mean to—"

"I know." He closed the hand and put it in his coat. "You can be mad at me later. Right now, I need you to tell me why you burned through two Mercer locks and a Graves ward to come stand in the middle of the street under the Synod's nose."

Her chin came up. A second later, it dropped. She looked past him, back up at the ceiling of the cavern, where the rain came out of the rock and the mist glowed violet and there wasn't anything else up there at all.

"There are no windows," she said.

Silas waited.

"In that room. There are no windows." Her voice cracked down the middle. "I lay down and I couldn't — I couldn't breathe. It's a box. You put me in a box and you sat on the lid."

He thought about the back room. Four walls and a lamp. Camille had built it like that on purpose, for people running from things, people who wanted walls.

Melena wasn't running from things. She'd lost somebody, and she'd just come down seven tiers to a place with no sky.

"Sky's six tiers up," he said. "You won't find it in Gutterlight."

"I know that."

"You were looking anyway."

She didn't deny it. She pulled the hood back up over her hair with hands that weren't glowing anymore, and when she spoke again her voice had gone flat and careful, like her voice in Dante's office.

"Is your hand going to be all right?"

"Been worse."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the one I got." He stepped out of the doorway and checked the street both ways. The skewer man was rolling his cart back out like nothing had happened, which was exactly what he'd do tomorrow if anybody asked. "Come on. Walk on my left. If I stop, you stop."

"You said that this afternoon."

"Yeah. You didn't listen this afternoon either."

He started walking. After a second, he heard her boots behind him. Rhea's boots, a size too big, slapping wet stone.

---

Camille was standing in the front room of the dark house when they got back.

She was in her house shoes. She'd come across Gutterlight in the rain in her robe and her house shoes, which meant she hadn't stopped to change after her thread snapped, which meant she was furious. She had her glasses on her face instead of on top of her head. That was worse.

She looked at Silas. She looked at Melena. She looked at the back-room door hanging open with a brass tear running down it.

"Ma," Silas said.

"Sit down."

He sat down on the cot.

Camille turned to Melena, and her face changed. All the fury went somewhere Silas couldn't see it, and what was left was the face she used for folks who came to her door after a funeral. She didn't step closer. She didn't reach.

"You're soaked through, baby," she said. "May I take your coat?"

Melena stared at her.

"I'll hang it by the stove. I won't touch you."

Melena unbuttoned the coat herself and held it out at arm's length. Camille took it by the collar and hung it on the hook by the cold stove, then came back and stood in front of Silas with her arms folded.

"Hand."

He held out the right one.

"Other hand, Silas."

He held out the left. She turned it to the lamp, looked at the blister, and made a sound in her throat he hadn't heard since Jude broke his arm jumping off the tram platform at nine.

"Who did this?"

"Shrouds came through Lamp Street under Silence," Silas said. "She was out in the open. I had to get her out of it."

"I asked who did this."

Melena said, "I did."

Camille didn't look away from his hand. "Did he grab you?"

"Yes."

"Did you tell him not to?"

"Yes."

"Mm-hmm." Camille opened the medical tin from his coat, the one Dante had given him that afternoon, and pulled out a jar of something grey that smelled like river mud. "So you both did something you're gon' have to live with. Hold still."

She packed the burn. It felt like sticking his hand in snow. He hissed through his teeth, and she ignored him and wrapped it in clean cloth until it looked like he was wearing a mitten.

"Shoulder."

"It's fine."

"Your shoulder been fine since last night, I know. Take the shirt off."

He looked at Melena. Melena turned around to face the wall without being asked, and he would've thanked her if he'd had the energy. He got the shirt over his head. Camille pressed two cold fingers under his shoulder blade, and he swore.

"Jerrick?"

"Elbow."

"He's dead and still getting his licks in." She put the shirt back in his lap. "You'll soak it again when you get home. If you ever get home."

She went to the back room.

Silas pulled his shirt on and followed her as far as the doorway. Camille stood in front of the service door with her hand flat on the iron, not touching the slag where the lock had been, just the door. She had her eyes closed. Her lips moved.

When she opened her eyes, she looked older.

"I sewed this ward myself," she said. "Double-stitched it. Three Graves knots at the seam."

"I know."

"She went through it like it was paper."

"I know, Ma."

Camille pulled a spool of funeral thread from her robe pocket. She always had one. Silas had seen her pull funeral thread out of her bra at a wedding. She started laying a new line across the seam of the door, fast, her fingers moving in the old pattern, and the thread hummed as it went down, low enough that he felt it more than heard it.

"It'll hold tonight," she said, still sewing. "It'll hold tomorrow. Maybe the day after, if she sleeps." She tied off the knot and bit the thread. "It won't hold a week."

"Dante said till Rae says."

"Rae better say soon, then." She turned around. "Whatever's in that girl don't answer to Graves thread. It don't answer to death at all. I don't know what it answers to." She looked at him over her glasses. "And neither do you. So stop acting like you do."

He opened his mouth.

"Don't," Camille said.

He closed it.

She went past him into the front room. Melena had turned back around. She was sitting on the very edge of the sofa with her knees together and her hands in her lap, and between her hands was the envelope from Dante's office, the crushed one with the broken red seal.

Camille stopped in front of her. "I'm going to go home and sit up and listen to my thread," she said. "If it moves, I'm coming back. In my house shoes. And I'm too old to be crossing Gutterlight in my house shoes twice in one night, so please, baby. Stay in the house."

Melena looked up at her for a long moment. "I'll try."

"That's more honest than he's ever been with me." Camille picked up her umbrella from by the door. "Silas. Eat something."

"Remy's bringing food in the morning."

"Lord, at least somebody in your life got sense." The door closed behind her. The thread on the frame drew tight.

Silas stood in the middle of the front room and listened to his mother's house shoes slap away down the wet street until he couldn't hear them anymore.

---

He should've gone back to the step.

He didn't. He leaned against the wall by the cold stove with his mitten hand against his chest and watched Melena not open the envelope for a while.

She turned it over. She ran her thumb along the broken seal. She set it on her knee and looked at it like it might get up and leave. He knew that look. He'd seen it on people at the Ring, holding the last thing somebody had given them before the Quiet Staff carried the body out.

"You don't have to read it tonight," he said.

"I've read it."

"So you don't have to read it again."

"I want to." She didn't look up. "I just haven't decided if I want you watching."

"I'll look at the stove."

He looked at the stove. It was a cold iron stove that hadn't been lit in months, and there wasn't a single thing about it worth looking at, and he looked at it anyway while paper rustled across the room.

She read for a long time. He could hear her breathing. It went ragged twice, and both times the room got warmer, and both times she pushed out a long breath, longer than she took in, and the heat backed off.

She'd learned it. Two tries and she'd already learned it.

He glanced over without meaning to.

She'd unfolded the letter all the way across her knees, three pages in a looping, slanted hand, and her head was bent over the bottom of the last page. From where he stood, the top of the first page was right side up and facing him, and the lamp was right on it.

It didn't start *Melena*.

It started *My dearest Elias.*

Silas looked at that name a second too long.

Melena felt it. She looked up, and she saw where his eyes were, and her whole face shut like a door. She folded the letter in half. She folded it in half again, fast, and the edges started to smoke between her fingers.

"Who's Elias?" Silas asked.

Melena held the smoking letter against her chest, both hands, the way she'd held it in Dante's office before anybody had told her where she was going.

"Ask me something else," she said.
# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Asha missed dinner, and in the Wraithmoor house, missing dinner without sending word was about one step below murder.

Selah set her plate out anyway. Cornbread, greens, the last of the smoked turkey neck cut up in the beans so it'd stretch. She put the plate at Asha's place at the end of the table, between Tevin and the wall, and laid a clean cloth over it like Asha was gonna walk in any second and sit down.

Nobody said anything about the plate. Everybody looked at it.

Omari came to the table last, the way he always did, winding his watch. He'd set it right that morning and it was already eleven minutes behind, the same as every day. He sat down at the head of the table, looked at the cloth over the plate, and looked at Isola.

Isola passed the cornbread to Micah.

"Where your cousin?" Omari said.

"Errands," Selah answered for her, and said grace before anybody could ask what kind.

Omari let her get through all of it. Thanks for the food, thanks for the roof, keep the names on the wall and the Shrouds off the block, amen. When she was done, he picked up his fork and didn't use it.

"Errands," he said.

"That's what she told me this morning." Selah spooned greens onto his plate without being asked. "Eat."

He ate. That was the thing about Omari. He'd let you have your little lie as long as you served it to him hot.

Across the table, Tevin was eating like somebody was gonna take the plate. He'd stayed in the spare room all afternoon with the door shut, and he'd come out the second he smelled cornbread, which Isola had known he would. You couldn't keep a grown man mad on an empty stomach. She'd learned that from Selah before she was ten.

His locs were still pulled back neat in front. He hadn't been out. She'd have heard the door.

"You good?" Isola asked him.

Tevin didn't look up. "I'm eating."

"I see that."

"So why you asking?"

"'Cause you eating like the beans owe you money."

Micah laughed into his cup. Tevin cut his eyes at both of them, and something in his shoulders let go a little.

"Didn't go nowhere," he said. "If that's what you asking."

"I know you ain't."

"How you know?"

"'Cause I'd be at the east gate right now, cussing out a boy with a red armband, and I'm not. I'm eating greens."

Tevin snorted and went back to his plate.

Micah's phone buzzed.

It was in Isola's skirt pocket, on the side without the coin, where she'd kept it since noon. Micah's whole body went up like a dog hearing a can opener.

"That's Danika," he said.

"You don't know that."

"Who else texting me?"

"Half of Sixth Ward, probably. Every girl you ever lied to." Isola pulled it out and looked at the screen anyway, because she wanted to know too.

Danika: Packet's here. Reading it tonight. Tell Kai I said don't get excited.

Isola read it twice. Something in her chest that had been clenched since twelve-oh-four eased just a little.

"Well?" Micah said.

"She said don't get excited."

"That mean she got it?"

"That mean she got it."

Micah put both fists in the air like he'd won a fight. Selah said "Micah Amari" without looking up from her plate, and his fists came down. He was still grinning.

Isola slid the phone across the table to him. He looked at it, then at her, suspicious.

"For real?"

"Packet's gone. Danika got it. I can't hold your phone hostage for the rest of your life."

"You could try," Tevin muttered.

"I could." Isola picked up her fork. "Don't make me."

---

She and Micah did the dishes after.

That was the deal. Selah cooked, the kids cleaned, Omari checked the locks. He was doing it now, out in the front room. Isola could hear him. The deadbolt slid, slid back, slid again. A pause, where she knew his fingers were resting on the upper lock. After that, his boots back down the hall and his door clicking closed.

Micah washed. Isola dried. He'd gotten too tall for the sink a year ago and had to bend over it like an old man, and he complained about it every single night like it was news.

"So," he said. "You find out anything down there?"

She'd been waiting for that since dinner. "Down where?"

"Shadowmere. You came back smelling like rain. Ain't no rain in Ashvein."

"I went for Rae's salt."

"Uh-huh. And you came back with the evening cut in double paper." He handed her a bowl. "Mama asked you if you found Asha. I heard her. And you said crowd."

Isola dried the bowl. "I saw her."

He stopped scrubbing.

"In the Back Market. By the bend behind the freight sheds. She had on a coat I ain't never seen before in my life, Kai. Long. Fitted. And a bag." She shook her head. "Expensive. Like Ironcross-expensive."

"Asha?"

"Asha."

"Where she get money for a coat like that?"

Isola picked up the next plate and didn't answer, and he watched her not answer. He'd been watching her not answer things his whole life. He was getting good at reading it.

"So what happened?" he said. "You just let her walk off?"

"I didn't *let* her do nothing. Some man backed into me and knocked the salt out my hand, and by the time I got done cussing him out, she was gone."

"What man?"

"Some Shadowmere man." She put the plate on the stack harder than she meant to. "Tall. Like, *tall* tall. Somebody had beat his face in recently, he had a busted lip and his eye all swole, and his hand was wrapped. Locs. Beard. Standing there in the rain letting it run all in his beard like he ain't have the sense to wipe it."

Micah had stopped washing.

"And he had a girl with him," Isola went on. "Behind him. Hiding. Red hair. And he paid Pell four Veils for my salt like it was nothing. *Four*. Pell charged me eighteen. And he looked at me like—"

She stopped.

Micah was looking at her with a slow, terrible smile spreading over his whole face.

"Like what?" he said.

"Like nothing. He was rude."

"Why you know what his beard look like?"

"Because it was *in front of me*, Micah."

"Pell call him anything?"

Isola thought about it. "Graves."

Micah dropped the sponge in the water and laughed so hard he had to hold onto the counter.

Isola waited. She folded her arms. She waited some more. He didn't stop. He laughed until he was wheezing, and Selah called "What's so funny?" from the back room, and Micah called back "Nothing, Mama!" in a voice that was still cracking, and went right back to laughing.

"I'm glad you think this is funny," Isola said.

"Isola." He wiped his eyes with his wet wrist. "Isola. That's *Silas*."

"Who?"

"Silas Graves. Jude's brother." He waited. She gave him nothing. "Dante's *son*. My godfather's son. Isola, come on."

She knew who Dante was. Everybody in this house knew who Dante was. He'd stood up in front of a whole room of witnesses and spoken Micah's name, and Asha's, and every year on their name days a package showed up on the step with no note and no return route on it. But Dante's world was Micah's world, and Asha's. Isola had Rae. She'd never gone to the godfamily dinners, and she'd never once wanted to.

"I never met Dante's kids," she said.

"Well, you met one today." Micah picked the sponge back up. He was still grinning. "Jude bring me to Gutterlight sometimes. You know what they call Silas at the Ring?"

"Graves, apparently."

"They call him Graves 'cause of what happens to the other man." Micah said it light, the way he said everything. "He fights for Rae sometimes. When somebody did something bad enough that it needs settling. Last night was Jerrick. Lowtorch man. The boards up here took more money on that fight than anything all month."

Isola stopped drying.

"He killed somebody," she said. "Last night."

"In the Ring. It's legal." Micah thought about it. "Mostly."

She thought about the man in the lane. The swelling under his eye. The split in his lip. The bandage across his knuckles. *Somebody had hit him recently.* Somebody had. And then that somebody had died.

And then he'd stood in the rain and paid for her salt.

"You let Silas Graves pay for your salt," Micah said, delighted. "Isola. You know how many girls in Sixth Ward would throw themselves in front of Silas Graves on purpose?"

"I didn't *let* him do nothing. I told him I ain't want his money."

"You told him *no*?" Micah started laughing again. "Oh, I gotta tell Jude."

"You're not telling Jude nothing."

"I'm telling Jude everything."

She snapped the towel at the back of his legs. He yelped and laughed harder. She snapped it again. Somewhere behind them Selah said "Y'all break one of my plates, I'm breaking something back," and they both shut up and finished the dishes in a silence so full of Micah trying not to laugh that Isola wanted to drown him in the rinse water.

She dried the last cup and hung the towel on the oven door.

*He killed somebody last night.* She'd felt it in her stomach when Micah said it, a cold little drop. That was the right reaction. That was what a sensible woman was supposed to feel about a man like that.

She was also still thinking about the rain in his beard.

That was not the right reaction. She hung the towel up a second time, straighter, like that would fix it.

---

Asha came home after final bell.

Isola was waiting for her. She'd sat down in the dark front room in Omari's chair at quarter till and hadn't moved since. The house was asleep around her. Tevin snoring through the spare-room door. "Si," Remy said from inside. "You coming?"

Silas looked at Remy's face in the dark of the doorway. Easy. Normal. Like this was just a door.

He filed it away and went in.

---

The den was bigger than he'd thought it would be.

The bathhouse had been built back when Shadowmere still had hot springs, and somebody had drained the pools and left them. Three of them, one deep and two shallow, all tiled in blue and white that had gone grey with age. People lay in the shallow ones on cushions and blankets and piles of coats. Twenty, maybe thirty. Most of them had their eyes open. All of them were smiling, the same small, empty smile, like they'd just remembered something nice and couldn't remember what.

Low blue lamps hung from the pipes overhead. Steam came up from somewhere and hung in the air. And all along the walls, sewn into the old tile grout in long careful lines, ran thread. Grey thread. Dozens of lines of it, crossing and crossing again.

Silas felt it hum as soon as he walked past it.

Grief thread. Lament work. Somebody had taken the grief of everybody who'd ever come through here and spun it into thread and sewn it into the walls. It hummed low, like a crowd of people crying in the next room. Camille would've burned this place to the ground if she ever saw it.

Melena had stopped walking. She was looking at the people in the pools.

"They look happy," she said.

"They look gone," Silas said.

"Is there a difference?"

Remy touched her elbow, then caught himself and took his hand back. "Sorry. Can I?"

She looked at his hand. "Yes."

He put two fingers on her elbow, light, and steered her toward the deep pool.

Miss Ora sat at the bottom of it.

Somebody had put a velvet chair down there on the drain grate, and Ora sat in it like a queen in a well. She was old. Older than Camille, older than Rae, thin as a broom handle, with her head wrapped in blue silk and a long pipe between her fingers. The smoke that came off it was blue too. A clerk stood at a counter by the steps with rows of tiny glass vials behind him, and two runners with blue cords on their wrists leaned against the tile, watching everybody who came in.

"Remington," Ora said. Her voice was soft, like somebody singing a baby to sleep. "You brought me a Graves."

"I brought you a friend, Miss Ora."

"Mm." Ora's eyes went over Silas, slow, from his boots to his face, and stopped at the bandage on his hand. "Dante's boy. The one who fights." She took a pull on the pipe. "Your mother sewed the threshold on my old house in Lowtorch, baby. Did you know that? Before your time. Best ward I ever had. Burned anyway."

"Didn't know that," Silas said.

"Not many do." She looked at Melena last. She looked at her the longest. "And this one."

Melena stood very still.

"This is who I told you about," Remy said. "One drop. Measured. In tea. We sit with her and watch."

"And then you go."

"And then we go."

Ora smoked for a while. The blue smoke rose up past the lip of the pool and spread out under the pipes.

"One drop," she said. "On the house. For you, Remington." She waved the pipe at the clerk without looking at him. "Make it clean."

---

The clerk measured it in front of them.

He was young, younger than Micah's age, with a thin face and nervous hands and a blue cord wrapped three times around his wrist. He took one vial off the shelf, uncapped it with his thumb, and let a single drop fall into a cup of tea he'd already poured. The drop was clear. It didn't change the color of the tea. It didn't change anything. He handed the cup to Remy, and Remy handed it to Melena, and Melena held it in both hands and looked at Silas.

"You don't have to," Silas said.

"I know." She drank it.

For about a minute, nothing happened. She sat on the steps of the pool with the empty cup in her lap and her shoulders up around her ears, and Silas stood two steps above her with his good hand near his knife and watched her face.

After that, her shoulders came down.

It was slow. Like watching somebody lower a heavy box to the floor. Her shoulders came down, and her hands, which had been holding the cup so tight her knuckles were pale, loosened. Her breath went long without her trying to make it long. She blinked a couple of times and looked around the den like she was seeing it for the first time.

"Oh," she said softly.

"How you feel?" Remy asked. He'd sat down on the step next to her. Not touching. Close.

"Quiet." She said it like a word in a language she was just learning. "It's quiet."

"Good quiet or bad quiet?"

She thought about it. "I don't know. I haven't had quiet since—" She stopped. She put her hand flat on her chest, where the letter was, under the borrowed sweater. "I can think about her. I can think about her and it doesn't—" She held her other hand up in front of her face. Normal. Brown. No black under the nails at all. "It doesn't come."

Remy smiled at her. It was the realest smile Silas had seen on him in a year. "Okay," he said. "Okay. That's good. That's real good."

Silas let out a breath he hadn't known he'd been holding since Dante's office.

It worked. It actually worked. One drop, and the fire was down, and she was sitting on a tiled step in a Hush den talking about her dead mother in a calm voice. They could do this. They could measure it, keep her steady, keep her hidden until Rae said, and nobody had to get burned and nobody had to know.

He looked up at the clerk's counter.

A girl was standing at it.

He hadn't seen her come in. She had her back to him, and she was in a long dark coat, fitted at the waist, a coat nobody in Gutterlight could afford, with a stiff-sided bag over her shoulder. She set a package on the counter. Wardcloth, tied with blue cord, about the size of a brick. The clerk picked up a black strip from a stack beside him, punched it with something that clicked, and handed it back to her without a word.

She left fast. Up the far stairs, toward the front gate, without looking back.

Silas watched her go. Something about the way she stood nagged at him. Weight back. Head turned, counting the turns.

He didn't know her. He turned back to Melena.

The clerk set the package on the shelf behind him, between the vials.

"Remington," Ora said from her chair. "Come here, baby. Let's talk about what you owe me now."

"I thought it was on the house."

"The drop is on the house. The door ain't."

Remy sighed and got up. He looked at Melena. "You good?"

"I'm good," she said, and she sounded it.

He went down into the deep pool. Silas watched him crouch next to Ora's velvet chair and start talking low, his hands moving, and Ora smiling her soft lullaby smile and smoking and shaking her head.

Silas turned back to the room.

The two runners against the far wall had stopped leaning. One of them was looking at Melena's head. The wrap had slipped again, just at the back, and there was red showing. Bright red under blue lamplight, the brightest thing in the room.

Silas moved. He went up two steps to put himself between the runner's line of sight and her, the same half step he'd done at Pell's, and stared at the runner until the runner found something else to stare at.

It took maybe fifteen seconds.

When he turned back around, Melena wasn't on the step anymore.

She was at the clerk's counter.

Silas saw it all in one piece, the way you saw a punch you couldn't stop. The clerk had a vial in his hand. A full one, capped, the clear liquid all the way to the top. Melena was holding something out to him across the counter, and the something was dark and caught the blue light, and it took Silas a second to understand it was one of the stones off her collar. He could see the loose thread where she'd torn it free.

The clerk was looking at Melena's face, not at the stone. He had a strange, slack look, like he was listening to something far away.

He took the stone. He gave her the vial.

"*Hey*—"

She uncapped it with her thumb, the way she'd watched him do it, and drank the whole thing before Silas got down the first step.

"*Melena*!"

Remy's head came up in the bottom of the pool. He saw the empty vial in her hand, and his face went grey.

"No," he said. "No, no, no—"

Melena turned around. She was smiling.

It was the den smile. The empty, gentle one, the one on every face in the shallow pools. She looked at Silas coming down the steps toward her, and she looked at Remy climbing out of the deep pool, and she held up the empty vial like she'd won something.

"It's so quiet," she said. "Silas. It's so *quiet*."

Her hands were normal. Her breath was slow. There was no black under her nails, nothing climbing, nothing going ragged. None of the signs. Nothing.

The air went white.

There was no warning. That was the thing Silas would remember later, lying awake with his hand throbbing. In Dante's office there'd been a warning. On Lamp Street there'd been a warning. This morning, with the letter, there'd been a warning. The Hush had taken every one of those away and left the fire sitting there in her with no door to knock on first.

It came out of her all at once.

It didn't come from her hands this time. It came from all of her. Heat rolled off her body like a furnace door kicked open, and the cup in the clerk's hand shattered, and every vial on the shelf behind him burst in a line, *pop pop pop pop*, like firecrackers. The clerk screamed. The blue cord on his wrist caught and he slapped at it.

The grief thread in the walls lit up.

It went like a fuse. Silas watched it happen and couldn't stop it. The first line caught by the counter and the fire ran along the grout, orange, fast, and hit the next line where they crossed, and that one caught, and that one, and in four seconds every line of thread in every wall of the den was burning, and the hum of all that grief turned into a scream.

The package on the shelf behind the clerk blew.

It went with a crack that Silas felt in his chest, and something inside it sprayed out in a bright silver mist that caught the fire and burned blue-white. Something hot and small hit Silas in the cheek. He touched it. A coin, or half of one, melted, stuck to his skin. He tore it off and kept moving.

The people in the shallow pools didn't run.

That was the worst part. The fire was running along the walls above their heads and the steam pipes were starting to scream, and they were lying there on their cushions with their gentle empty smiles, watching it like it was pretty.

"*Get up*!" Silas yelled at the nearest one, a man about Dante's age. "Get up, get out, *go*!"

The man smiled at him.

Silas grabbed him by the coat with his good hand and hauled him off the cushions and shoved him toward the stairs. The man stumbled, caught himself, kept walking, still smiling. Silas grabbed the next one. And the next. The runners were running, the clerk was running, Ora was standing up out of her velvet chair at the bottom of the deep pool with her pipe in her hand and her soft voice gone hard and loud, shouting at the clerk, and the clerk was shouting back.

"She got in my head! Ora, I swear, she got in my *head*, I didn't—"

Remy had reached Melena.

She was standing exactly where she'd been, by the counter, in the middle of it. Heat was coming off her in waves. The borrowed sweater had started to smoke at the hem. Her eyes were open and she was looking at the fire on the walls with that empty smile, and Remy stood in front of her, as close as the heat would let him, which wasn't close.

"Melena." His voice was shaking. "Melena, look at me. What color's my coat?"

She looked at him. Her smile didn't change.

"*What color is my coat*?"

"Green," she said, dreamy. "Ugly green."

"Good. Good. Can I pick you up? I need to pick you up and carry you out of here. Can I?"

She thought about it. Silas, dragging a woman out of the shallow pool by her arm, watched her think about it while the den burned down around her.

"Yes," she said.

Remy picked her up.

He did it fast and he did it right, one arm behind her back and one under her knees, and the heat coming off her must have been like hugging a stove, because Silas watched Remy's face twist and his jaw lock. He didn't put her down. He turned for the stairs.

"Not the front!" Silas shouted. "They'll see her!"

"The door!" Remy yelled back. "Our door!"

Silas shoved the woman up the stairs after the others and turned around one more time.

The clerk was gone. Ora was gone. The counter was on fire. And sitting on the counter, right in the middle of the flames, was a dark stone with a loose thread still hanging off it. Melena's mourning stone. The one she'd bought the vial with.

He didn't think about it. He grabbed it.

It burned his good hand, and he didn't care. He shoved it in his coat and ran.

---

The plain door in the plain brick wall was standing open when they got to it. Remy went through first with Melena in his arms, and Silas came through after, and behind them, Blue Circle came down.

He heard it more than saw it. A deep groan from the bathhouse above, and then the crack of the tile roof giving way, and then a rush of hot air up the stairwell behind them that knocked him into the wall. Smoke came boiling up out of the gap in the fence. Somewhere on the street side, people were screaming. A bell started ringing somewhere, then another.

Silas pulled the door shut. He didn't know if it would lock behind them. He didn't know if it locked at all.

They came out of the alley behind the noodle counter into the rain.

It was chaos. People pouring out of the front gate of the bathhouse onto the street, some of them burned, some of them still smiling. Runners with blue cords scattering in every direction. A man in a work coat standing in the middle of the road with his hands on his head, saying "*My package, my package*," over and over. Steam rising off everything. Blue Circle's lantern lay shattered in the gutter, still burning.

And somewhere out there, one of those runners was going to remember a red-haired girl at the counter.

"Where?" Remy said. He was breathing hard. Melena was limp in his arms now, her eyes half closed, still smiling a little. The heat had dropped enough that Remy's face wasn't twisted anymore, but steam was rising off both of them in the rain. "The dark house?"

Silas thought about the dark house. Camille's thread, already sewn twice. *It won't hold a week.* Every runner in Blue Circle had seen Remy and Silas walk in with her. Half of them would know Silas's face from the Ring by morning. Some of them had probably watched him sit on that step all last night.

And whatever was in Melena right now, full of Hush and burning without a warning, Camille's thread wasn't going to hold it till morning.

There was one threshold in Shadowmere he knew would hold. Camille had built it with her own hands the year he moved in. She'd sewn it with his blood in the thread, so it would know him. Every door in this city answered to somebody. His answered to him.

"Home," Silas said.

Remy looked at him. "*Your* home?"

"You got a better one?"

Remy didn't.

---

They took the last south tram.

Silas paid for the whole back car. It cost him most of what he had on him, and the tram man didn't ask questions because the tram man had seen the smoke rising over the Gutterlight rooftops and wanted no part of whatever these three were. They sat in the back. Remy held Melena across his lap with Silas's coat over her to hide the steam. Nobody else got on. At every stop, the doors opened onto empty platforms and closed again.

Silas sat across from them and watched the rain go by.

His burned palm was throbbing. His other hand had a fresh burn across the fingers from the stone. His cheek stung where the melted coin had hit him. He reached into his coat and took out the mourning stone and turned it over.

It was smooth and dark and heavy for its size, and when he tilted it toward the lamp, something moved inside it, deep down, like smoke trapped in glass. He'd never seen a stone like it. Neither had anybody in Gutterlight, he'd bet. She'd ripped it off her own dress and paid it to a boy with a blue cord for a vial of Hush, and the boy had taken it like he didn't know what it was.

*She got in my head.*

He put the stone away.

Across from him, Melena stirred. Her eyes opened a little. She looked up at Remy holding her, and she didn't pull away.

"You smell like smoke," she said.

"So do you," Remy said.

"I'm sorry."

"I know."

"I wanted it quiet." Her eyes closed again. "Just for a little while. I wanted it to be quiet."

Remy didn't say anything. He looked out the window at the rain with his jaw tight, and Silas watched his best friend hold a burning girl on a tram and try not to cry.

---

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane was dark when they got there. Silas went up the steps first and put his hand on the frame of the outer door.

The threshold knew him. It always did. He felt it the way he felt Camille's thread, a low hum under his palm, and when he pressed the burned hand flat to the wood and leaned his weight into it, the hum went deeper. Camille's work. Graves knots in every corner of the frame and his own blood stitched into the line.

"She's with me," he said to the door.

He didn't know if it understood words. He said it anyway.

The door opened.

Remy carried Melena across the threshold, and Silas held his breath and watched. If the ward didn't take her, it'd throw her back out into the street like the Mercer lock had thrown off his token last night. It'd fight her.

It didn't fight her. The hum under his hand went tight, then settled. Like it had decided.

The second floor had been empty since spring. Silas had a key, because Camille had a key to everything in this building and had given him copies of all of them in a jar labeled *DON'T LOSE THESE*. The apartment was bare. A mattress on the floor. A table. A radiator under the window that knocked every time the tram went by underneath the street.

It had a window. A real one, looking out over the lane. Silas noticed it as soon as he walked in and felt something in his chest unclench.

Remy laid Melena on the mattress. She curled onto her side and was asleep before he'd taken his arms back. The heat was coming off her slower now, steady, like a banked stove.

Remy stood over her and didn't move.

"I'm staying," he said.

It wasn't a question. Silas didn't treat it like one.

"I'll be upstairs," Silas said. "Bang on the radiator if something happens. I'll hear it."

"Where you going?"

Silas was already at the door. He turned around.

"That clerk sold her a full vial for a rock," he said. "And somebody's package blew up on his shelf. Somebody in that den knows who she is, or they're gonna find out by morning. I want to know who before they do."

"Si. Your hands."

"I got two more burns than I had this morning. I'll live."

"Dante's gonna find out."

"Dante already knows." Silas held up his phone. It had been buzzing in his pocket since the tram.

Remy looked at the screen. His face did something complicated.

The message was from Dante. Four words, no punctuation, sent eleven minutes after Blue Circle came down.
