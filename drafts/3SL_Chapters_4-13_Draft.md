# CHAPTER 4: ISOLA

The noon courier took fourteen Veilmarks, two more than Isola had meant to pay. The route inspectors were out. She'd known they might be, and she'd brought the fourteen, and it still felt like being robbed by somebody with manners.

He was a thin man with an Ironcross stamp on the back of his hand, and he checked the seal on Micah's packet twice before sliding it into the flat case at his hip. Isola counted the coins into his palm one at a time. She'd never listened to money before this morning. Now each one rang against the one beneath it on the way down, low and full, and she didn't let go of a single coin until she'd heard it.

Micah stood in the doorway behind her with his hands in his jacket, as if he could make the packet travel faster by staring at it. When the courier stepped back into the lane, Micah let his shoulders down for the first time since first bell.

"That's it?"

"That's it." Isola shut the door. "Now you just gotta not do nothing stupid until Danika write back."

"I been not doing nothing stupid for thirty days."

"Twenty-nine and a morning." His eyes went to his phone on the table, and Isola picked it up before he could. "And you still ain't getting this."

He sucked his teeth and dropped onto the couch, which meant he'd given up for now. With Micah, *for now* usually lasted until he got hungry. Isola looked up at the clock over the name wall. The minute hand had slipped past twelve while the courier counted.

Noon had come. Asha hadn't.

That was the deal Isola had made with herself in Asha's room that morning, standing over a torn bag with a counterfeit in her hand. If Asha came home before the courier, Isola would ask her to her face. If she didn't, Isola would take the salt run into Shadowmere and see what she could find out down there. She'd been hoping for the first one. The clock didn't care.

Asha's door was still open down the hall, the way Isola had left it. She went in and shut it behind her.

The bag stood where she'd put it against the wall, torn pocket turned toward the plaster. She'd folded the strap under it before she walked out, and the strap was still folded. Nobody had touched it. Isola worked two fingers through the tear and felt along the lining until the coin was there, cold and a little too light, and drew it out. She didn't look at it again. She'd already looked at it enough.

She stood there with it closed in her fist and made herself think it through one more time, because she only got to decide this once. If she left it here, it stayed under Omari's roof with every name on that wall standing beside it. If she took it with her, the only person it could tie itself to was the one who'd chosen to pick it up. And if Asha came home and went feeling for it, she'd find the lining empty and know somebody had been in her things.

Isola wanted her to know that.

She slid the coin into the hidden pocket of her skirt, behind the seam where she kept the money, and stood the bag back up so the tear wouldn't show from the doorway. The rest of Asha's bags waited in their row beside it, packed and buckled, the way they'd been waiting for two years. Isola had quit asking what they were for a long time ago. Standing in the middle of the room with High Heresy against her hip, she wished she'd been the kind of cousin who kept asking.

In the kitchen, Selah was packing roots into a jar. The humming had started back up sometime after breakfast, the same six notes, and it hadn't let up long enough for Isola to decide whether that was better or worse.

"I'm going for the salt."

Selah wiped her hands on her apron and drew the order slip out of her pocket. She didn't hand it over right away. She held it a second and looked at Isola the same way she'd looked at her over the dishes, long and level and not asking anything out loud, then laid it in her palm.

"Pell," Selah said. "Not nobody else."

"I know."

"Funeral grade."

"Mama. I know."

Selah went back to the jar. "Then you ain't got nothing to worry about."

Selah didn't repeat herself. When she did, she wasn't talking about the thing she was saying. Isola put the slip in her front pocket, where she'd see it every time she reached for money, and set Micah's phone face down on the counter. Selah looked at the phone, then at her, and moved the jar so it sat right on top of it. It was as good as a lock.

Tevin's door was shut. Isola didn't knock, because knocking would only tell him she was leaving. She checked the front room on her way out instead. His route coat was still hanging off the chair where he'd dropped it. Good.

The token Mother Rae had given her for runs hung on a cord under her shirt. It was a flat brass piece, worn soft at the edges, with marks on its face Rae had never taught her to read. Isola had never asked. Rae answered questions when Rae felt like it, and that wasn't often enough to waste one on a piece of brass that worked fine without being understood. She pulled the cord out in the lane and kept the token in her fist all the way down the cliff stairs, past the shrine at the second landing, where somebody had left a cup of tea for the dead that was already grey with dust, and past the women hauling water up from the vent cisterns. One of them asked after Selah without breaking stride. Isola told her Selah was fine without breaking hers. The stairs cost nothing and saved her the lift fare. She'd pay for the lift coming back if the salt was heavy.

The chasm bridge was loud with people trying to get across before the mist came up. It had come up twice already today, according to the boy selling grounding charms at the head of the span, and the second time, three people had stood frozen in the middle for ten minutes because they couldn't see the rail. Isola could see the rail. She could see the whole bridge, the black stone arching over the vent, the violet glow breathing up out of the dark beneath it, and the Shadowmere guard post at the far end with its lamp and its line.

The line moved slowly. The guard was a big man in a mercenary's coat with half the buttons gone, and he had a route scanner on a chain and a metal plate set into the counter in front of him. The woman ahead of Isola paid at the plate. She pricked her thumb on the needle beside it without being told and pressed it down, and the guard watched the metal drink until it had enough. The boy behind her either didn't have blood to spare or didn't want to give it. He gave a name instead, a crew name Isola recognized from the east gate, and swore to run for them whenever they called. The guard wrote it in his book without looking up.

Isola knew that crew. She watched the boy walk out onto the bridge like he'd paid with something he could earn back, and she hoped somebody at home loved him enough to be mad about it.

She'd bled on that plate herself before Mother Rae gave her the cord.

She stepped up and opened her fist. The guard looked at the token before he passed the scanner over it. It made the short flat sound she'd been waiting for, and he lifted his chin toward Shadowmere without asking where she was going or what she had in her skirt. She kept her free hand on her bag strap the whole time, nowhere near the pocket. She didn't know whether a scanner that read tokens could read anything else. She wasn't about to find out by touching her own hip in front of it.

Isola didn't like Shadowmere.

She'd never had to explain that to anybody at home, because nobody at home liked it either. In Ashvein, a crew wore its colors where you could see them. You knew whose corner you were on before you stood on it, and if you didn't, somebody's auntie would tell you from a window and then tell your mama you'd needed telling. Shadowmere didn't have aunties in windows. It had neon and violet mist and rain falling out of solid rock, and people who smiled at you while they figured out what you were worth. Down here, nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something. Everything was for sale, and most of it was for sale twice.

*Running errands or something*, Selah had said about Asha this morning. Isola had a counterfeit in her skirt that said otherwise. If Asha's errands ran anywhere, they ran through here.

That was why she went to the assayer before she went to Pell. If she was going to walk around Shadowmere looking for her cousin, she needed to know exactly what her cousin had left behind in that bag.

The woman kept a stall at the inside edge of the Back Market, under a stone arch where the rain didn't reach. Isola had stood at that counter twice before, both times holding a cloth bundle of melted shadow-silver for Mother Rae and watching the woman weigh it on a little brass scale with weights no bigger than teeth. She could weigh anything. People brought her scrapings, filings, and coins that had been through fire, and she put on her loupes and told them what it was worth. Isola had never once heard her ask where any of it came from.

She was weighing something now for a man in a dock coat who was arguing with the result. Isola waited until he lost and left, then stepped up, set her hand on the counter palm down, and turned it over.

The coin lay in the middle of her palm. She kept her thumb along the edge so nobody passing could see more than a corner.

The assayer looked at it. Then she put two fingers on the lid of her scale case and shut it. "I don't weigh High Heresy."

The man at the next counter, selling charm wire, stopped talking to his customer, and the customer stopped listening to him.

"Get it off my counter."

Isola closed her hand and took it off the counter.

She walked away without hurrying, because hurrying in the Back Market was the same as asking to be followed. She'd known what the coin was since Asha's room. Knowing it by herself had been one thing. Hearing a woman who never asked questions call it by its name out loud, in front of strangers, was something else, and the words followed her out from under the arch.

High Heresy meant the square. It meant the woman from the mining district on her knees while the Synod took her breath, and her fingers scraping at the stone long after there was any point to it. Isola had been a girl when she watched that, standing with her mother's hand clamped on her shoulder so she couldn't turn away. For one second, coming out into the rain, the fingers on that stone were Asha's. Isola had to stop walking.

She let the rain hit her face until the picture went somewhere else. Then she went looking for Pell, because the salt was the reason she was down here, and it was the only thing on her list today she still knew how to do right.

His stall stood in the lane behind the freight sheds, where the market thinned into thread sellers and funeral goods and the counters took up half the walking room. Pell sat under his heat lamp the way he always did, three braids in his beard resting on his belly, rings on every finger catching the amber light whenever he lifted one to point at somebody's purse. He had a scale nobody else was allowed to touch, a drawer he kept locked, and a mouth that never stopped running, which was usually the only annoying thing about him. Mother Rae bought her funeral grade from him because he kept it dry and didn't cut it. Selah had made that very clear the first time she sent Isola down here alone, and she'd made it clearer when Isola came home and admitted what Pell had charged. Pell had a price for Shadowmere and a price for Ashvein, and he'd never once been ashamed of either.

Usually, by the time Isola got to the counter, he'd asked after Selah, complained about his knees, and tried to sell her something she hadn't come for.

"Rae's girl," Pell said. He was already reaching. "What she need?"

He hadn't asked about Selah. Isola set the slip down. He read it, nodded, and scooped from the open barrel at his elbow into a square of brown paper.

She watched the scoop go in and come out. The salt was grey and coarse, with dark grit caught between the grains, and it clumped where the damp had gotten into it. Isola had been packing salt into Selah's windows since she was small enough to stand on the counter to reach them. She'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name: fine and white and so dry it ran through your fingers like it was trying to get away from you. Pell kept it in the locked drawer under the counter, ground fresh every morning, and every time Isola came for Rae, he'd unlocked that drawer without being asked.

He was folding the brown paper.

"That ain't funeral grade."

Pell didn't look up. "It's what I got today."

"That's quarry salt."

"It's salt."

"It's quarry salt, and it's wet. You had to knock it loose to get it in the scoop." She put her hand flat on the slip. "Rae's slip say funeral grade."

"Rae can come get it herself."

He'd never said anything like that in front of her. Pell talked too much, sure, but he talked the way people did when they wanted to keep you happy, not the way they did when they wanted you gone. Isola looked at his hands. They were folding the paper too carefully, the corners squared up over and over, the way people kept their hands busy so their faces didn't have to do anything. She pulled the cord out from under her shirt and held the token up between them so he'd have to look at it.

"She sent me," Isola said. "Same as last time. Same as the time before that. Unlock the damn drawer, Pell, and quit playing with me today."

Pell stopped folding.

He looked past her. Isola knew better than to turn around. She'd grown up in a house where you learned what was behind you by watching the face in front of you, and Pell's face had just gone blank as a wall.

Two men stepped up to the counter on her left. One stopped at the far end, and the other stood right beside her, close enough that she could smell the rain on his coat. Neither of them said a word to Pell or to her. In Ashvein, she'd have known whose men they were by their sleeves before they ever reached the counter. These two wore plain dark coats and the kind of patience a person only had if they'd never once been told no at a counter.

Pell reached under the counter, farther back than the drawer. He came out with an envelope and set it down. The man beside Isola picked it up and slid it into his coat without looking at it. The man at the end of the counter looked at Isola's hand instead, the one still holding Rae's token out in the open, and he kept looking long enough that she felt it all the way up her arm. She closed her fist over the brass. He followed the first man out of the stall, and the two of them went up the lane toward the bend without hurrying.

Pell didn't unlock the drawer until they'd been gone a good while, and when he finally did, his hands were shaking hard enough that the key took two tries.

Isola dropped the cord back under her shirt while she waited. *Nobody told you who owned the street until you owed it something.* Pell owed it something. She'd just watched him pay.

He took out the morning cut in its grey paper and weighed it out, and when he went to fold the packet, a thin stream of white ran off the edge of the scale and onto the counter. Pell watched it go and didn't say a word.

Isola had never seen him not say anything about anything.

"Who was that?"

"Who?"

She waited.

"Rae account," Pell said. His letters wandered across the line as he logged it in his book. "Eighteen. Tell her I said hey."

Eighteen was robbery, and the both of them knew it. On any other day she'd have stood there and argued him down to fourteen just to watch him sweat. Today she looked at the grit still sitting in the brown paper he'd tried to hand her, and she saw it all the way through. Pell hadn't been cheating Rae. He'd been trying to get Rae's girl gone before those two walked up and found her at his counter with Rae's token around her neck. *Rae can come get it herself* had been the only warning he could afford to give.

She couldn't prove a bit of it. She took the packet anyway, and she didn't argue the eighteen. That was the only thanks Pell was getting.

"You want me to tell her who was here when I came for her salt?"

He didn't answer that. His eyes went up the lane, toward where the men had gone, and then came back down and found something on his scale that needed looking at.

Isola followed the look up the lane.

Asha was standing at the bend.

Isola knew her by how she stood before she could see her face: weight back, head turned just enough to count the turns ahead of her, the way you stood when somebody had told you exactly where to be and you were scared of getting it wrong. Isola had never seen Asha stand like that anywhere she was supposed to be. The coat was dark, fitted close at the waist, falling to her knees. A structured tote hung off one shoulder, stiff-sided and new, the kind of bag that held its shape whether anything was in it or not. Isola had never seen either one in her life.

Coats like that cost money. Bags like that too. She thought about the row of packed bags against Asha's wall, and the coin against her own hip, and she didn't like how easily it all fit.

The bend sat a hair past Gutterlight's back wall, close enough that the crowd noise came through the stone in waves. Asha turned her head toward it. She was about to go.

Isola stepped away from the counter with the packet in her hand.

"Move!"

A boy came down the lane at a dead run, elbows out, a route coat flapping open behind him, and in front of her, a man stepped back from the freight wall to get out of his way and put his whole back straight into her.

He didn't knock her down. She'd been knocked down by bigger, and her feet stayed where they were. But the packet left her hand on the hit, and she watched it go, turning once in the air, and burst against the wet stone at her feet. White salt went everywhere. It hit the puddles and went grey on contact, sinking into the black water like it had never been anything at all.

One bumps. One runs. You find out what's gone when you get home. Every story Isola had ever heard about the Back Market went like that, and her hand was already in her skirt before she'd finished thinking it, fingers closed over the coin, holding it so hard the edge bit.

"Damn, my bad," the boy called over his shoulder, already past the bend.

Isola looked down at the salt. Then she looked up at the man. And up.

He was tall. Not regular tall. She had to tip her head back to get all of him, and she was too mad to be embarrassed about it. Somebody had hit his face recently. There was a swelling high under one eye, a split in his bottom lip, and a bandage wrapped across the knuckles of his right hand, white gone grey at the edges. Rain had soaked his locs and kept coming off them, down past his ear and into his beard, and he didn't wipe it.

"You've got to be fucking kidding me," Isola said, staring at the puddle where the salt had already gone dark. "That's eighteen Veils dissolving in that puddle."

"Eighteen?" The man glanced past her at Pell. His voice was low and unbothered, like he had all the time in the world and hadn't just cost her a whole afternoon. "You robbing folks in daylight now?"

Pell lifted both hands, rings flashing. "Price is the price, Graves. Ashvein orders come with a fee."

*Graves.* Isola filed it. It wasn't the kind of name people got from their mamas.

Behind the man, something shifted against the freight wall. A hooded figure, small, pressed flat to the brick. Isola only got a corner of it before he moved. The hood was down low, but the lamp from the next stall caught one curl that had slipped loose at the edge, and the curl was red. Not dyed red. Not rust. Red, like the inside of a coal.

"Pell," he said, without turning around. "Bag her another one. Put it on Camille."

Pell's eyebrows climbed his whole forehead. "Camille know she paying for this?"

Isola did not know who Camille was. Either Camille was this man's woman and the girl at the wall was something else entirely, or the girl at the wall was Camille, or he was a whole lot more trouble than his face already said he was. None of the possibilities improved her afternoon.

He dug into his coat with his good hand and came out with four Veilmarks. He set them on Pell's counter one at a time, so the quicksilver caught the lamp. "Bag her another one."

Pell looked at the coins. Then he looked at the man's face, long enough to place it, and whatever he found there shifted something under his beard. "Don't got another morning cut."

"Then give her the evening cut early."

Pell sighed like he'd been asked to carry the whole tier up the cliff stairs on his back, and reached beneath the counter.

Four Veilmarks. Pell had put her down for eighteen on Rae's account not five minutes ago, for the same salt, and he'd just taken four off this man without so much as arguing the difference. Isola stared at the coins sitting on the counter and decided she was going to have words with Pell about it someday, and that today wasn't that day, and that she hated it.

She'd crouched to look at the salt without remembering doing it. She stood back up, hand still in her skirt, and looked at him properly this time. She took her time about it. She didn't see why she shouldn't. He'd taken her whole packet; she could take a minute. The swollen eye. The lip. The wrapped hand, which somebody who knew what they were doing had tied, because nobody got a bandage that neat with one hand. He was fine. She noticed that the way you noticed weather, without deciding to, and she didn't appreciate it any more than she appreciated the rain. He had the kind of face that would've been easy to look at even without somebody having already tried to rearrange it, and he was standing there letting her look like he knew exactly what she was doing and didn't mind.

She looked past him again for the red curl.

He shifted half a step left, easy as anything, and put his shoulder right in her line of sight.

Isola knew a block when she saw one. She'd grown up with Tevin. Whatever that was at the wall, he didn't want her looking at it, and he wasn't going to say so.

She went back to his face, and her chin came up. "I ain't ask you to pay for it. I ain't trying to have no debt tied to someone in Shadowmere. Everyone knows how y'all get down."

He almost smiled. She saw it start in the corner of his mouth, saw the split lip stop it, and saw him decide it was worth the pain anyway for about half a second. "I knocked it out your hand. Debt's mine."

He said it the way people said things that were already settled, not like he was offering. Isola had a whole answer ready for a man trying to make her owe him. She didn't have one for a man who'd just taken the owing for himself and left her standing there with nothing to argue.

Pell slapped a fresh packet on the counter, wrapped twice in waxed paper. Isola took it with her free hand. The other one stayed in her skirt. She wasn't letting go of that coin for anybody, and certainly not for a man named after a cemetery.

"Next time," she said, "watch where you backing up."

"Next time, stand somewhere else."

Oh, she had something for that. She had several things for that. She could feel all of them lined up behind her teeth, and she looked at him and decided he hadn't earned a single one, and she could see that he knew it, and that he was enjoying it. That was the worst part.

She tucked the packet under her arm, stepped around the dark patch on the stone, and went up the lane toward the bend.

She didn't look back. She wanted to. She could feel the exact spot between her shoulders where she'd be able to feel him watching if he was watching, and she wasn't going to give him the satisfaction of finding out whether he was. She had a cousin to catch.

The bend was empty.

Two runners went across it with a box between them, arguing about which door they'd been paid to use. Behind them there was nothing but the lane curving away toward the noise, and the noise. Isola stood exactly where Asha had been standing and turned in a slow circle, and nobody in a fitted coat was anywhere.

Ten steps. Maybe fifteen. That was how close she'd been.

She checked the crossing and the doorways on both sides and the mouth of the next alley, where the drainage ran ankle-deep and stank of dye. She walked the length of Gutterlight's back wall and looked into every doorway that would let a body stand out of the rain. She kept at it until a match let out inside the Ring and the roar came through the stone hard enough to hum in her back teeth, and by then the crowd at the bend had doubled, and Asha could have been anywhere in it or nowhere near it.

Isola stood in the rain with the packet under her arm and her hand still in her skirt, and she was so angry she couldn't decide who to aim it at.

Asha first. Asha for the coat and the bag and the coin and the two years of packed luggage. Pell second, for eighteen. The two men in the dark coats third, for whatever they were. Then the boy with his elbows out. Then the man.

*Graves.*

He hadn't known what he'd cost her. She knew that. He'd backed into her getting out of a running boy's way, and he'd paid for the salt without making a show of it, and he'd kept his body between her and whatever was at that wall like it was the most natural thing in the world. None of that made her feel any better about the fact that she'd been fifteen steps from her cousin and she'd spent them looking at a man's face.

It was a good face. That wasn't the point.

She took the lift home. The salt wasn't heavy, but she paid the fare anyway. After the assayer, she wanted walls around her and a cage full of strangers between her and anybody who might've decided to follow a girl with Rae's token and a coin nobody would weigh.

The lift groaned up the shaft with eleven people pressed into a cage built for eight. Somebody's elbow sat in her ribs the whole way. Isola kept the packet flat against her chest and her hand over her skirt, right where the seam bunched around the coin, and the operator called out the levels without looking up from his lever. Halflight. Service Shaft. Ashvein West.

She spent the whole ride irritated by six seconds of him.

Six seconds was about how long she'd stood there looking at him like she had the right, and about how long he'd stood there letting her. She went back over *Debt's mine* and couldn't find anything wrong with it, which was its own problem. She went back over *Next time, stand somewhere else* and found plenty wrong with that, and found herself almost smiling at it, and quit. She thought about the way he'd shifted half a step without anybody asking him to. She thought about the rain going into his beard.

*Graves.* It wasn't a name. It was a warning people had decided to call somebody.

Somewhere past Service Shaft, she caught her thumb working one of her rings around and around her finger. She made herself stop. By Ashvein West, she was doing it again.

By the time the gate opened, the rain on her coat had started drying into dust. Coal smoke hung low over the lane, and she'd missed at least one bell while she was gone. The boys from across the street had disappeared from under their lantern. Isola trusted the empty spot less than she'd trusted the boys standing in it. Somebody had swept the red dust off the Wraithmoor step and left it piled at the edge, and she looked at that little pile longer than a pile deserved.

The lantern above the door still burned amber.

She stood under it a moment longer than she meant to. Then she knocked the dust off her boots against the frame and let herself in. Micah was asleep on the couch with one arm hanging off the cushion and his shoes still on. He'd given up on the day, but not on the shoes. Tevin's coat hadn't moved from the chair, and his door was still shut. She listened at it on her way past and heard him breathing on the other side, slow and even, the way he breathed when he was pretending not to be awake. Good. He could pretend all evening.

Asha's door was open. Isola didn't go in. She could see the bag from the hall, standing exactly where she'd left it. If Asha had come home, she'd come and gone without touching it.

In the kitchen, Selah was at the stove. She turned when Isola came in and held out both hands for the salt before Isola reached the counter. "Pell?"

"Pell."

Selah turned the packet over. "This ain't the morning cut."

"Evening cut." Isola set her bag down. "He tried me with quarry salt first."

"And?"

"And I told him no."

Her mother untied the waxed paper, pinched up some salt, and let it run through her fingers. It ran the way it was supposed to. She looked at the double wrap a while longer than she'd looked at the salt, then nodded once and folded it closed again.

"You find your cousin?"

Selah had told her this morning not to go looking. Now she hadn't asked whether Isola had looked. She'd asked whether she'd found her. Isola reached for a rag that didn't need reaching for. "Saw her. Lost her in the Market."

"Lost her how?"

A coat Isola couldn't have bought with three months of courier fees. A bag to go with it. A coin the assayer wouldn't touch. Two men who never said a word, and Pell, quiet. And a man called Graves who'd backed into her at the worst possible second and then stood there like he'd been glad to.

"Crowd," Isola said.

It was the second time in one day she'd handed one of her parents a word that was only mostly true.

Selah held her stare long enough that Isola wiped the same spot on the counter twice. Then her mother turned back to the stove, and after a moment, the humming started again.

"Wash up," Selah said over it. "Food almost ready."

Isola went to the sink and turned on the water. She held her hands under it and watched the Shadowmere rain go down the drain with the red dust from the stairs, and she prayed that whatever Asha had gotten herself into, it wouldn't end with her back in Shadowmere.


# CHAPTER 5: SILAS

The step was wet by the time the street lamps came on, and so was he.

Silas didn't mind the rain as much as he minded the waiting. Rain at least did something. It ran off the awning in a steady line about a foot in front of his boots, and it beaded on the bandage across his knuckles, and it found the gap at the back of his collar every time he turned his head to check the far end of the street. The waiting didn't do anything but give his shoulder time to set, and by the time Gutterlight started calling the first match of the night through the stone, the joint had gone stiff enough that he'd quit trying to roll it.

Nobody came down the street who shouldn't have. A man with a cart of empty bottles went by, then two women arguing about whose turn it was to pay for something, then a girl about eleven carrying a covered dish with both hands like it was worth more than she was. Each one of them looked at the dark house and then at the man sitting on its step, and each one of them looked away again without slowing down. A man sitting outside a house with no lantern meant somebody was inside it who wasn't supposed to be, and in Shadowmere, the smartest thing you could do with that information was not have it.

His phone buzzed once against his hip.

Remy: You home?

Silas: No.

Remy: Dante?

Silas looked at the door behind him for a while before he answered.

Silas: Working.

The dots came up and went away. Remy had known him long enough to hear everything that one word was leaving out, and long enough not to push on it through a phone.

Remy: Ledger names in the morning. Eat something.

Silas: Yes, Daddy.

Remy sent back the same two words he'd sent last night. Silas put the phone away.

He thought about the fifteen-year-old. Somewhere behind a Dawnveil stamp and a line Jerrick had sold, a boy was either still breathing or he wasn't, and Jude was the one with the token, and Remy was the one with the ledger, and Silas was the one sitting on a wet step babysitting a stranger. He didn't hold it against her. She'd had a worse day than he had, and he'd been raised better than to blame somebody for being the job. But he knew what he'd rather be doing with his night.

He thought, for longer than he meant to, about a woman with two Ironcross rings and her hand shoved in her skirt like she had a knife in there. Maybe she did. She'd looked him over like she was pricing him, and he hadn't minded nearly as much as he should have.

Then he smelled the thread burning.

It wasn't smoke, not yet. Funeral thread didn't burn like cloth. It went bitter first, a sharp, mineral stink like a struck match dropped in salt, and Silas had grown up in rooms where that smell meant one of Camille's lines had just been asked to hold something it wasn't built for. He was on his feet before he'd decided to stand, and his shoulder charged him for every inch of it.

The front door was still sealed. He pressed the brass token into the plate and felt Camille's ward check him and let him through, and the smell hit him full in the face as the door opened.

The bedroom door hung half off its frame. The lock had gone soft and dripped down the wood in a single dark run, like candle wax, and the brass was still glowing at the edges where it had cooled. Across the front room, the window stood open to the rain. Camille's double line of thread had burned black along the sill, both strands, and the rain blowing in through the gap was hissing where it landed on what was left.

The room was empty. So was the bedroom. The folded blanket was still folded on the cot, and the crackers he'd seen her pocket in Dante's office were sitting in a neat little stack beside it.

She'd planned it. Not well, but she'd planned it.

Silas went out the window because it was faster than the door. He came down on the wet stones of the side lane with his bad shoulder against the wall and found the lane empty in both directions. Then he found her the way he'd have found anybody in Shadowmere who didn't know Shadowmere: by watching where people were turning their heads. At the far end of the lane, where it opened onto the row of shuttered shops, a man with a basket had stopped walking to look at something. Two kids on a stoop had quit their game. All of them were looking toward the light.

People who didn't know the tier always went toward the light. It was the first thing that got them.

The row ran straight back toward the tram stop and the lit market beyond it, and she was halfway down it in a mourning dress with the hood of Dante's coat fallen back and her hair coming loose, red as a struck flare under the lamps, and every person she passed was going to remember her for the rest of their lives.

Silas didn't follow her down the row. He cut through the gap between the pawn counter and the bakery behind it, a space so narrow he had to turn his shoulders sideways and grit his teeth to get through, and came out ahead of her where the row bent past a freight door. She saw him and swung hard into the nearest opening.

It was the wrong alley. He'd known it would be. It ran forty feet and stopped at a wall of wet stone with a drain grate at the bottom, and by the time she reached the end and turned around, he was standing at its mouth with his good hand open at his side.

"Don't," she said.

Heat came off her in waves he could see. It bent the rain before the rain could reach her. Her hands were black past the second knuckle, and she was holding them out from her body like they belonged to somebody she didn't trust.

"I'm not coming any closer."

"I am not going back in there."

"Then where you going?"

"To find Maven."

"Who?"

Her mouth closed. Then, quieter, like she'd caught herself saying something in the wrong room: "Mother Rae."

Silas filed *Maven* in the back of his head next to the stones on her collar and the way she said *I am.* He didn't have anywhere useful to put it yet. "You know where Rae is?"

She opened her mouth and nothing came out of it. She didn't know. She'd burned through a lock and a ward and a window and run out into a tier she couldn't have named three streets of, and she had nowhere in Shadowmere to run to. He watched her understand that. It made her angrier. The heat climbed.

Then the sound went out.

The rain didn't stop. He could see it falling, streaking past the lamp at the mouth of the alley and breaking on the stones at his feet, but the hiss of it was gone all at once, like somebody had shut a door on it. Gutterlight's roar went with it. The drip off the gutters went. When Silas breathed, he couldn't hear that either.

He knew Silence the way he knew his mother's thresholds, by the shape it left behind.

Out on the row, people had frozen. The man with the basket stood with one foot lifted. A woman in a doorway had her mouth open on a word that wasn't going to come out. Past them, three figures came down the middle of the street with no sound to them at all, veiled from crown to collar in grey that drank the lamplight, and where their faces should have been, pale marks showed through the cloth in the shape of bone. Nobody looked at them. Everybody on that row had learned a long time ago what happened to people who could say afterward that they'd seen something.

Behind him, Melena's hands were giving off light at the knuckles like coals somebody had leaned down and blown on. Her hair was loose. If one of those veils turned toward this alley, there wasn't a shadow anywhere in Shadowmere deep enough to hide her in.

There was no time to ask. There wasn't even sound to ask with.

Silas crossed the alley in four steps, put her back to the wall, and opened his coat around her. He pressed her into the stone with his body between her and the street, pulled the coat closed over her hair, and laid his right hand flat over both of hers to smother the light.

It was like closing his palm on a stove.

He didn't move it. She went rigid against him, every inch of her furious, and he felt her try to shove him off and find nothing to push against but stone. Her heat came through his shirt and up into the bones of his hand. He kept his hand where it was and turned his head so his face was the only part of them the street could see, just another man in a dark coat leaning on a girl in an alley, which was nothing anybody on that row would look at twice.

The three veils came even with the alley mouth. One of them slowed.

Silas didn't breathe. He couldn't have heard himself if he had. The veiled head turned a quarter of the way toward the dark where they stood and held there for a count he felt in his teeth. Under his palm, Melena's hands had started to shake. He pressed down harder, and she let him, and that was somehow worse than the fighting.

The veil turned back to the street, and the three of them went on.

Sound came back slowly, the way feeling came back into a foot you'd been sitting on. First the rain. Then the gutters. Then, from very far off, Gutterlight, finishing whatever had happened in the Ring while the whole street held its breath. Then his own breathing, ragged and loud and much closer than he'd wanted it to be.

He let go of her hands and stepped back.

His palm was blistered across the middle, raw and shining where the skin had given up. He didn't look at it long. Melena came off the wall like it had thrown her and put three feet of alley between them. Her hands had gone dark again. She was shaking, and he didn't think all of it was the cold.

He figured he owed her the truth more than he owed her an apology, so he gave her that.

"I touched you without asking," Silas said. "I'd do it again."

She looked at his hand. Then at his face. Whatever she found there didn't soften anything about her own.

"Then don't ask me to thank you."

He didn't. He jerked his chin toward the mouth of the alley, and after a long moment she went, because she'd seen the same three veils he had, and whatever else she was, she wasn't stupid. He walked her back down the row with his burned hand held away from his coat and his eyes on every doorway. The man with the basket had gone. The kids on the stoop had gone inside. The whole row had decided, collectively and without discussing it, that it had seen nothing tonight, and Silas could have kissed every one of them for it.

She went in through the front door this time. He came in behind her, locked it, and pressed the token back into the plate, and the ward settled along the frame like a held breath being let go.

Then he stood in the front room and looked at the window.

Camille was going to kill him.

---

His mother came through her own seal about an hour later.

He heard her before he saw her. Camille's thresholds knew her the way a dog knew its owner's step on the stairs, and the funeral thread along the doorframe made a low, satisfied sound as she pressed her palm to it, one Silas had never once heard it make for him. The lock turned without the token. She came in shaking rain off a dark wrap, a covered pot in the crook of one arm and a newspaper folded under the other, glasses already pushed up on top of her head.

She stopped two steps in.

She looked at the window, and the black line under it, and the bedroom door hanging off its hinges with the lock run down the wood. She looked at Melena, who was sitting at the little table exactly where Silas had put her, hands flat on the wood in front of her, not touching anything. Then she looked at Silas, standing by the stove with his right hand held palm-up and away from his body like it had insulted him.

"Mmm," Camille said.

That was all. She set the pot on the cold stove and the newspaper on the counter, and she took her wrap off and hung it on the hook by the door, and she rolled her sleeves up past her elbows one at a time, and through all of it she didn't say another word. Silas had been her son for twenty-some years. He knew exactly how much trouble that silence could hold, and he'd never seen it this full.

"Let me see it."

He gave her the hand. She turned it toward the lamp and studied the blister, and her mouth went flat.

"How long you hold it?"

"Long as I had to."

"That ain't a number."

"Three Shrouds' worth."

Her eyes came up to his. Then they went to Melena and stayed there. Camille didn't ask what Shrouds. She didn't ask whose. She took the medical tin out of his coat pocket herself, because she'd watched him put it there in a hundred other rooms, and she opened it without looking down.

"Sit."

He sat. She cleaned the burn with something that stung like a second fire and then laid a cool salve over it that smelled like crushed mint and cellar dirt, and she wrapped it tight, palm and wrist, so his whole right hand turned into a white mitt with the old bandage still showing across the knuckles underneath like a bad decision nobody had taken back. When she was finished with the hand, she put two fingers on his left shoulder, right where it lived, and pressed.

He hissed through his teeth.

"Mmm-hmm," Camille said. "When was you planning on telling me about that one?"

"Wasn't."

"I know you wasn't." She pressed again, gentler. "Dante say anything?"

"Said let you look at it."

"Well. That's the first sensible thing that man's said all week." She took her hand back. "Soak it again before you sleep. Hot as you can stand. And don't lift nothing heavier than a spoon."

"I'm on a door all night."

"Then sit on it with your arm down." She turned around and looked at Melena again, longer this time, and Silas watched his mother's face do something it rarely did, which was go completely still.

He knew that look. It was the look she gave the crossword when she already knew the word and was deciding whether to let him find it. Whatever Camille saw sitting at that table, she saw more of it than he did, and she wasn't going to tell him.

"You eat?" Camille asked her.

Melena blinked. Nobody had asked her a question in this house that didn't have to do with burning something. "I had crackers."

"That ain't food." Camille took the lid off the pot. Steam came up, and the room filled with the smell of last night's stew, potatoes and mushrooms and whatever meat she was never going to admit to buying. "Bowls in that cabinet. Silas."

He got up and got the bowls. One-handed, it took two trips. Camille ladled out a bowl and set it in front of Melena with a spoon laid across the top, and then she sat down across the table from her, and she didn't say eat, and she didn't say anything else. She just picked up her newspaper, unfolded it to the crossword, and took the pencil from behind her ear.

Melena looked at the stew. Then at Camille, who was counting boxes. Then, slowly, she picked up the spoon.

Silas stood by the stove and watched it happen and didn't say anything, because there wasn't anything to say. His mother had gotten more food into that girl in two minutes than Dante had gotten in an hour, and she'd done it by refusing to care whether it worked.

"Seven letters," Camille said, to nobody. "'Kept against one's will.'"

Silas looked at the ceiling.

"Captive," Melena said quietly, around the spoon.

Camille counted the boxes. "Could be."

Melena put her spoon down.

"Eat," Camille said, without looking up, and Melena picked it back up.

---

When the bowl was empty, Camille sent Melena to wash her face. As soon as the bathroom door shut, she folded the newspaper closed and got up and went to the window, and she crouched down beside the burned line without touching it and looked at it the way she looked at stitching somebody else had done badly.

"Both strands," she said.

"Yeah."

"She went through both strands." Camille ran a fingertip a half inch above the black, not touching. "Then the bedroom lock. Then the front seal held, so she went out this way instead." She sat back on her heels. "You know what I put under that window?"

"Funeral thread."

"Double funeral thread. Over a grave nail. I set that line when Jude was nine years old, and I've had grown men try it with blades and crowbars and blood that wasn't theirs, and not one of them has ever done more than make it hum." She looked at him over her shoulder. "That girl walked through it."

Silas didn't have anything for that.

Camille got up, slowly, one hand on the sill, and came back across the room to him. She dropped her voice, though the water was still running in the bathroom. "Gutterlight ward won't hold a week with that girl in it."

"Dante thinks it will."

"Dante ain't built it." She looked at the bathroom door, and something crossed her face and went away again. "You hear me. A week. Less, if she has another day like today. I'll restitch the window tonight, and it'll hold her tonight, but she's gon' cook every line in this house one after another, and I'm not about to stand here sewing behind her like a seamstress chasing a burning hem."

"So what you want me to do?"

"I want you to tell your father what I said." Camille picked her pencil up off the table and stuck it back behind her ear. "And then I want you to listen when he don't believe it. Because he won't. He never does the first time."

She restitched the window. It took her the better part of an hour, crouched on the floor with her spools and her small, curved needle and a grave nail she drew out of her own wrap, and she wouldn't let him help, and she hummed while she did it, which she only ever did when she was angry enough that talking would've been a mistake. Melena came out of the bathroom halfway through and stood by the table watching her work. Camille didn't send her away. When it was done, Camille set her palm flat against the new line, and it gave that same low sound the door had given her, and she nodded once like the thread had told her something she was willing to accept.

She picked up her wrap. She picked up her empty pot. At the door, she turned around.

"First shift is yours," she told Silas. "Don't sit outside. Sit in front of the door. Inside." She looked at Melena. "Anybody wants to go through it, they gon' have to go through him first. And he heals slow, so let's don't." Then she looked at Silas again, at the white mitt of his hand and the shoulder he still hadn't lifted. "I'll be back in the morning. I expect to find everybody in this house still in it."

"Yes ma'am."

She left. The thread along the frame made its sound for her, and then the lock turned, and the house was quiet.

---

Silas dragged a chair over to the front door and then decided against the chair. He sat on the floor instead with his back against the wood, legs out in front of him, his bad arm down the way Camille had told him and his wrapped hand resting on his thigh, throbbing in time with his pulse. From there, he could see the whole front room, the new line of thread under the window, and the broken bedroom door.

Melena didn't go back to the bedroom. He hadn't expected her to. She sat at the table instead, under the one lamp, and after a while she reached into Dante's coat where it hung over the chair beside her and took out the envelope.

He watched her decide to open it. It took a long time. She turned the envelope over twice and ran her thumb along the fold where the dark red seal had broken, and then she sat with it in both hands, not moving, long enough that he thought she'd changed her mind. Then she slid two fingers inside and drew out the letter and unfolded it on the table under the lamp.

He wasn't trying to read it. He was sitting on the floor across the room with his head against a door, and the lamp was behind the paper, and the light came through it the way light comes through anything thin enough. The writing was a sloping, careful hand in dark ink, small and close. He couldn't have made out a single line of it if he'd tried.

But the first word was by itself, set above the rest on a line of its own, the way you'd set the name of the person you were writing to. It was short, and the light came through it clean.

*Elias.*

Melena's hands closed around the edges of the page.

She didn't cry. He'd half expected her to and was glad she didn't, because he wouldn't have known what to do with it from the floor. She just sat there and read, or looked at the paper long enough that it amounted to the same thing, and her face did something he didn't have a name for. It wasn't grief exactly. It was what happened to somebody's face when they'd carried a thing a long way, through seven tiers and a veil and a fire, and opened it, and found it addressed to somebody else.

Silas didn't know an Elias. He went through everybody he'd ever moved, everybody who'd ever come through Dante's office, everybody whose name had crossed Lorraine's ledger while he was standing at her desk. He came up empty. He had a feeling, watching Melena's face across the room, that nobody in Shadowmere had ever heard that name, and that she'd rather he died on that floor than asked her about it.

So he didn't ask. He sat with his back against the door and his hand on fire under Camille's wrap, and he watched her read a letter that wasn't hers, and he kept the name where he'd kept *Maven*, in the back of his head, next to the stones on her collar and the way she'd walked straight through his mother's thread like it was nothing at all.

Fire belonged to Remnia. Everybody knew that much, the way everybody knew the sky was somewhere up past Crownlight. Nobody born under the stone carried it. He'd known since Dante's office that she'd come down from farther than any tier he had a name for.

He just hadn't known, until tonight, that she'd come all that way carrying somebody else's mail.


# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Selah set a plate for Asha anyway.

She did it the way she did everything that mattered, without discussion. Five plates went down on the table, and then a sixth, at the end where Asha always sat with her back to the wall so she could see the door, and Selah spooned rice and greens and two pieces of the fish onto it and turned a bowl upside down over the top to keep it warm. Then she sat down in her own chair and folded her hands and waited for everybody else to get theirs together.

Nobody said anything about the covered plate. That was its own conversation.

Tevin came out of the spare room for dinner because Selah's rule about the table had outlived every fight anybody in this house had ever had, and he wasn't about to be the one to break it over an east-end boy with a big mouth. He sat down across from Isola without looking at her and pulled his plate closer like somebody might take it. His locs were still neat in the front. He'd retwisted them for nothing, and he knew she knew it, and that was most of why he wouldn't look at her.

Omari said the blessing short, the way he always did when the food was hot. Then the forks started.

"Danika ain't gon' write back tonight," Micah said, to nobody, around a mouthful of rice. "Is she."

"Courier ain't even reached Ironcross yet," Isola said.

"I know. I'm just saying."

"You just saying it every ten minutes."

"Because I'm just *thinking* it every ten minutes."

Selah reached over and moved the pepper sauce out of Micah's reach without looking at him, because he'd put too much on already and was about to put more. He watched it go with real grief.

"She'll write when she writes," Omari said. "Packet's good. You did your thirty."

"Twenty-nine and a morning," Tevin said into his plate.

Micah pointed his fork at him. "You don't get to talk. You almost got me through the door this morning."

"Ain't nobody tell you to put your shoes on."

"Ain't nobody tell you to call him out in front of the whole east gate."

"He talked about Dani."

"Jude said those east-end boys don't even fight," Micah said. "Said they just stand around and talk until somebody's uncle comes and gets them."

The table went still in the specific way tables did when somebody had said a name they shouldn't have. Isola put her fork down.

"Jude who?"

Micah's mouth stayed open for a second with nothing coming out of it. Then he put a piece of fish in it, which bought him almost nothing.

"Mercer," Omari said.

He said it mildly, the way he said most things. He didn't even look up from his plate. But Isola watched Micah's shoulders come up around his ears, and she knew that her father had known exactly who Jude was and exactly how much Micah talked to him before Micah had ever opened his mouth tonight. Omari usually did. He just waited for you to tell him.

"He my godbrother," Micah said, once he'd swallowed. "I'm allowed to talk to him."

"Talk to him where?" Isola asked.

"On the phone." He looked at her. "That you took."

Tevin snorted into his rice.

Dante Mercer had stood for Micah and for Asha both, back when they were babies, and Isola had grown up knowing that the way she knew which stair on the cliff route was loose. If either of them ever called that man's name, everybody in his house moved. It was the kind of promise you made in front of witnesses and the dead, and it outranked blood down in Shadowmere, and up here it meant Micah had a whole second family two tiers below them that Isola had never once met. She'd always figured that was a phone call on holidays. She hadn't figured on a godbrother telling her baby brother what east-end boys did or didn't do in a fight.

"Jude Mercer been taking you down to Gutterlight," she said. It wasn't a question.

Micah looked at Omari. Omari ate a forkful of greens.

"Not in thirty days," Micah said.

"Micah."

"Not in thirty *days*. I swear on the wall."

Isola believed him, which made it worse, because it meant there'd been a thirty-first day and a thirty-second and who knew how many before the packet, when she'd been at this same table every night counting work chits and telling Danika in her letters that Micah was doing better. She looked at her father.

Omari set his fork down and wiped his mouth. "Thirty-one days from now," he said, "it's still a no."

That was all. Micah said "Yes sir" so fast it came out as one word, and Selah slid the pepper sauce back within his reach as a kindness, and dinner went on. But Isola sat there with her fork and her fish and her brother across the table, and she thought about how much of this house went on without her knowing. Asha's bags. Micah's godbrother. Tevin's three stories about one scar. She ran this family. She counted its money and wrote its letters and chased its people. And she was starting to think everybody in it had been walking around her this whole time like she was a piece of furniture they'd all agreed not to trip over.

Nobody touched the covered plate.

---

Micah washed. Isola dried. That had been the arrangement since he was tall enough to reach the sink, and neither of them had ever thought to renegotiate it.

He was quiet for a while, which was unusual enough that she let it go on. The water ran. The plates came out of it one at a time and went into her towel. Through the kitchen vent, the night shift bell rang somewhere down the block, and the grounding wires in the walls answered it with a hum so low she felt it in her teeth more than heard it.

"I'm not mad at you," she said finally.

"You mad at me."

"I'm a little mad at you."

"You was a lot mad at me at dinner."

"I'm a lot mad at Jude Mercer. You just sitting closest."

He grinned at the dishwater. Then he went quiet again, and she let that go on too, and then she asked the question she'd been carrying around since the lift.

"You know somebody down there they call Graves?"

Micah's hands stopped in the water.

He turned his head and looked at her, slow, the way he looked at her when she'd said something he wanted to be very sure he'd heard right. "Where you hear that name?"

"Pell said it."

"*Pell* said it?"

"He was at Pell's." She took the plate out of his hands, since he'd forgotten he was holding it. "Some man backed into me. Knocked Rae's salt in a puddle. Pell called him Graves."

"What he look like?"

Isola dried the plate. She was going to say tall. That was all she'd meant to say. "Tall. Taller than you. Face all busted up on one side like somebody caught him good, lip split down the middle. His hand was wrapped. Somebody else did it, too, he couldn't have done it that neat with one hand. And he had all this rain coming off his locs, and he wasn't even bothering to wipe it, he just let it run right down into his—"

She stopped.

Micah's grin had started somewhere around *taller than you* and had been growing steadily the whole time, and now it took up most of his face. He didn't say anything. He just stood there with his hands in the dishwater and let her hear herself.

"Into his collar," Isola said.

"Uh-huh."

"I was gon' say collar."

"You was not gon' say collar."

"Micah."

"That's Jude's brother." He was laughing now, quietly, the way you laughed when somebody in the next room might hear you and ask what was so funny. "That's *Silas*. Graves is what they call him in the Ring. Jude's big brother. You met Silas Graves at a *salt stall*, and you looked at him long enough to know who wrapped his hand."

"I looked at him long enough to know he owed me salt."

"He paid you?"

"He paid Pell."

"Silas Graves bought you salt." Micah put both wet hands flat on the edge of the sink and leaned on them like he needed the support. "Oh, I gotta tell Jude."

"You ain't telling Jude nothing. You ain't got a phone."

That sobered him for about a second. Then it didn't. "I'll get it back."

"Not this week, you won't."

He went back to washing, still grinning, and handed her a cup, and she dried it harder than a cup needed drying. It was ridiculous. She knew it was ridiculous. A man had knocked her salt into a puddle and paid for it, and she had a cousin who hadn't come home and a coin in her skirt that could put a woman on her knees in the square, and here she was in the kitchen getting laughed at by her baby brother over a man's beard.

"So Jude's brother," she said, because if Micah was going to enjoy this, she might as well get something out of it. "What's he do? Besides fight."

Micah's smile changed a little. Not gone, just thinner at the edges.

"Mostly fight," he said. "For Dante, sometimes. Moving people. Jude says he does things for their mama, too, but Jude don't talk about that, so I don't ask." He rinsed a pot. "He fought last night."

"Okay."

"He killed a man last night."

Isola stopped drying.

Micah didn't look at her. He scrubbed at the bottom of the pot like it had personally done something to him. "That's what Jude said. Some Lowtorch man who sold out some runners. Jude said Silas had been waiting on that match for a week. Said the whole Ring knew it was gon' end that way before the gate even opened, and Silas didn't even hurry." He set the pot on the rack. "Jude said it like it was regular. I guess down there it kind of is."

She thought about the swelling under the man's eye, and the split lip, and the hand. Somebody hit him recently. She'd thought it at the counter like it was a joke, a little bit of satisfaction that somebody had gotten to him before she could. She hadn't thought about what had happened to the somebody.

She thought about him shifting half a step left without being asked, putting his shoulder between her and whatever was at that wall. Easy. Like he'd done it a thousand times. Like keeping things out of other people's line of sight was just something his body did.

"Runners," she said. "What runners?"

"Kids. Some kids on a route. Jude didn't say much." Micah pulled the plug, and the water started to go. "Three of 'em went missing on a line the man sold."

Isola looked at the towel in her hands.

*Debt's mine,* he'd said, like it was settled. Like debts were the kind of thing he settled.

"Don't tell Mama I asked about him," she said.

"I would never." Micah dried his hands on the front of his shirt instead of the towel, because he was Micah. Then, at the door, he turned back around with that grin creeping up again. "You want me to find out if he's seeing anybody?"

She threw the towel at him. He caught it and was gone down the hall, laughing, before she could find anything heavier.

Now she had his name. That was something. She had his name and his brother's name and what he did on Ring nights and what he'd done last night, and he had nothing of hers at all. Not her name, not her block, not one thing but her face and her mouth telling him to watch where he was backing up. Isola didn't mind that. She stood alone in the kitchen with the water gurgling down the drain and found that she didn't mind it even a little.

---

Asha came home well after the last bell, when the house had gone dark and quiet and even Selah's humming had stopped.

Isola heard her before she saw her. The front door had a way of sticking in wet weather, and Asha had a way of lifting it on its hinges to keep it from scraping, and the soft double click of the latch going and coming back was a sound Isola had been hearing in the middle of the night for two years without ever once getting up to see what it meant. Tonight she was already up. She was sitting in the dark on the end of the couch where Micah had been sleeping that afternoon, with her legs pulled up under her skirt, and she didn't move.

Asha came in sideways and shut the door the same way. She bent and unlaced her boots by feel and stepped out of them, leaving them on the mat. In the thin amber light coming through the security mesh from the lantern outside, she was mostly shape, but Isola knew the shape. The fitted coat. The stiff, structured tote on her shoulder. She carried it like it weighed more than it looked.

Asha straightened up and turned toward the hall, and stopped.

"You trying to scare me to death?"

"If I was trying, you'd know."

Asha laughed, low. She had a good laugh, Asha, warm and a little husky, the kind that made people want to say something else funny just to hear it again. It had gotten her out of more trouble than any of the rest of them combined. "It's late, Isola."

"I know what time it is."

"Then go to bed."

"Where you been?"

"Out." Asha shifted the tote higher on her shoulder. As she did, the light caught her wrist, and Isola saw two things at once. A thin cord around it with a small dark charm hanging off it, the kind of charm she'd seen dangling from the wrists of people coming up out of Shadowmere's dens with their eyes too soft. And on the cuff of the fitted coat, just above the charm, a smear that caught the lamplight and held it, silver, liquid, shifting when Asha moved.

Quicksilver.

Isola didn't say anything about either one. She didn't trust her voice yet.

"I'm going to wash up," Asha said, into the silence, and went down the hall, and a minute later the water started running in the washroom at the back of the house.

Isola sat for a moment longer. Then she got up, and she didn't go to the boots.

She went to the wall.

The name chains looked different in the dark. In daylight they were just there, the way the stove was there, four generations of Wraithmoors in thread and ink and pressed metal running up the wall beside the spare-room door. At night, with the lantern light coming through the mesh in thin crooked lines, they looked like something a person had built to hold up the house. Isola found Asha's section by feel, two strips to the left of Micah's, below Tevin's. Her fingers knew it. She'd straightened that strip a hundred times when it went crooked from somebody slamming a door.

She laid two fingers against the metal.

Asha Noelle Wraithmoor.

She said it the way Omari had taught them all, low and even, each part of the name set down carefully before the next one came, the rhythm her father used for the wall and nowhere else. The words weren't anything. Saying them right was everything. When she'd done this for Micah this morning, the thread had pulled taut under her fingers and a warmth had come up through the metal, and for one breath something on the other side of the wall had pressed back against her palm like a hand.

Tonight, nothing pressed back.

The metal stayed cold. The funeral thread wrapped through Asha's name didn't tighten. It went the other way. Isola felt it go under her fingertips, a soft, terrible give, like a held rope somebody had let go of at the other end. It didn't break. It just went slack and stayed slack and lay there against the strip doing nothing at all.

Isola took her hand away and stood very still.

A Wraithmoor name didn't fail because somebody was in trouble. It didn't fail for a lie, or a bad night, or a coin in a bag. Their names had held through raids and sickness and Synod paper, through Omari's grandfather going to his grave and every funeral this family had ever stood at since, through every bad thing they'd ever walked into. A Wraithmoor name only went slack one way, and every child in this house had been taught it before they were taught to tie their shoes.

It went slack when the person was leaving it.

The water was still running in the washroom. Isola looked down the hall at the line of light under the door. Then she looked at the boots on the mat, wet at the toes and dusty red at the heels, and this time she didn't make herself think it through. She'd done all the thinking she had in her.

She picked up the left boot and turned it over. Nothing. She slid her hand inside, down past the ankle to the toe, and found the insole tacked down at the heel and loose at the front. She worked her fingers under it.

The route strip was folded in quarters and still warm from Asha's foot.

Isola took it to the window and unfolded it in the crooked amber light. It was a narrow strip of stiff black paper, the kind that didn't tear and didn't take water, printed down one edge with route marks she didn't recognize. Down the middle, in white, someone had punched six neat holes in a column, and next to each hole was a small stamped circle, blue. Six deliveries. At the bottom, in the same white ink, a number: 180 Veils. And beneath that, in letters so small she had to tip the paper toward the light to read them:

*Name clears when balance closes.*

Across the back, in somebody's handwriting, hurried, the ink smeared at one end: *First bell. Blue Circle.*

The water stopped.

Isola didn't put it back. She stood at the window holding it, and when the washroom door opened and Asha came down the hall in her sleep shirt with her face wet and her hair wrapped, Isola was the first thing she saw.

Asha looked at the strip in Isola's hand. Then at the boot lying on its side by the door. Then back at Isola.

She didn't run. She didn't grab for it. She didn't do anything Isola had braced for. She just stood in the hall with water drying on her face, and for a long moment she looked so tired that Isola almost said her name the old way, the way she'd said it when they were girls sharing a bed and Asha was scared of the storm flashes.

"You been in my bag," Asha said instead.

"You been in High Heresy."

Asha's face didn't change. But her hand went to the cord on her wrist, and her fingers closed over the dark charm, and held it.

"What's Blue Circle?" Isola asked.

"A place."

"What kind of place?"

"The kind you don't need to know about."

"I already know about it. I'm holding it." Isola lifted the strip. "Six deliveries. A hundred and eighty Veils. Who you owe a hundred and eighty Veils, Asha?"

"Nobody, by the time I'm done."

"And what's this?" She turned the strip and put her thumb under the small white letters. "Name clears. Whose name? Yours? What's wrong with your name?"

Asha laughed again, and this time it wasn't warm. "You would ask that."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means go to bed, Isola."

"I put my hand on the wall."

That stopped her.

Asha didn't move. She didn't even blink. But something went out of her shoulders, the way something had gone out of the thread, and Isola watched her cousin understand exactly what she'd done and exactly what she'd felt, and decide not to say anything about it at all.

"It went slack," Isola said. Her voice wasn't doing what she wanted it to. "I said your name, and it went *slack*, Asha. You know what that means. You know what that means better than me. You been packing your bags against that wall for two *years*—"

"Keep your voice down."

"—and I let you. I let you, because I figured you'd tell me what for when you was ready, and instead I'm finding Shadowmere coins in your lining and your name coming loose off the wall in my hand—"

"Isola." Asha crossed the hall in three quick steps and put her hand over Isola's, over the strip, and pressed. Her palm was still damp from the washroom. Her eyes were very dark and very steady and much too close. "Don't tell them."

"Asha."

"Don't tell them." Her fingers tightened. "Not Mama. Not Daddy. Not Tevin, Lord, not Tevin. Not anybody. I'm almost done. Six. That's all it is. I'm almost done."

"Done with *what*?"

Asha took the strip.

She didn't snatch it. She slid it out from under Isola's hand like she was taking a dish Isola had finished drying, and folded it once, twice, three times, and held it against her chest.

"I just told you," she said. "You read it."

Then she picked up her boots from the mat, one in each hand, and went back down the hall to her room, and the door closed behind her with a sound so soft it was almost polite.

Isola stood in the front room for a long time.

She looked at the wall. She didn't touch it again. She didn't think she could stand to feel it go a second time, and she knew it would. Then she went to her own room and shut her own door and sat down on the edge of her bed in the dark with her hands between her knees, and she made herself breathe until her chest quit doing what it had been doing since the hall.

First bell. Blue Circle. She had a place and a time. She didn't have a reason, or a single idea what *name clears* meant, or any way to stop her cousin from walking out of this house before morning. But she had a place and a time, and she'd built a lot of days on less.

She hadn't promised. She went back over it to be sure, word by word, the way you went back over a contract. Asha had said *don't tell them* twice, and Isola had said *Asha* twice, and neither one of those was a yes.

She lay back on the bed without taking her clothes off. The coin was still in her skirt, and she put her hand over it, and lay there in the dark with her eyes open.

She tried to think about first bell. She tried to think about the route down to the bridge and how early she'd have to be up to get there before Asha did, and whether Tevin would hear her go, and what she'd say to Selah if Selah was already in the kitchen. She tried to think about Danika's letter, and Micah's phone, and the hundred and eighty Veils. She tried very hard.

She kept thinking about the water sliding into his beard.

She'd seen it at the counter and not thought anything about it. He'd been standing in the rain off Pell's awning, and the rain had been coming off his locs and running down past his ear and into his beard in a slow line, and he'd let it, like he couldn't be bothered, like getting wet was something that happened to other people. She'd thought, at the time, *wipe your face*. Now she was lying in the dark thinking about it on purpose, which was a different thing entirely.

She stopped thinking about it.

Mostly.

"Silas," she said, out loud, quietly, to the ceiling, just to hear what it sounded like.

It sounded fine. It sounded like it fit in her mouth a little too well, like a word she'd been meaning to say for a while. Isola lay very still and listened to the house, and to the last of it fading, and she did not like how much she liked it.

She turned over, put her face in the pillow, and decided that if she had to be up before first bell, she was going to be up before first bell mad, and that was going to have to be enough.


# CHAPTER 7: SILAS

Silas woke up on the floor with his back against the door and no memory of deciding to sleep.

His neck had locked at an angle God had not intended. His shoulder had set overnight into something closer to stone than muscle, and his right hand, inside Camille's wrap, had moved past burning into a deep, steady throb that kept time with his pulse. He sat still for a moment and took inventory the way he would've after a fight. Stomach steady. Hands, one and a half. Nothing broken that hadn't already been broken yesterday.

The house was quiet. The lamp had burned down to almost nothing. Across the room, the new line of thread under the window still held, dark and whole, and the bedroom door still hung crooked off its one good hinge.

Melena was asleep at the table.

She'd folded her arms on the wood and put her head down on them, and at some point in the night she'd pulled Dante's coat off the chair and dragged it over her shoulders like a blanket. The letter was under her hand. Even asleep, she had her fingers spread over it. Her hair had come all the way loose and lay across her arms and the table in a heavy red sheet, and in the low light, it was the only color in the room.

Remy hadn't answered. Remy hadn't needed to.

He knocked at first bell.

Silas knew it was him by the knock, three short and one long, the same knock he'd been using on every door between them since they were fourteen. He got himself up off the floor in stages, one hand on the frame, and by the time he got the door open, Melena had jerked awake at the table and was sitting bolt upright with her hand flat over the letter and her eyes wide and hot.

"It's all right," Silas told her. "He's mine."

Remy stood on the step under the awning with a paper sack in one hand and a lidded jar in the other, rain beading on the shoulders of his green coat. His locs were tied back off his face, and the silver cuff on his ear caught what little light the street had. He had his messenger bag across his chest like always, and he looked at Silas the way he always looked at him the morning after anything, top to bottom, cataloguing.

"You look terrible," Remy said.

"You say that every time."

"Every time it's true." Remy's eyes went to the white mitt of Silas's hand and stopped there. "And that's new."

"Work."

"Camille wrap that?"

"Who else."

Remy's mouth went sideways. Remy's mama and Camille had known each other since before either of their sons could walk, and Remy had grown up getting fussed over and fed and occasionally smacked by Camille Graves the same as if he'd been hers. He knew her wraps. He knew what it meant when one went all the way to the wrist. He didn't say anything else about it, which meant he was saving it.

He looked past Silas into the room, and Silas watched him find the window, and the burned line under it, and the bedroom door, and finally the girl at the table with the coat around her shoulders and her hair down and her eyes still too bright. Remy's face did something Silas had seen it do a few times in his life and never gotten tired of watching. It got gentle. Not soft, not stupid. Just gentle, all at once, like a light somebody had dimmed so you wouldn't have to squint.

It's from Odette's by the Third Lane tram, so it's real bread, not that sawdust they sell by the Ring. And there's tea, but I put honey in it, so if you don't like honey, I'm sorry, that's on me."

Melena looked at him like he'd spoken a language she used to know.

Remy came in. He talked the whole time. He talked about the line at Odette's and the old man who'd tried to cut it and the woman who'd stopped him with one look. He talked about the rain, which he said had been coming down sideways on the Third Lane platform like it had somewhere better to be. He found three cups in the cabinet and rinsed them and poured the tea, and the room filled up with the smell of honey and something smoky, and somewhere in all that talking, Melena's shoulders came down from around her ears.

He'd watched him talk a crying runner off a ledge and a Bookkeeper out of a beating and Silas himself out of more bad decisions than either of them would ever say out loud. He just hadn't watched him do it to a stranger before, and he found he didn't have a word for what it was like to stand there and see it work.

Something happened at the corner of Melena's mouth. It wasn't a smile. It was the place where a smile would've been if she'd let it.

"Tea," she said.

Remy brought the tea to the table and set it down and then sat down across from her, not beside her, with the bread between them. Silas stayed in the doorway with his own cup and his bad arm hanging, close enough to move, far enough not to crowd. Melena drank half the tea and ate a piece of the bread, slowly, pulling it apart with her fingers like she was trying to remember whether she was hungry. Remy didn't watch her eat. He ate his own and looked at the window and told her what each of the stitches in Camille's thread line meant, which was a lie, because Remy didn't know what the stitches meant any more than Silas did, but he told it so well that Silas almost believed it.

She'd forgotten it was under her palm. Silas saw the exact second she remembered. Her fingers spread across the page, and her face changed, and he knew the shape of what was coming before it came because he'd watched it come twice already.

"Hey," Remy said.

The paper under Melena's hand began to curl at its corners, and black crept out from under her nails and up the ends of her fingers like ink in water, and she looked down at it and made a small sound that Silas would remember for a long time afterward, a sound like somebody watching their own house burn from across the street.

"Don't," Remy said, without looking at him. Not loud. He said it the way you'd say it to somebody about to step on a loose stair.

He dug into his coat pocket and came out with one of the mints he'd been handing out to everybody in Shadowmere since they were boys, and he unwrapped it, slow, and held up the empty wrapper between two fingers so it caught the light.

"Melena," he said. "What color is this?"

"Silver."

He'd done everything he knew how to do, and he knew how to do a lot, and every bit of it had been him getting between her and something. He'd never once thought to ask her what color his shirt was.

Remy had walked in with bread and done it in five minutes, kneeling on the floor.

Silas didn't resent him for it. He didn't think he did. "Something my mama used to do," he said. "When we were little. Storm nights." He shrugged. "Seemed like the kind of thing that might work."

Silas didn't ask anything else. He knew which *we* Remy meant, and he knew better than to make him say it.

"Yeah," he said, quieter. "I know what that is."

"Ora." Remy met his eyes. "Blue Circle. She owes me."

"Everybody owes you."

"Ora owes me *specifically*."

"Dante would kill me," he said.

"Dante would kill you," Remy agreed.

"Camille would kill you and then she'd kill me for coming up with it."

Because his mother had crouched by that window last night with her hand a half inch off the burned thread and said *a week*, and Camille didn't round down. Because he had a bell he didn't have and a door that didn't latch and a girl who could walk through grave nails. Because Silas had been between her and something since noon yesterday and he was tired, and he was hurt, and he'd watched Remy do in five minutes with a mint wrapper what he couldn't have done with a whole night and a knife, and he didn't have any better ideas.

"One drop," Silas said.

Remy sat up.

"One. I hold it, not her, not you. We see what it does. If it does anything I don't like, anything, we walk out, and we don't argue about it in the street." He looked at Melena. "And you don't buy anything. You don't touch anything. You don't let anybody in that den put anything in your hand. You hear me?"

"I hear you."

"I'm serious."

"I can see that you're serious." Melena looked at his hand, at Camille's wrap, and something moved across her face that wasn't quite an apology and wasn't quite not. "I'm serious too."

Remy was already standing up, already checking his bag, already halfway to the door with that look on his face, the one that meant he'd be talking the whole way there and wouldn't hear a word anybody said back. He stopped beside Melena's chair. He didn't touch her. He just nodded at the silver wrapper still folded in her fist.

"Bring that," he said.

She looked down at it. Then she slid it into the pocket of Dante's coat, carefully, like it was worth something.

Silas sat at the end of the table and watched her do it, and he thought it was the first time since Dante's office he'd seen her face look like it belonged to somebody her age. Easy, almost. Not happy. But easy, for a minute, in the morning light coming grey through Camille's window, with tea in her and bread in her and a boy in a green coat who'd known to get down on the floor.

He didn't know what he felt about it. He thought maybe it was fine not to know yet.

He got up, and got his coat, and went to find his knife.


# CHAPTER 8: SILAS

Blue Circle sat under an old bathhouse three streets from Gutterlight, and Silas had walked past it a few hundred times in his life without ever once going in.

He knew where it was the way he knew where most of Shadowmere's dens were. Dante's map had them. Lorraine's ledger had them. Anybody who'd ever moved people for the Mercers learned early which doors to keep their cargo away from, and the bathhouse was one of them, a squat, tiled building with its old name chipped off the lintel and steam still leaking out of its foundation from pipes nobody had paid to heat in twenty years. The front door opened onto Bath Row. The Mercer map showed a service door around the side for deliveries and a coal chute at the back that had been bricked up since before Silas was born.

Remy took them in through neither.

He walked them past the front and past the side and down a narrow cut between the bathhouse and the laundry next to it, where the steam was thickest and the stones underfoot were slick and warm. Halfway down, he stopped at a section of wall that looked like every other section of wall, crouched, and put his hand flat against a tile near the ground that was a slightly different shade of blue than its neighbors. He said something under his breath that Silas didn't catch. The tile went in under his palm, and a low door swung open in the wall beside it, just high enough for a person to duck through.

Silas looked at it for a long second.

He knew every door the Mercers had in Shadowmere. He'd been learning them since he was nine. This one wasn't on any map Dante had ever let him see.

"Coming?" Remy asked, already halfway through.

Silas filed it next to *Maven* and *Elias* and the stones on Melena's collar, in the part of his head that was starting to get crowded, and let it go for now. He put his good hand on Melena's back without thinking about it, remembered, and took it away. "After you."

She ducked through. He followed.

---

The inside of Blue Circle smelled like warm stone and old water and something sweeter underneath that stuck to the back of the throat.

They came down a short flight of tiled stairs into what had been the bathhouse's main pool, a long, sunken room with a vaulted ceiling and benches running down both sides. The water had been drained out of it a long time ago. In its place were cushions, dozens of them, heaped into nests in the bottom of the pool and along the steps, and on the cushions were people. Not many, at this hour. A dozen, maybe, scattered through the dim like things somebody had set down and forgotten to come back for. Some of them were talking quietly. Most of them weren't doing anything at all. They lay with their eyes open and their faces slack and peaceful, and the blue lamps strung along the ceiling made all of them look like they were underwater.

The walls hummed.

Silas felt it before he understood it, a low vibration coming up through the tile into the soles of his boots. Then his eyes adjusted, and he saw the thread. It ran everywhere, woven through the old grout between the tiles in fine dark lines, crossing and recrossing, so thick in places that the walls looked furred. Grief thread. Lament work. He'd grown up around enough of it to know it by the way it pulled at the edge of his attention, the way it made the room feel like it was holding something heavy and trying very hard not to put it down. Somebody had spent years stitching this place. Somebody had been feeding it grief the whole time.

Beside him, Melena had gone very still.

"You all right?"

"It's loud," she said. She didn't mean the sound.

"Yeah," Silas said. "It's supposed to be."

Miss Ora sat at the far end of the pool on a raised tile platform where the bathhouse attendants used to stand with their towels. She was an older woman, heavy and slow-moving, wrapped in layers of blue so dark it was almost black, with a pair of thick spectacles pushed up into hair gone white at the temples. A long ledger lay open on the table in front of her. Beside her, on a stool, a young clerk with a blue cord knotted around his wrist was counting something into a tin box and very carefully not looking at anyone.

Ora watched them come all the way down the length of the pool. She didn't get up.

"Remington," she said, when they got close enough. "You came in my back door."

"You told me I could."

"I told you that eight years ago." She looked past him at Silas, and her eyes went over his face and his bandaged hand and the way he was standing, and they didn't change. "And you brought a Graves into my house."

"He's with me."

"I can see that he's with you. I can also see he's got a knife on his hip and blood on his hand and he's looking at my walls like he wants to know who stitched them." Ora finally sat back. "What do you want?"

"A drop," Remy said. "One. Measured. For her."

Ora turned her head and looked at Melena for the first time.

It was a long look. Melena held it. Silas watched the old woman take in the coat that was too big, and the boots that were somebody else's, and the hood pulled low, and whatever was underneath all of that that made the hum in the walls change pitch when Melena came near them. Ora's face didn't do anything. But her hand, resting on the edge of the ledger, closed slowly into a fist.

"One drop," Ora said. "You know what my drops cost."

"I know what you owe me."

They looked at each other for a while. Silas didn't know what was being settled, and he didn't ask. It went on long enough that the clerk stopped counting.

"Sit her down over there," Ora said at last, nodding toward a nest of cushions on the pool steps, away from the others. "I'm out of measured. Courier's due any minute with the morning package. You can wait."

---

They waited.

Melena sat on the cushions with her back against the tile and her knees pulled up, and Remy sat beside her, not touching, talking quietly about nothing. Silas stayed on his feet. He found a spot against the wall where he could see the stairs they'd come down and the other door at the far end of the pool and Ora's platform all at once, and he leaned there with his bad arm against his ribs, and he watched.

He watched the people on the cushions. A man about his mother's age was lying on his back with a small dark vial in one loose hand, smiling at the ceiling like it had told him something kind. A woman near the deep end was crying without making any noise, and every few minutes she'd stop and look surprised, and then start again. A boy who couldn't have been older than sixteen had a blue cord on his wrist and was going from nest to nest with a tray of tea, and every time he passed one of the walls, he walked a little faster.

He watched the clerk, who had gone back to counting and kept losing his place.

The courier came down the far stairs about twenty minutes later.

She was young, early twenties maybe, and she came in like somebody who'd done it before but not often enough to stop being careful about it. Her coat was dark and long and fitted close at the waist, nicer than anything else in the room. A stiff-sided tote hung off one shoulder. She walked straight to Ora's platform without looking at any of the cushions, took a sealed package out of the tote, and set it on the table in front of the clerk.

Silas didn't know her. He looked anyway, because looking was what he did.

There was red dust on the heels of her boots. Not much, a fine crust where the leather met the sole, the kind of dust that got into everything on the cliff stairs and stayed there no matter how many lanes you walked afterward. He'd seen it yesterday on the hem of a heavier coat, on a woman who'd looked him over at Pell's counter like he was something she'd have to decide about later.

*Ashvein.* He thought it the same way he had then. *Long way to come.*

The courier turned to go. As she did, her eyes went across the room once, quick, the way anybody's would in a place like this, and they landed on him and stopped.

It was only for a second. But Silas saw her know him. Saw it plain as daylight, the little catch in her face, the way her hand tightened on the tote strap. Then she looked away and kept looking away, very deliberately, all the way back up the far stairs and out.

He didn't know her. She knew him.

In Shadowmere, that was usually somebody else's problem. Silas filed it anyway.

The clerk broke the seal on the package. Inside were small vials packed in grey felt and, underneath them, a wrapped bundle that the clerk lifted out and set aside without opening. Ora took one of the vials, held it up to the blue lamp, and nodded. She drew out a slender glass rod, dipped it, and let exactly one drop fall into a cup of tea. Then she held the cup out over the edge of the platform.

Silas came and took it from her. "Just this?"

"Just that." Ora's eyes went to Melena on the steps. "If one does what I think, you won't need two. And if one don't do anything, two won't either. It's not the kind of thing you chase."

He brought it to Melena himself. He crouched in front of her on his good side and held it out and didn't let go until she had it in both hands.

"Small sips," Remy said.

She drank it in small sips.

For a little while, nothing happened. Then something did.

It was hard to see at first. It happened in her face before it happened anywhere else, the tight line between her eyebrows going smooth, the corners of her mouth losing their grip on whatever they'd been holding since Dante's office. Her shoulders came down. She let out a long breath that seemed to go on longer than her lungs had room for, and she looked down at her hands where they held the empty cup.

"It's quiet," she said.

She sounded astonished. She turned her hands over and looked at her palms, at her fingertips, at the clean brown skin under her nails where the black had been creeping all morning. Nothing moved under them. The hum in the walls had gone back to the same pitch it had been before she came in.

"It's so quiet," Melena said again, and her eyes filled up, and she didn't seem to notice.

Remy was looking at her like she'd done something miraculous. Silas had to look somewhere else.

He went back to Ora's platform because somebody had to pay for it, and Remy's debt was Remy's business but the tea was his. Ora told him what it cost, and he told her it cost less than that, and they argued it the way people in Shadowmere argued anything, not because the number mattered but because not arguing would've meant something. It took maybe five minutes. Remy came up halfway through to say his piece about what Ora owed, and Ora told him to sit down, and he didn't, and the three of them stood there at the edge of the platform going back and forth over the price of one drop while the blue lamps hummed overhead.

When Silas turned back around, the clerk wasn't at the table.

He was on the pool steps, crouched in front of Melena's cushions, and Melena was holding out her hand to him.

Silas was moving before he'd decided to move. He saw it all at once from across the room, the way you saw a fight going wrong. The clerk had one of the small dark vials from the morning package. Melena had something in her fingers that caught the blue light and threw it back green: one of the stones from her collar, pried loose, the thread still hanging off it. The clerk took the stone. Melena took the vial.

"Melena—"

She had the stopper out and the vial at her mouth before he got halfway down the steps. She drank it all.

---

Hush took the warning away and left the fire.

That was what Silas would understand afterward, sitting in his mother's kitchen with his hand in a bowl of cold water, going back over it the way he went back over a fight. All morning there had been a warning. The black under her nails. The steam off her tea. The breath going ragged before anything else did. He'd been watching for those things since yesterday the way he'd watched for Jerrick's bad knee, and they'd always come first, giving him a second, sometimes two, to move.

The Hush took all of that away. It quieted whatever part of her had been ringing the alarm.

It didn't quiet anything else.

The fire didn't climb. It didn't build. It just *happened*, all at once, out of nothing, the way a pipe bursts. One second Melena was sitting on the cushions with the empty vial in her hand and a soft, astonished look on her face, and the next second the cushions underneath her were burning, and so was the air around her, and so was the clerk.

He screamed and went backward down the steps with his sleeve on fire. Silas caught him by the collar with his good hand on the way past and threw him at the pool floor, where the tiles were wet, and kept going.

The heat hit him like a wall. He put his arm up over his face and pushed into it anyway. The cushions were going up one after another, a chain of them along the steps, and the smoke was thick and black and smelled like burning feathers. And worse, under that, the walls. The fire had found the thread. It ran along the grief lines in the grout in thin bright seams, racing across the tile faster than any fire had a right to move, and wherever it went, the hum in the walls rose into something that wasn't a hum anymore. It was closer to a wail.

People on the cushions were waking up. Not fast. Hush didn't let you do anything fast. They sat up into the smoke with slow, confused faces, and some of them started crawling toward the stairs, and some of them didn't move at all.

"Remy!"

Remy was already there.

He'd come down the steps on the far side of the fire, and he was crouched as close to Melena as the heat would let anybody get, which wasn't close. His green coat was smoking at the hem. His face was wet. He didn't reach for her.

"Melena," he said. "Melena, look at me. What color is this?"

He was holding up a mint wrapper. Silas didn't know where he'd gotten it from, which pocket, how he'd had the presence of mind. It was silver and it caught the firelight and threw it back at her.

She didn't see it. She was staring at nothing with her eyes wide open and her whole body burning, and there was nothing behind them. The Hush had taken her somewhere soft and quiet and very far away, and it had left her body here to do this.

"Melena. *Silver.* Say silver."

Nothing.

Silas got the man with the vial off his cushion and onto his feet and shoved him toward the stairs. He got the crying woman by the arm and hauled her up and pointed her at the door, and she went, still crying, still surprised. He got the tea boy by the back of his shirt, the one with the blue cord, who'd frozen in the middle of the pool with his tray still in his hands, and he took the tray and threw it and pushed the boy toward the far stairs. He couldn't do anything about the fire. He couldn't do anything about Melena. But he could empty the room, and so he emptied the room.

"Green," Remy was saying behind him, over the roar. "My coat's green. You told me that this morning. You remember? Green. And his shirt's black. And the floor—Melena, put your feet on the floor. You feel the floor? Put your heels down. You don't have to do anything else. Just your heels."

On the steps, Melena's boots moved.

It wasn't much. Silas only saw it because he happened to look back at the right second, through the smoke. Rhea's old boots, scraping against the tile, pressing down.

"There you go," Remy said. His voice was shaking. "There you go. Green. Silver. Black. Floor."

He put his hand on the tile between them, flat, and started to tap. Six. Two close, a pause, one, then three stumbling ones. Then again. The heat was so bad Silas could see the skin on the back of Remy's hand going red, and he didn't stop. Again. Again.

On the seventh time through, Melena's eyes moved to his hand.

The fire didn't go out. Hush had put it beyond that. But it stopped spreading, and it stopped climbing, and the flames along the cushions began, slowly, to sink back down into themselves. The thread in the walls kept burning, all those years of somebody's grief going up in bright, thin lines, but the fire in the middle of the room, the one with Melena at the center of it, folded down and down and finally guttered out, leaving her sitting in a black ring of ash on the pool steps with her heels pressed into the tile and her eyes on Remy's hand.

Remy didn't stop tapping until she said it.

"Silver," Melena whispered.

Then she slumped sideways against the wall, and he caught her before she hit it, and this time she let him.

---

The clerk was still on the pool floor where Silas had thrown him.

He'd put out his own sleeve against the wet tile, and he was sitting there cradling his burned arm against his chest and rocking a little, and when Silas came down the steps toward him through the smoke, he tried to scramble backward and couldn't get his feet under him.

Silas crouched in front of him. He didn't touch him. He didn't need to.

"Who told you to sell her that?"

"Nobody." The clerk was crying. "Nobody, I swear on my mama, she just—she looked at me, and I—" He shook his head hard, like he was trying to get water out of his ears. "She got in my head somehow. I don't know. I wasn't going to. I don't sell full vials to new faces, Ora'd skin me. And then she was holding out that stone, and I just—"

Silas held out his hand.

The clerk looked at it. Then he dug into his apron pocket with his good hand and came out with the mourning stone, the thread still hanging off it, and put it in Silas's palm. It was warm. It caught the light from the burning walls and threw it back green.

Silas closed his hand around it. "What else."

"Nothing else."

"The package. The other thing in the package. The wrapped one."

The clerk's face went from scared to something worse. "No."

"Where is it?"

"Mister Graves, please. Please. You don't—" His voice cracked. "You don't understand. That package was ours. Ora's. It came in under her name, and it's burned now, and they don't care how it burned. They charge it to the den. Every package we lose, they charge it to the den, and Ora charges it to us, and the people above Ora—" He stopped. His eyes went to the far stairs, the ones the courier had come down, like somebody might be standing there. "They *collect*, Mister Graves. You don't know how they collect."

"Who?"

The clerk shook his head. He just kept shaking it. He was more scared of the people above Ora than he was of the man crouched in front of him with a knife on his hip, a man the clerk had clearly already heard about, and Silas didn't know what to make of that, except that he didn't like it.

He let the clerk go. He went and found the package himself.

It was where the clerk had set it, at the end of Ora's table, though the table had caught on one corner and the felt inside the package had gone to black curls. The vials had burst in the heat. Their smell came up off the wreckage thick and sweet and made his eyes water. The wrapped bundle underneath them had split open along one side, and the cloth had burned through, and something inside had spilled out across the tabletop and onto the tile.

Coins.

A dozen of them, maybe more, scattered in the ash. Silas crouched and picked one up out of the soot with two fingers, and turned it to the light of the burning walls.

It looked like a Veilmark. Hexagonal. Dark silver with a black center. The Veiled One's face in the middle, the prayer around the edge. He tilted it, and the quicksilver flashed bright near one corner of the face, and died before it reached the other side.

He rubbed his thumb across it. Grey-black residue came up out of the grooves.

He didn't need Jude to tell him what it was. He'd need Jude to tell him what it meant. He wrapped it in his handkerchief and put it in the inside pocket of his coat, next to the mourning stone, and he left the rest of them in the ash where they'd fallen.

Then he did the last thing, the thing the Mercers taught their children before they taught them almost anything else.

He went to every door.

There were three. The front stairs up to Bath Row. The far stairs the courier had used. The low door in the wall that Remy had opened with his hand and his under-the-breath word. At each one, Silas found the route marks where somebody had cut them into the frame, small and old and worn by years of hands, the marks that told the routes where this door went and who was allowed to use it. He took his knife out, and he crossed each mark through with two clean cuts, and then he cut a new mark beside it, one Dante had taught him when he was twelve and told him to use only when he had to. It didn't close the doors. It turned them. Anybody who tried to follow a route out of Blue Circle today would find it leading back into itself, or nowhere, or somewhere they hadn't meant to go.

It wouldn't hold forever. It didn't need to. It only needed to hold until whoever collected for Ora's losses came looking, and found nothing that led to Mercer doors, and nothing that led to a red-haired girl in a borrowed coat.

When he got to the low door, Remy was standing there with Melena in his arms.

She was awake, barely. Her head was on his shoulder and her eyes were half closed, and her face had gone soft and slack and peaceful in a way that Silas didn't like at all. Remy's hand was blistered across the back. His coat was scorched to the knee. He watched Silas cut the new mark into the frame, and he didn't say anything about it.

"Ora?" Silas asked.

"Out. Took the ledger." Remy shifted Melena higher against his chest. "Said tell you she'll put it on your bill."

"I don't have a bill with Ora."

"You do now."

---

Camille's thread caught when they brought her through the door.

Silas had wondered whether it would. He'd stopped wondering about halfway up the side lane, when he'd seen Remy's face and the way the heat was still rolling off Melena in his arms, not fire, nothing anybody could see, just heat, a fever's worth of it, like her whole body had been banked and left to smolder. They came in through the front with the token, and the ward along the frame took one long look at her and went from black to orange in a slow line from the top of the door to the bottom. The smell of burning thread filled the room. Under the window, the line Camille had spent an hour restitching last night started to smoke.

Remy laid her on the sofa. Silas called his mother.

She came in less than half an hour, which meant she'd dropped whatever she'd been doing and come on the tram, and she came through her own seal without slowing down. It didn't make its sound for her this time. It barely made a sound at all.

She stood in the middle of the room and turned around once, slowly, looking at every line she'd put in this house. The door. The window. The corners, where her thread ran into the stone, all of them gone brown and brittle like old paper. Then she looked at the girl on the sofa, half-dimmed and burning, with Remy sitting on the floor beside her holding a silver wrapper.

"I said a week," Camille said.

"I know what you said."

"I didn't say a day."

"I know."

She didn't yell. He'd almost have preferred it. She looked at his hand, which was black with soot and still wrapped in her bandage, and at Remy's hand, which was worse, and at the ring of ash Silas hadn't noticed until now on the floor where Melena's boots had been. Then she put her own hand flat against the wall by the door and held it there, the way she'd held it against the window last night.

The wall didn't answer her.

Camille took her hand back. She looked at it.

"It's cooked," she said quietly. "The whole house. Every line in it." She wasn't talking to him. She was talking to the wall, maybe, or to whatever part of her had built it. "I can't stitch behind that. Not here. There's nothing left in these lines to stitch to."

"So where?"

His mother turned around and looked at him, and he knew before she said it.

"Mine," Camille said. "Only my own thresholds are gon' hold her now. The ones I live behind. The ones I check every day." She picked up her wrap off the back of the chair where she'd thrown it. "That means your building."

"Ma."

"Second floor's empty. Has been since spring."

"Dante said one of your houses."

"And I'm telling you which one." She was already tying the wrap. "My house, my thresholds, my say. You want to take that up with your father, you go ahead, and while you do it, I'll be getting this child somewhere she won't burn down by dinner."

---

They took the tram.

There wasn't another way to do it in daylight without carrying her through the street, and Silas wasn't about to carry anybody anywhere with his shoulder. So Remy held her up on one side and Silas walked on the other, and Camille went first with her wrap pulled up and her chin up and her whole body daring anybody on the platform to look at them twice. Nobody did. Melena walked like somebody in a dream, slow and loose, her head tipped against Remy's shoulder, her hood up over all that hair. Anybody who saw her would've taken her for a girl who'd had too much at a den and was being walked home by her people, which in Shadowmere was nothing anybody needed to remember.

Twenty minutes later, the tram let them off at Third Lane.

Number 3 Shadowmere Lane sat where it always sat, between the tailor shop and the threshold worker, its brick gone black from years of rain, the old building number painted over twice with the three still showing through. Silas had come home to it a thousand times. He'd never once brought anybody to it like this.

Camille's thresholds started at the street door. They ran up the stairwell on every landing, thick and fresh, the threads she checked every morning when she came by to tell him what he was doing wrong. Melena walked through the first one, and Silas held his breath, and it hummed low and deep and held. He let the breath out.

The second floor had been empty since spring, like Camille said. Two rooms and a kitchen, a window that looked out at the brick of the building next door, a bed with a bare mattress that Camille had already started making up before Remy got Melena through the door. Camille's thread ran under the window and around the door frame and across every corner. When Melena sat down on the edge of the bed, the thread in the corners went taut and then settled, the way a dog settles when it's decided you're allowed in the house.

Remy sat on the floor by the bed. He didn't say he was staying. He didn't need to.

Silas stood in the doorway for a while and watched them, the girl lying down on his mother's clean sheets with her eyes already closed, and his oldest friend sitting on the floor beside her with his burned hand in his lap and the silver wrapper in his fingers. Then he went up one more flight of stairs to 3C, and let himself in, and shut the door.

The radiator knocked against the wall. Camille's plant was still alive in the window. The crossword from two nights ago was still on the counter, *WRIT* written in his own hand with a broken pencil point beside the last letter.

He took off his coat. He took the handkerchief out of the inside pocket and unwrapped it on the table, and set the counterfeit beside the mourning stone, and looked at the two of them lying there in the lamplight. One coin that wasn't what it said it was. One stone from somewhere he didn't have a name for.

He thought about the clerk, more scared of whoever collected than of him. He thought about the courier, who hadn't known him and then had. He thought about Ashvein dust on the heel of a boot.

One floor down, through the floorboards, he could hear Remy talking, low and steady, about nothing at all.

The Address had been his home for years. As of this afternoon, it was something else too. Silas sat down at his own table with his burned hand in his lap and the counterfeit in front of him, and he tried to decide whether he minded, and found that he didn't have the time to.


# CHAPTER 9: ISOLA

Asha left the house an hour before first bell, and Isola was already dressed.

She'd slept in her clothes. She'd slept, if that was the word, on top of her blanket with her boots on the floor beside the bed where she could get her feet into them without looking, and she'd woken every time the radiator ticked or the wind came through the vent or Tevin turned over on the other side of the wall. By the time she heard the front door lift on its hinges and settle again, soft as anything, she'd been lying there with her eyes open for most of an hour.

She counted to sixty. Then she counted to sixty again, because Asha always stopped on the step to look up and down the lane before she went anywhere, and Isola knew that the same way she knew Asha took her tea with too much sugar and couldn't sleep with a door open. Then she went.

The lane was dark and still. Coal smoke sat low over the cobbles, and the coded lanterns hung above the doors in their quiet rows, amber, amber, amber, one blue at the end of the block where the Penhallows had been watched for a month. Asha was already at the corner. She walked the way she always walked, quick and a little too upright, the structured tote on her shoulder and the long coat swinging. She didn't look back. She didn't need to. In Ashvein, at this hour, nobody was awake but the miners and the women who fed them, and none of them would have stopped a girl walking with somewhere to be.

Isola stayed a block behind. She knew every turn Asha would take before she took it, because there was only one way down to the bridge from Sixth Ward that didn't cost money, and Asha didn't have money, or hadn't, until recently. Down the long street past the shuttered market. Left at the shrine with the broken roof. Down the cliff stairs, all four hundred and some of them, with the vent breathing violet up out of the dark below.

Halfway down, Asha stopped on a landing and stood there. Isola stopped too, flat against the rock face two turns above her, and held her breath.

Asha wasn't looking for anybody. She was just standing there with one hand on the rail, looking out at the chasm and the faint purple glow of the mist and the far lights of the guard post on the Shadowmere side. She stood there long enough that Isola started to wonder whether she was going to turn around and come back up. Then she put both hands over her face, and stayed like that for the space of maybe three breaths, and took them away and kept walking.

Isola didn't move for a while after that.

At the bridge, Asha didn't pay at the plate. She didn't give a name. She walked up to the guard and held up her wrist, and the guard looked at whatever she had there and waved her across without passing the scanner over anything. Isola watched it from the back of the line and felt something go cold under her ribs. Whatever Asha had shown him, or paid him, or promised him, it had gotten her across without blood. Isola had only ever known one thing that did that, and it was hanging around her own neck.

She held up her own token when her turn came, and the scanner made its short flat sound, and she crossed into Shadowmere with the mist starting to come up through the gaps in the stone and Asha's long coat disappearing into it ahead of her.

---

Asha didn't go to the Back Market. She went the other way, toward Gutterlight.

The streets on that side were narrower and older and wetter, and the buildings leaned in over them like they were trying to hear something. Isola had been down here maybe twice in her life, both times with Rae, both times walking fast. She didn't know these turns. She stayed closer than she wanted to and farther than she should have, and twice she lost Asha around a corner and found her again only because the long coat was the nicest thing on the street.

Asha stopped at the end of a row called Bath Row, where a squat old building with a chipped-off lintel was leaking steam out of its foundation in slow white curls. She didn't go in. She went across the street and stood in a doorway with her back against the frame, and she waited.

Isola found a stoop two buildings down with a broken lamp over it and sat down on the step like somebody's tired auntie waiting on a tram that wasn't coming. She pulled her coat in. She watched.

She'd been sitting maybe ten minutes when three people came down Bath Row from the other direction and turned into the narrow cut between the bathhouse and the laundry next door.

Isola knew the first one by his height before she knew him by anything else.

He walked with his left arm held close against his body, and his right hand was wrapped in white all the way to the wrist now, a whole mitt of bandage instead of just the knuckles. The swelling under his eye had gone purple overnight. He walked like his shoulder hurt and he'd decided that was the shoulder's problem. Beside him was a man she didn't know, about the same age, in a green coat that was too good for the street, with his locs tied back and a silver cuff on one ear and a bag across his chest. He was talking. Isola could tell from across the street that he was the kind of man who was always talking.

Between them, half a step behind, walked a girl in a hooded coat that was too big for her, and boots that were too big for her, and as the three of them turned into the cut, a curl of red slipped out of the edge of her hood and caught the grey light, and the man with the wrapped hand reached over without looking and tucked it back in for her. He didn't touch her. He just put it back where it belonged and kept walking.

*Silas.* Isola thought it before she could stop herself, and then she sat there on the cold stoop feeling like a fool for knowing his name, and then feeling worse because the knowing made her look harder.

He looked good, was the problem. He looked like a man who'd gotten hit in the face two days running and was still walking down a street in Shadowmere like he owned the stones, and the green-coat man kept talking at him, and he kept not answering, and once, just once, the corner of his mouth went up at something the green-coat man said. It was the same almost-smile she'd seen at Pell's counter. She was annoyed to recognize it.

Then they were gone into the cut, and the street was empty again, and across the way, Asha was still standing in her doorway.

She'd seen them too. Isola could tell by the way she'd pressed back into the frame.

Twenty minutes later, Asha crossed the street, went down the side of the bathhouse, and disappeared through a door Isola couldn't see from where she sat.

Isola waited. She didn't know what else to do. She sat on the stoop with her hands in her lap and her heart going and the coin in her skirt, and she watched the side of that building like she could see through it if she looked hard enough, and she thought about Asha's hands over her face on the cliff landing, and she thought about the route strip, six holes in a neat line, and she thought about the name going slack under her fingertips in the dark.

Asha came out again a few minutes later. Her tote was flatter. She didn't leave. She stopped under the awning at the corner of the bathhouse and took something out of her coat and looked at it, and put it away again, and stood there a long while like a girl waiting to be told she could go. Then, finally, she turned onto Bath Row and started toward Isola's end of the street with her head down, walking fast.

Isola got up off the stoop.

That was when the bathhouse caught fire.

---

It didn't start the way a building fire started. There was no smoke first, no smell. There was just a sound, a deep, hollow *whump* that came up through the paving stones into the soles of Isola's boots, and then every one of the bathhouse's low basement windows lit up orange at once, like somebody inside had opened the door of a furnace.

Then the screaming started.

People came out of the side of the building and up out of a set of stairs at the front, and they came slow. That was the worst part. They didn't run like people running from a fire. They stumbled out into the street with soft, confused faces, blinking at the smoke pouring out behind them like they were trying to remember what it meant. A man came out with a little dark vial still in his hand. A woman came up the stairs crying, and when she got to the top, she stopped and looked around, surprised, like she'd forgotten where she was going. A thin boy came out with a blue cord on his wrist and his shirt scorched up one side, and he didn't stop at all. He just ran.

Asha had frozen in the middle of the street with her mouth open.

Isola reached her in about four strides, got her by the arm hard enough to bruise, and pulled.

"Isola—"

"Not now."

She didn't know where she was going. She knew she wasn't staying in the open with every eye on Bath Row turning toward that building. There was a gap between the laundry and the shop beside it, a dark, narrow space where the laundry kept its vats, three of them, big as carts and stinking of lye. Isola dragged her cousin into it and down behind the farthest one, and pushed her to the ground, and got down beside her.

Asha was shaking. Isola could feel it through the arm she was still holding.

"That was your package," Isola said, low. "Wasn't it. Whatever you just carried in there."

Asha didn't answer. Her eyes were on the mouth of the gap.

Footsteps. Several sets, coming fast and then slowing. Voices. Isola pressed Asha flat against the side of the vat and put her own body between Asha and the light and stopped breathing.

Three of them came into the mouth of the gap and stopped there, out of the rain. Isola could see their legs past the curve of the vat, and their hands. Every one of them had a blue cord knotted around the wrist. One of them was burned. She could smell it on him, singed hair and cooked cloth, and he was cussing under his breath in a high, steady stream like he couldn't stop.

"—*whole* den, the whole thing, it just went—"

"Shut up about it. I saw."

"Ora's gone. She took the book and went straight out the back, she ain't even look—"

"Ora's always gone. That's Ora." This one sounded older, and tired, and scared in a way he was trying very hard to make sound like anger. "Where's Lio?"

"Graves had him."

The tired one swore.

"Graves had him on the floor by the table, I saw it. And then he went through the package. What was left of it. He took something out of it, I saw him put it in his coat."

"What'd he take?"

"I don't *know* what he took. I wasn't about to go ask him."

"They gon' ask *us*," the tired one said. "You know they gon' ask us. That package came in on Ora's name, and it's burned, and they don't care how it burned. They'll charge it to the den, and Ora'll charge it to us."

"It wasn't us that burned it." The burned one's voice cracked. "It was the girl. The fire girl. You saw her. That wasn't no lamp tipping over, she *lit up*—"

"Then they'll charge it to the fire girl."

Silence. Rain hissing on the stones beyond the gap.

"You think they can find her?"

"I think they can find anybody." The tired one spat. "Blackwood's collecting. That's what I heard. Not the regular ones. Blackwood."

Nobody said anything after that. Isola didn't know the name. She knew the sound it made in a man's mouth, though. She'd heard men in Ashvein say the names of certain crew bosses with exactly that sound, the sound of somebody saying a word they hoped wouldn't hear them.

The legs moved. The voices went with them, down Bath Row and away from the smoke, the burned one still cussing under his breath, and then it was just the rain and the fire and, very far off, somebody shouting for water.

Isola let her breath out.

Beside her, Asha had both hands pressed over her mouth.

---

They stayed behind the vat until the screaming in the street turned into the ordinary noise of people standing around watching a building burn. Then Isola got up, and got Asha up, and looked at her.

Her cousin's face was grey. There was soot on her cheek and on the collar of the nice coat, and her eyes were enormous, and she looked about fourteen years old.

"What was in it," Isola said. "The package."

"I don't open them."

"Asha."

"I *don't*. That's the whole—" Asha's voice broke. "You don't open them. You carry them, and you hand them over, and they punch your strip, and that's all. That's all it is. You don't ask what's in them because the second you ask, it's yours."

"It's yours anyway. You heard them. It came in on Ora's name, and it burned, and somebody's gon' be charged for it."

"Not me. The fire girl." Asha laughed, high and awful. "You heard them. They'll charge it to the fire girl."

Isola thought about a red curl being tucked back into a hood by a bandaged hand. She thought about the hooded figure pressed against the freight wall at Pell's, and the man shifting half a step left to put his shoulder in her line of sight.

*Then they'll charge it to the fire girl.*

"And Blackwood?" Isola asked. "Who's Blackwood?"

"I don't know." Asha shook her head. "I swear to you, I don't know. I've never heard that name. I just carry. I just carry, and I don't ask, and they said six, they said six and the balance closes, they *promised*—"

"Who promised?"

Asha shut her mouth.

Isola looked at her for a long time. At the soot and the coat and the tote that was flatter now than it had been an hour ago. At the cord around her wrist with the little dark charm hanging off it. She thought about Selah setting a sixth plate at dinner and putting a bowl over it. She thought about Micah laughing at the sink, and Tevin's door shut, and her father saying *thirty-one days from now, it's still a no*, mild as anything, like the world could be held in place by a man who meant it.

She thought about the name going slack.

"You got until final bell," Isola said.

Asha looked at her.

"Final bell. Tonight. You tell Mama and Daddy, all of it, every bit, the strip and the coin and whatever this is." Isola put her hand flat against her own skirt where the coin was. "Or you tell me. Every bit. Who promised, and what clears, and why you'd let your own name go loose on that wall for a hundred and eighty Veils. And if you don't, Asha, if final bell rings and you ain't said a word, I'm putting this coin on the table at dinner my own self and letting everybody look at it."

"You wouldn't."

"Try me."

"Isola, you don't know what you're—"

"I know I watched that building go up with you standing in front of it." Isola's voice came out of her low and hard, and she didn't recognize it. "I know I sat in that house last night and put my hand on your name and felt it let go. I know that. That's what I know. So you're right. You're right, I don't know nothing else. And you got until final bell to fix it."

Asha stared at her. For one second, Isola thought she'd say something. Her mouth opened. Her hand came up, the one with the cord on it, like she was going to reach for Isola's sleeve the way she used to when they were small.

Then she turned around and ran.

She ran out of the gap and down Bath Row through the crowd watching the fire, the long coat flying out behind her, and Isola didn't follow. She stood behind the lye vats with her hand still flat against her skirt and watched her cousin go until the coat disappeared around a corner, and she didn't move, because she was afraid that if she moved, she'd go after her and drag her home by her hair, and that wouldn't fix a thing.

---

She should have gone home.

She knew that. She knew it the whole time she was sitting on her stoop again with the broken lamp, watching the bathhouse burn itself down to wet black stone, watching the Shadowmere fire crews come with their hoses and their buckets and their tired, bored faces, like this kind of thing happened every week on Bath Row and they'd long since stopped finding it interesting. She knew it when the crowd thinned, and when the crews left, and when the smoke stopped coming out of the basement windows and the steam took over again, white instead of black. She knew it when her stomach told her she'd missed a meal, and then another one.

She stayed anyway.

She told herself it was because this was the only place she knew in all of Shadowmere that Asha was tied to, and if the people above Ora came looking for what they were owed, they'd come here first. She told herself she wanted to see their faces. She told herself she was waiting for Asha to come back, though she knew Asha wouldn't.

She didn't tell herself she was waiting to see whether he would come back. She didn't have to. She was sitting there anyway.

He came back in the late afternoon, alone.

He came down Bath Row from the same direction as before, and he didn't go to the cut by the laundry this time. He went to the front stairs of the bathhouse, where the smoke had come up the worst, and he crouched at the top of them with his wrapped hand resting on one knee. He stayed there for a while, looking at the stones. Then he reached down with his good hand and touched something on the step, and rubbed it between his fingers, and looked at his fingers.

He stood up, and turned, and started walking. He walked slowly, looking down. Every so often, he'd stop and crouch again, and touch something on the stones, and stand back up and change direction.

He was following something. Isola didn't know what. But she'd watched Omari walk a line of footprints in the red dust outside the house after a raid, crouching and standing and crouching again, reading a path in the ground that nobody else could see, and she knew what it looked like.

She got up off the stoop and followed him.

She didn't do it well. She knew that too. She'd tailed her cousin down four hundred steps that morning without being seen, but Asha hadn't been looking, and Asha wasn't him. She stayed far back, so far she lost him twice and found him again only because he was the tallest thing on the street, and she kept to the sides where the shopfronts threw shadows, and she tried to walk like a woman with somewhere to be.

He led her out of the streets around Gutterlight and down toward a part of Shadowmere she'd never seen, where the buildings gave way to freight yards and the ground dropped into a deep, wide cut in the rock. A rail line ran along the bottom of it, old and rusted and half-buried in weeds that grew pale and thin without any light to grow toward. A bridge crossed the cut, a high iron span on stone legs, built for rail and gone over to foot traffic sometime after the rail stopped running. Isola went up onto the near end of the bridge and crouched down behind one of the iron uprights where the shadows were thickest, and looked down.

He'd gone under it.

Down at the bottom of the cut, where the bridge's stone legs met the rail bed, there was a space where somebody had made a kind of den out of old rail ties and a tarp, and somebody was in it. Isola saw a flash of blue at a wrist. She saw a scorched sleeve.

The burned runner from behind the vats.

He tried to run. He didn't make it three steps. Silas moved like he'd known exactly which way the boy would go before the boy did, and he caught him by the back of the coat with his good hand and turned him and put him down on his face in the rail bed, and then he had the boy's arm twisted up behind his back, high, at the angle where one more inch of pressure would snap something.

He didn't push the inch.

Isola watched him not push it. She was close enough to see the runner's face, mashed against the gravel and twisted with pain and terror, and to hear him yelling something she couldn't make out. She was close enough to see Silas's face too, and it didn't look like anything. It didn't look angry, or cruel, or excited. It looked like a man doing a job that he knew exactly how to do and didn't enjoy. He held the arm right at the edge of breaking, and he leaned down close to the runner's ear, and he said something.

The runner said something back. Isola couldn't hear what.

Silas asked him something else. The runner answered again, shorter.

Then Silas let him go.

He didn't throw him. He didn't hurt him on the way off. He just straightened up and stepped back and let the arm go, and the runner lay there in the gravel for a second like he couldn't believe it. Then he scrambled up and ran down the cut along the rail bed, holding his arm against his chest, and he didn't look back once.

Silas stood there and watched him go.

Isola didn't breathe. She sat behind the upright with her knees pulled in and her heart going so hard she could feel it in her teeth, and she thought about what Micah had said at the sink. *He killed a man last night. Jude said it like it was regular.* She'd been carrying that around all day like a stone in her shoe. She'd looked at his face on Bath Row that morning and thought it, and she'd watched him tuck a red curl back into a hood and thought it, and she'd been thinking it just now when she watched him put that boy in the gravel.

He'd had the arm. He'd had it right there.

He hadn't broken it.

"You can come out," Silas said.

He didn't turn around. He didn't look up. He said it to the empty rail bed in front of him, in that same low, unbothered voice he'd used at Pell's counter, like he was telling somebody the time.

Isola didn't move.

She didn't know how he knew. She'd been careful. She'd been as careful as she knew how to be, and he hadn't looked back once the whole way here. But he knew. He knew somebody was on the bridge, maybe not who, maybe just that, and he stood there in the gravel and waited, giving whoever it was a chance.

She didn't take it. She stayed where she was, behind the iron, with her hand over the coin in her skirt and her pulse going like a bell.

He stood there a little longer. Then he nodded, once, to himself, like he'd gotten the answer he expected, and he started walking. Not fast. He went along the rail bed and up the far bank of the cut on a path through the weeds, and he took it slow, slower than a man with his legs needed to, slow enough that anybody who wanted to follow him could have kept him in sight without trying.

Isola got up.

She stood at the rail of the bridge and watched him climb the far bank, and she opened her mouth, and his name was right there. She felt it come up out of her chest. She felt her lips start to shape it.

"S—"

She stopped.

She shut her mouth so hard her teeth clicked. Down in the cut, he didn't hear it, or if he heard it, he didn't stop. He climbed the rest of the way to the top of the bank, and there, at the very lip of it, he turned around and looked back.

Right at her.

Isola didn't duck. She didn't know why. She stood at the rail with both hands on the iron, too far for him to see her face, close enough that he could see she was there, and she let him look, and he looked. Then he turned and went over the lip of the bank and out of sight, and she stood on the bridge alone with the wind coming up the cut and her face hot, and the whole rest of his name stuck in her throat like a bone.

What would she even have said?

*Hey. You don't know me. You bought me salt. My cousin's carrying something for the people your fire girl just burned out, and they're gon' charge it to her, and somebody named Blackwood is collecting, and you just didn't break a boy's arm when you could have, and I watched you not do it, and I don't know why that's the thing that's going to keep me up tonight.*

She went to Mother Rae instead.

---

Rae's shop sat at the end of a crooked lane off the Back Market, behind a door with no sign on it and a single silver bell hanging from the frame that never rang when the wind blew, only when somebody touched it. Isola touched it. It rang once, low and clear, and the door opened before the sound had finished.

Inside smelled like cedar and cold stone and something underneath that she'd never been able to name. Funeral cloth hung from the ceiling in long dark lengths, layer on layer, so the room was all shadow and soft edges and narrow paths between them. Rae sat at the back at a small table with a lamp on it, wrapped in black from throat to ankle like always, the silver bells around her wrist quiet. She didn't get up.

"Renae," she said.

Nobody called Isola that but Rae, and Rae called her nothing else. It was the name Rae had given her in front of witnesses when she was a baby, the one that sat third in her chain, the one that meant this woman had chosen her. Isola had never been sure whether hearing it made her feel safe or watched. Most days, she decided it could be both.

"I brought your salt yesterday," Isola said.

"I know. It was the evening cut." Rae looked at her over the lamp. "Pell says you don't like his prices."

"Pell ain't got prices. He's got a mood." Isola sat down across from her without being asked. She'd never once been asked. She put her hand in her skirt and took out the coin and set it on the table between them, under the lamp, and took her hand away.

Rae looked at it. She didn't pick it up. Her face didn't change at all.

"Where'd you get this?"

"In the house."

"Whose house?"

"Ours."

Rae looked at her for a long time. Isola looked back. She didn't say Asha's name. She'd decided on the bridge, somewhere between the *S* and the end of it, that she wasn't going to say Asha's name to anybody until final bell, because she'd given her cousin until final bell, and that meant something, even if her cousin had run.

Rae didn't ask again. She looked at the coin a while longer, and then she reached out one finger and turned it over, once, so the prayer side faced up.

"The assayer wouldn't weigh it," Isola said.

"No. She wouldn't."

"I need to know what it is. Not what it's called, I know what it's called. What it *is*. Where it came from. Who's making them and who they're for." She swallowed. "Because somebody's carrying them, Godmother. Somebody's carrying them for a den on Bath Row that burned down this morning, and the people the den owes are gon' come collecting."

Rae's eyes came up off the coin.

"You were at Bath Row."

"I was."

"You saw who burned it."

"I saw who went in before it burned." Isola heard her own voice go careful. "A man they call Graves. A man in a green coat. And a girl with red hair."

The bells on Rae's wrist made a sound. Just one, small, the way they sometimes did when Rae moved, though Isola hadn't seen her move. Rae looked down at them. Then she looked at Isola again, and something was different in her face, though Isola couldn't have said what.

"I'll set a meeting," Rae said.

"With who?"

"With somebody who can tell you what's in that coin, and whose job it is to care." Rae folded her hands on the table. "Halflight. Tomorrow. Third bell. You'll bring that, and you'll sit on the Ashvein side of the line, and he'll sit on the Shadowmere side, and Ironcross will write it all down."

"He."

Rae looked at her and didn't say anything.

Isola felt her face go hot again, all at once, from her collar up. "Godmother."

"You followed him to the rail bridge," Rae said. "And you didn't come out when he asked. I'd say you're halfway to deciding whether to trust him already."

Isola didn't ask how Rae knew. Nobody ever got to ask Rae how she knew. "I didn't say I'd go."

"No," Rae agreed. "You didn't." She slid the coin back across the table to Isola with one finger. "I'm not sending you, baby. I'm telling you where he'll be. Whether you sit down across from him is your business. Whether you believe a word he says is your business. I don't make your choices. I just make sure you have some."

Isola looked at the coin. Then she picked it up and put it back in her skirt.

---

She crossed the bridge home in the last grey light of the day with the mist coming up and the vent glowing violet underneath her. The guard didn't look at her twice. She climbed the cliff stairs, all four hundred and some, past the shrine at the second landing where the cup of tea for the dead had gone cold and grey, and the women hauling water had long since gone home, and the steps were empty except for her.

She thought about Asha's hands over her face on the landing.

She thought about him standing in the gravel, saying *you can come out* to nobody.

She was three streets from home when the final bell rang.

It rolled out over Ashvein the way it did every night, deep and long, from the tower at the top of the ward, and the grounding wires in every wall on the street answered it with their low metal hum, and the miners coming off the last shift slowed on the cobbles and looked up the way they always did, like they were checking that the sky was still there. Isola didn't slow. She walked the last three streets with the bell still ringing in her teeth, and she let herself into the house, and she stood in the front room and listened.

Dinner was on the table. Five plates and a sixth with a bowl over it. Selah at the stove. Omari in his chair. Micah on the couch with his shoes off for once, and Tevin's door open, and Tevin's voice coming out of it, arguing with somebody over the phone about money.

Asha's door was shut.

Isola went down the hall and stood in front of it. She could hear her cousin on the other side. Not crying. Not talking. Just moving around in there, quiet, the way you moved around a room when you were folding things.

The bell finished ringing.

Asha didn't come out.

Isola stood there in the hall for a long time with her hand over the coin in her skirt, and she listened to her cousin pack.


# CHAPTER 10: SILAS

He'd left the rest of the coins in the ash. That was the mistake, and Silas knew it about ten minutes after he'd gotten Melena up the stairs at Third Lane.

He'd been sitting at his own table with the counterfeit in front of him and the mourning stone beside it, and his mind had gone back over Blue Circle the way it always went back over a fight, piece by piece, looking for the moment something had gone wrong that he hadn't caught. He'd found plenty of them. Melena and the clerk. The Hush. The thread in the walls going up. But the one that stuck was the coins. A dozen of them, maybe more, scattered across Ora's table and the floor around it in the soot, and he'd picked up one and wrapped it in his handkerchief, and he'd left the others lying there for anybody to find.

Somebody would have found them. In Shadowmere, somebody always did.

So he'd gone back.

Bath Row had burned itself out by the time he got there. The fire crews had come and gone, and the crowd had thinned down to a couple of kids poking at the steam coming up out of the basement windows with sticks. Silas went down the front stairs as far as the heat would let him and looked at what was left of the pool room from the doorway. It was black from end to end. The cushions were gone. The thread in the walls had burned out of the grout and left the tile cracked and scorched in long, thin lines like somebody had drawn a map on it in soot. Ora's table was a heap of charcoal.

The coins were gone.

He hadn't expected anything else. He crouched at the top of the stairs and looked at the steps instead, and found what he was actually looking for.

It was faint. It would have been nothing to anybody who didn't know to look for it, a smear of grey-black on the edge of the stone, mixed in with the ordinary soot. Silas touched it with two fingers and rubbed them together, and it came off the way it had come off the counterfeit in his handkerchief, fine and slick and wrong. Not soot. Something that lived in the grooves of those coins and came away on whatever touched them.

Whoever had picked them up had been carrying them in his bare hand. And he'd been in a hurry.

Silas followed it.

It wasn't hard, once he had the color in his head. A smear on a doorframe where somebody had steadied himself. A print on a railing. A grey thumbprint on the side of a water barrel at the corner of Bath Row and the next street, where somebody had stopped to drink with his hand on the rim. They led him away from Gutterlight and down through the back streets toward the old freight yards, where Shadowmere's buildings thinned out and the ground dropped away into the long rail cut that nobody had run a train through in thirty years.

Somewhere along the way, he picked up somebody behind him.

He didn't turn around. He didn't speed up. He just knew it the way you knew rain was coming, a certain weight in the air at his back that wasn't there and then was. Whoever it was, they weren't good at it. They stayed too far back and then, when they lost him, they came too close. Twice he heard boots on the stones behind him stop when his stopped. Once, at a corner, he caught a flicker of movement at the very edge of his eye, somebody small stepping back into a doorway.

It wasn't one of Ora's. Ora's runners would've come at him with a knife or not come at all. It wasn't one of Dante's. Dante's people were better than this. He thought about the courier with the red dust on her heels, the one who'd known his face, and then he thought he didn't know enough to think anything, and he left it.

The grey led him down onto the rail bed and under the old iron bridge, where somebody had made a lean-to out of rotted ties and a tarp against one of the stone legs.

The runner tried to bolt. Silas had known he would, and he'd known which way, because there was only one way a scared boy would go from that spot, which was toward the open end of the cut. Silas was already there. He caught the back of the boy's coat with his good hand, turned him, and put him face-down in the gravel, and then he had the boy's arm up behind his back.

He knew exactly how far it would go. He'd known since he was sixteen. He took it to the edge and held it there.

The boy screamed. He had a blue cord around his wrist, and the sleeve above it was scorched, and his hand was black with burns and grey with something else. Silas could see the coins through his coat pocket, pressing a lumpy shape against the fabric.

"Stop," Silas said. "Breathe. I'm not breaking it."

"You *are*—"

"I could. I'm not." He leaned down. "You took those off Ora's table."

The boy sobbed into the gravel.

"I don't care about that. I don't care about you. I want to know where they go."

"I don't—"

"You do. You carry for her. You know where the den sends what comes in." He didn't push the arm any farther. He didn't have to. "Where do they go after Ora."

The boy said something into the ground. Silas leaned closer.

"Blue Six," the boy gasped. "Blue Six. Letter Row. The dead boxes in the old archive. You put them in, somebody takes them out. That's all I know, that's all anybody knows, I never seen who takes them, I swear—"

"Who's above Ora?"

"I don't *know*."

"The clerk knew. The clerk was so scared of them he'd rather I broke his face."

"I don't know their *names*. Nobody knows their names. They come and they take what's owed, and you don't ask, you just—" He was crying hard now. "Please. Please, mister. I got a sister."

Silas held the arm a second longer. Not to hurt him. Just long enough to decide whether he believed it.

He did.

He let go and stepped back. The boy lay in the gravel for a moment like he couldn't understand what had happened, and then he was up and running down the cut along the rail bed, holding his ruined hand against his chest, his coat flapping, and he didn't look back.

Silas reached down and picked up the three coins that had fallen out of the boy's pocket when he went down. He wrapped them in the same handkerchief as the first.

Then he stood there with his back to the bridge and listened.

Whoever had followed him was still up there. He could feel it. Small, still, holding their breath behind one of the iron uprights. Probably thinking they'd done it right. Not one of Ora's. Not one of Dante's. Not the kind of person who'd come down here to finish what Ora's people had started.

"You can come out," Silas said.

He said it to the empty rail bed. He didn't turn around.

Nothing happened.

He gave it a while. He gave it longer than he should have, standing there in the wet gravel with his shoulder throbbing and three counterfeit coins in his pocket and the light going grey at the top of the cut. Whoever it was, they'd decided. He could respect that.

So he nodded to himself and started walking, and he walked slowly. Slower than he needed to. Up the path through the pale weeds on the far bank, taking his time, giving whoever was on that bridge every chance to follow him if they wanted to find out where he went. Halfway up, he thought he heard something behind him, a sound like the first letter of a word, cut off. He didn't stop to find out what word.

At the top of the bank, he looked back once.

There was somebody at the rail of the bridge. Small. A woman, by the shape of her, by the way she stood. Too far to see her face. She was standing very still with both hands on the iron, and she was looking right at him, and she didn't move to hide.

Silas looked back at her for a second. Then he went over the lip of the bank and kept going.

Lorraine didn't look up when he came in.

"He's waiting on you," she said, and turned a page in the ledger.

That was all. No lecture about grown folks' business. So it was like that.

He walked down the long hall past the brokers' offices. The silence-thread over his head hummed, patched near the third office, new stitching against old. He knocked once on Dante's door.

"I know what she said."

That landed. Silas watched it land. His father's jaw moved once.

In Dante's office, under Dante's lamp, it looked even less like anything that had ever been dug out of a mine in the Underside. Dante looked at the stone. He didn't touch it.

Silas didn't take it.

They looked at each other across the desk. Silas could feel his pulse in his burned hand, slow and heavy, beating against the wrap. He could feel the place in his chest where something had cracked open and didn't feel like closing.

He left the stone on the desk. He didn't say anything. He just turned around and walked out, and down the long hall, and past Lorraine at her ledger, and she still didn't look up. But when he got to the door, she said, without lifting her head, "Eat something."

Jude's room was behind the old tram yard, two flights up over a lamp shop that had been closed for as long as Silas could remember, and it always smelled like hot metal and vinegar and the cheap tea Jude drank by the pot.

Jude opened the door before Silas finished knocking. He was barefoot, in a stained shirt, with a pair of jeweler's loupes pushed up into his hair and a burn on the back of one hand that was older than anything Silas was carrying. He looked at his brother. He looked at the bandage. He looked at the soot.

"Mind your business," Jude said, before Silas could open his mouth, and stepped back to let him in.

He sounded exactly like their mother. He always did. Silas had stopped pointing it out around the time Jude had stopped finding it funny, which was about when Jude was eleven.

A scale so fine it had its own little glass house. Pinned to the wall above all of it, a holding-ward map of Shadowmere and the tiers above it, marked up in three colors of ink, with Jude's small, tight handwriting crawling across every inch of it. Silas recognized some of the marks. He'd watched Jude put them there two nights ago, when they were still counting three missing runners instead of two.

In the middle of the table, under the brightest lamp, sat Jerrick's route token.

It didn't look like much. A disk of dull brass the size of a thumbnail, with a thin layer of gold over one face and the Dawnveil stamp pressed into the gold. At least, that's what it had looked like the night of the fight. Jude had been at it since. "Sit," Jude said.

Silas sat. He took the handkerchief out of his coat and unwrapped it on the table beside the token. Four coins. Grey-black at the edges. Jude looked at them and didn't say anything, and then he pulled his loupes down over his eyes and picked up the first one in a pair of fine tongs and held it under the lamp.

Silas waited.

He'd learned a long time ago that you didn't talk to Jude while Jude was working. You could talk all you wanted. He just wouldn't hear it. So Silas sat there with his bad arm in his lap and watched his little brother turn a counterfeit coin under a lamp, and scrape at it with a needle, and drop the scraping into a glass dish with something clear in it, and watch the clear thing go cloudy, and grey, and then a thin, dark blue.

Jude sat back.

He reached for the token without looking, the way he reached for anything on that table. He turned it so the lifted corner faced the lamp and scraped at the brass underneath the gold, very carefully, with the same needle. He dropped the scraping in a second dish. The clear liquid went cloudy, and grey, and then the same thin, dark blue.

Jude looked at the two dishes for a long time.

"Same binding," he said finally.

"Same how?"

"Same." He pulled the loupes off. His eyes were red-rimmed and tired, and they were the only thing in his face that looked his age. You'd have to make it, and you'd have to know how, and you'd have to have access to real quicksilver to make it with." He tapped the edge of the dish with the needle. "These aren't crew coins, Si. Crews make lead and lacquer. These are good. These are built to pass. Not down here. Up."

"Up where?"

"Up as far as anybody'll take them." Jude picked up one of the counterfeits and turned it to show him the face. "Look at the strike. Look at how fresh. Somebody's dressing these up to read like new Crownlight issue. You don't do that to buy bread in the Back Market. You do that to move them up through Ironcross and past it."

Silas looked at the coin in his brother's fingers and thought about the woman at Pell's counter with her hand shoved down deep in her skirt, holding something she wasn't about to let go of for anybody. He didn't know why he thought of her. He let it go.

"And the token?"

Jude set the coin down and picked up the token again, and turned it so the lifted gold faced Silas. He pointed with the needle at the mark underneath.

"That's not a Dawnveil mark."

Silas leaned in. It was small, and it was old, and it had been pressed into the brass long before anybody had laid gold over it. A square with a line through it, and a little hook at one corner.

"Theft mark," Jude said. "It's what the wards stamp into their own stock so they can tell if it walks. This came out of a holding ward. Somebody stole a blank token out of a holding ward's cabinet, gilded it, stamped Dawnveil on the gold, and handed it to Jerrick so his route would look clean." He turned in his chair and pointed at the map on the wall. At a spot on the lower edge of Shadowmere, near the freight yards, where the ink was thickest. "Warehouse row. That ward's mark."

Silas looked at the map. At the warehouse row ward, and the lines Jude had drawn out of it in red.

"Somebody bought Jerrick's route," he said slowly, "to fill that ward."

"Somebody bought Jerrick's route, and his runners, and paid him in a stolen token gilded with the same compound they're using on these." Jude laid the needle down. "And you know where Remy's burial lists don't show that ward's intake?"

"Where?"

"Anywhere." Jude looked at him. "The youngest one's not on them. Not on any of them. I checked every list Remy pulled, and I checked them twice. Three runners went into that line. One's with a block medic. One's in the ground. And the third one, the fifteen-year-old, went into that ward four nights ago under no name, and he hasn't come out of it in a box."

Silas sat very still.

"He's alive," Jude said. "Somewhere behind that ward. As of tonight, he's alive."

He didn't sleep much.

He sat at his own table in 3C with the lamp low and the radiator knocking and his burned hand in a bowl of cold water the way Camille had told him, and he listened to the building. Downstairs, through the floor, Remy was still talking, low and steady. Every so often there'd be a long silence, and then Remy's voice again, and once, very late, the sound of somebody laughing, quiet and surprised, cut off.

He thought about the warehouse row ward. He thought about a fifteen-year-old with no name sitting in a cell somewhere behind a stolen token, and about Jude saying *as of tonight* like it was a clock. He thought about his access suspended, every route mark with his name on it gone dark, and about what he could do for that boy now without any of them. Not much. He thought about Dante's face going soft for one second over a green stone, and then closing.

He thought, for a while, about a small woman at the rail of a bridge with both hands on the iron, not hiding.

His phone rang at dawn.

He knew who it was before he looked. Nobody called him at this hour but his mother, and his mother would've knocked.

"Mother Rae," he said.

"Graves." Rae's voice came down the line the way it always did, low and dry and unhurried, like she had a hundred other calls to make and had saved his for last on purpose. He could hear something behind it, a faint chiming, her bells. "You burned Ora's house."

"Melena burned Ora's house."

"You brought the match." She let that sit. "I have a meeting for you."

"I'm suspended."

"I know. That's why I'm calling you instead of your father." Something in her voice changed, went more formal, the way it did when she was saying something she meant to be held to. "Godbinding rules apply. You'll hear me."

Silas sat up.

Godbinding didn't come up often. It didn't have to. Everybody in Shadowmere knew what it meant, the way everybody knew what Silence meant, or what a black lantern meant. A godchild was yours because you stood up in front of witnesses and the dead and said their name and chose them, and once you'd done that, nobody got to come between you and them. Not blood. Not a father. Not a suspended route. If Rae was calling it, then whatever she was about to say came down from somewhere higher than Dante's ledger.

"I'm listening."

"Halflight. Today. Third bell. Ironcross will hold the line and write it down." A pause. "You're meeting your godson's sister."

It took him a second.

*Godson.* Dante had stood up for two children in Ashvein, a long time ago, before Silas could remember. That made them Silas's too, the way it made them Jude's and Rhea's, whether any of them had ever met or not. One of them was a boy named Micah, who Jude had been sneaking into Ring nights for the better part of a year, who stood at the fence with his hands wrapped around the chain-link and yelled for Silas louder than anybody, a skinny, bright-eyed kid with too much mouth who Silas had bought noodles for twice and couldn't have picked out of a crowd without Jude standing next to him pointing.

He knew Micah had sisters. Jude had mentioned it once. Silas had never asked their names.

"Micah's sister," he said.

"She has something you'll want to see," Rae said. "And you have something she'll want to know. That's all I'm going to tell you."

"Why me?"

"Because it's your kind of trouble." The bells chimed once, faint. "And because she asked for somebody who could read it. You're what I had."

She hung up.

His phone rang again before he'd put it down.

"Rae called you," Dante said.

Silas didn't answer.

"She called me first. Godbinding. She had to." His father's voice was flat and tired and very close, like he hadn't slept either. "I can't stop it, and I won't try. But you hear me, Silas. Halflight is Ironcross. You sit on your side of the line, and you stay there. You don't cross it. You don't let her cross it. That girl is Micah's sister and Rae's goddaughter, and if anything happens to her because of you, there is not a route in this tier I can open to get you out of it."

"I wasn't planning on anything happening to her."

"You weren't planning on Blue Circle either." A pause. "And keep your hands off her too."

*Too.* Silas heard it. Melena, and now this one. He leaned back in his chair and looked at the ceiling, at the old water stain over the window that looked like a map of somewhere he'd never been.

"Which way you mean that?"

His father was quiet for a moment.

"Both," Dante said, and hung up.

Silas sat at his table in the grey light with the phone in his good hand and his burned one throbbing in its bowl, and the radiator knocked, and downstairs, through the floor, he could hear Remy finally, finally go quiet.

Third bell. Halflight. Micah's sister.

He got up to find a clean shirt.


# CHAPTER 11: ISOLA

Asha came to breakfast.

Isola hadn't expected that. She'd lain awake half the night listening to her cousin move around on the other side of the wall, the soft sounds of somebody folding and rolling and buckling, and she'd waited for the double click of the front door lifting on its hinges, and it had never come. Sometime before dawn, the sounds had stopped. And when Isola came into the kitchen at first bell with her coat already on, Asha was sitting at the end of the table with her back to the wall, eating the plate Selah had kept warm for her two nights running, like nothing in the world had happened.

She didn't look at Isola. She ate her eggs. She asked Selah if there was more tea.

Final bell had come and gone last night, and Asha hadn't said a word, and Isola had stood outside her door with her hand over her pocket and done nothing, and now here they both were, and Selah was pouring tea, and Micah was asking for the pepper sauce, and Omari was reading the ward notices with his glasses down his nose, and the coin was still in Isola's skirt instead of on the table where she'd promised it would be.

She'd meant it when she said it. She'd been sure. "You going somewhere?" Selah asked her. "Coat on at first bell?"

"Errand for Godmother."

Selah looked at her. Then at Asha. Then back at her. She didn't say anything, which said plenty.

At the end of the table, Asha finally lifted her eyes. Just for a second. Halflight was half a staircase below Ashvein and one locked door above Shadowmere, and it belonged to Ironcross, the way most of the doors between tiers belonged to Ironcross, which meant it belonged to nobody who'd ever had to walk through it.

Isola had been through it once, years ago, with Rae. She remembered it bigger. It was an old transfer station from back when the tiers had run trains between them, a long, low hall with a curved ceiling of grey tile, and the tracks that used to run down the middle of it had been pulled up and paved over a long time ago, so that now there was only a pale scar in the floor where they'd been. At either end of the hall stood a gate. The one behind her led up to Ashvein. The one in front of her led down to Shadowmere. Both of them were locked, and both of them had an Ironcross clerk sitting in a little glass booth beside them, and both clerks looked like they'd been sitting there since the trains stopped running.

Along one wall, under a layer of grime nobody had bothered to clean, the old route board still hung. Nine tiers, painted in fading white letters one above the next, the way every child in the Underside learned them before they learned to cross a street. Crownlight at the top, nearest the surface, where the Synod made laws for places its officials rarely set foot. Dawnveil under it, keeping the people rich enough to benefit from those laws. Ironcross turning both into contracts. Then Stoneveil, and Lowtorch, and Ashvein, and Shadowmere, and Gravebend, and Nightroot, all the way down, everything beneath the top three supplying whatever the top three needed.

Isola had always thought the board was honest in a way the Synod probably hadn't intended. It didn't pretend the tiers were equal. It just listed them in order, top to bottom, like a ledger.

Going down was easy. That was the thing nobody told you when you were small and learning the order. The gates only ever wanted to know where you were headed. Going back up, that was different. Going back up required somebody important to claim you deserved to return.

"Name," said the clerk at the Ashvein booth.

"Isola Wraithmoor."

"Entry tier."

"Ashvein."

"Thank you," he said.

Isola hadn't expected that either. She didn't know what to do with it, so she didn't do anything.

"That's not the only thing you heard," he said.

"No."

"You heard where this came from." He nodded at the coin, which was back on her side of the table. "Who carried it. Whose house it was in."

"I told Rae it was in my house."

"I know. But not who brought it there."

Isola didn't say anything.

He leaned forward a little, his elbows on his knees, his good hand loose between them. He was so close now that if either one of them moved their knees, they'd touch. Neither of them did.

"I could find out," he said. "You know that. I could pull that name out of you if I had to."

He said it quietly. He didn't say it like a threat. He said it like a fact he thought she should have, the way he'd said *Debt's mine* at Pell's counter, settled and plain.

"I watched you not break a man's arm yesterday," she said. "You ain't gone break mine."

He didn't move. But she saw it go through him, all at once, the rail bridge and the boy in the gravel and the shape at the top of the iron span that hadn't come down when he asked. She saw him put it together. She saw his eyes go to her coat, the Ashvein cut of it, heavy at the shoulders, and her boots, and then back up to her face.

"That was you," he said.

"That was me."

"On the bridge."

"You could've looked back sooner."

"I looked back at the top."

"I know. I saw you." She lifted her chin. "You stood there like you had all day."

"I was letting you decide."

"I decided."

He laughed again, quieter this time, mostly in his chest. He shook his head and looked down at the table and back up at her, and his eyes were different, sharper and warmer at once, and Isola understood that he'd just figured out she'd been paying attention to him for two days, and that he was enjoying it, and that he knew she knew he was enjoying it. She also understood that he'd been paying attention right back, the whole time. *I was letting you decide.* He'd walked slow up that bank on purpose. He'd known somebody was following him and he'd made it easy.

It was a terrible thing to know about a man you'd only met twice.

"You followed me," he said.

"You led me."

"I didn't know it was you."

"Would you have walked faster?"

He considered it. He actually considered it, sitting there in the Ironcross chair with his elbows on his knees, and she watched him do it and wanted very badly to laugh, and didn't.

"Slower," he said.

---

She asked him things after that. She'd come to ask, and she'd decided on the stairs that she was going to get her money's worth, and when he started to ask her something back, she held up one hand.

"You said the debt was yours," she said.

He tilted his head.

"At Pell's. I said I wasn't trying to owe nobody in Shadowmere, and you said the debt was yours. So it's yours." She folded her hands on the table. "I'm collecting. You answer what I ask, and we're square."

"That's not how debt works."

"That's how it works in my family."

"Your family must be terrifying."

"You have no idea."

He looked at her a while. Then he spread his good hand open on the table, palm up, on his side of the line. "Ask."

She asked him what the stolen token had looked like, and he told her. She asked him whether the boy at the rail bridge had said anything about who took things out of Blue Six, and he told her no, and she believed him. She asked him what a dead box was, exactly, and he explained it, and she asked him how long they'd been in the old archive, and he said longer than he'd been alive. She asked him what happened to the fire girl if the people above Ora came looking, and his face shut, and he said, "Nothing," and she believed that too, though for different reasons.

She didn't ask him about the man in Lowtorch. She'd thought about it on the stairs. She'd thought about Micah at the sink saying *Jude said it like it was regular*. She'd thought she'd ask, and then she'd sat down across from him and found she didn't want to hear him answer, not here, not yet. So she left it.

At some point, he reached for the coin.

It was on her side of the line, under her hand. She'd been turning it over without thinking. He reached across, just to the brass, just to the edge, like he wanted to look at it one more time, and his fingers stopped a half inch from hers.

Isola closed her fist over it.

"Ask," she said.

He looked at her fist. Then at her face. His hand stayed where it was, right at the line, not crossing it.

"Can I see it again?"

She opened her hand. He picked it up from her palm without touching her skin, which she wouldn't have thought anybody could do. He looked at it for a while. He gave it back the same way.

Then he reached into his coat again and came out with something else.

It was small and flat and black. A route tag, the kind Isola had seen on the coats of runners and brokers in the Back Market, enameled, with a single route mark cut into it in silver. Three short lines, stacked.

He set it on the table on his side of the brass line, right at the edge of it, and took his hand away.

"Black three," he said. "It's mine. Hold it to your phone, and anything you send comes straight to me. Nobody else. Nobody reads it, nobody routes it, nobody knows it went." He looked at her. "If you find out where that coin came from. Or who's carrying for Blue Six. Or if somebody comes to your house asking about a package that burned."

Isola looked at the tag. She didn't reach for it.

"Why do you trust me?"

"I don't."

She looked up.

"I'm giving you a way to tell me when I should," he said.

She sat there for a moment. Then she looked at his hand, which was still on the table, right beside the tag, an inch from the brass.

He moved it. He took it back, slowly, and set it on his knee.

Isola reached across the line and picked up black three.

It was warm from his coat. She turned it over in her fingers and looked at the three silver lines, and then she put it in the inner pocket of her coat, over her heart, where the cord with Rae's token already hung.

"The route's mine now," she said.

"Nothing's yours down here," Silas said, "until somebody buries it."

He said it the way you'd say a proverb your grandmother used to say, and Isola didn't know what it meant, and she could tell by his face that it was some kind of joke, and that the joke was on somebody, and that she didn't have all of it. She decided not to ask. She'd find out. She stood up instead, and settled her coat, and he stood up too, on his side, and the table between them was so small that standing put them closer than sitting had.

He was very tall. She'd known that. It was different with nothing between them but a dirty brass strip and a table the size of a dinner plate.

"What if I need something," she said, "that ain't about the coin?"

He looked at her. He didn't answer right away. She watched him not answer, and she watched him think about it, and she watched him decide.

"Use the same route," he said.

She turned to go. She took three steps toward the Ashvein gate before she stopped. She hadn't meant to stop. She could feel the clerks watching her from their booths. She could feel him watching her from the line.

She turned around.

"Isola," she said.

That was all. Not the rest of it. Not Amara, not Renae, not Wraithmoor, not her block. Just the first one, the one her mama had picked, the one that was hers before anybody else got a say in it.

He didn't say anything. He just stood there on his side of the brass with his wrapped hand at his side and her name in the air between them, and she watched him take it, and hold it, and put it somewhere careful.

Then she went.

---

The man was waiting in the corridor on the other side of the Ashvein gate.

She'd come through the gate and signed out in the clerk's door-sized ledger and started up the half staircase toward Ashvein, and she'd felt him before she saw him, the same way she'd felt the two men walk up to Pell's counter. Then she saw his coat. Plain and dark, cut long. He was leaning against the wall at the top of the stairs where the corridor turned, like a man waiting for somebody, and he didn't look at her when she came up. That was how she knew. Everybody looked at you in a corridor like that. It was only the ones who were supposed to be watching who knew better.

She'd seen that coat at the end of Pell's counter, watching her hand. Watching Rae's token.

Isola didn't slow down. She didn't speed up. She kept her eyes on the stairs and her hand off her pocket and climbed past him like he was a piece of the wall, and at the turn, where the corridor bent and the lamps thinned and there was a single breath of shadow between one light and the next, she let the Veil slip.

She'd been doing it since she was a girl, and she'd never once been able to explain it to anybody who couldn't already do it. It wasn't hiding. You couldn't hide in an empty corridor. It was more like going thin. Like letting go of the part of yourself that made people's eyes catch, the way you'd let go of a rope, so that anybody looking at you would slide right off and land on the next thing over.

She felt it go. She felt the man's attention come around the turn after her, and reach for her, and slide.

She kept walking. She didn't look back. She went up the rest of the stairs and through the door at the top and out into the coal-smoke morning of Ashvein, and by the time the man came around the corner looking, there was nothing in that corridor but lamps and a dirty floor.

She didn't let it go until she was three streets from home.

When she did, it came back into her all at once, the way it always did, like walking into a warm room after the cold. She stood on the corner by the shrine with the broken roof and breathed for a minute. Her hands were shaking. She looked down at them and made them stop.

Then she put her hand inside her coat and touched black three, warm in her pocket, against her heart.

She thought about his face when she'd said *Silas*. She thought about him saying *slower*. She thought about him taking his hand back from the line, slow, so she'd have room to reach.

She thought about a man in a dark coat waiting at the top of the Halflight stairs, watching for Rae's goddaughter.

She took her hand out of her coat and went home, and she didn't let herself smile until the door was shut behind her, and then only for a second, and then she went to find out what Asha was packing now.


# CHAPTER 12: SILAS

Black three stayed dark all afternoon.

Silas knew it would. She'd taken the tag maybe four hours ago, and she'd taken it the way she'd taken everything else at that table, like she was doing him a favor and wanted him to know it. She wasn't going to use it today. She probably wasn't going to use it tomorrow. If she used it at all, it would be because something had gone wrong, or because she'd found out something she thought he ought to know, and either way it wouldn't be soon.

He checked anyway. He checked on the tram home from Halflight, and he checked on the stairs, and he checked standing in his own kitchen with the kettle going. He checked enough times that he started to feel stupid about it, and then he checked once more to prove to himself that he didn't care.

*Isola.*

He'd been carrying it around since she said it. Three steps toward the Ashvein gate, and then she'd stopped, and turned around, and handed it to him across all that dirty tile like she was setting something down on a counter. Not the rest of it. Just the first one. He knew the rest, or enough of it. Micah's sister. Rae's goddaughter. A Wraithmoor out of Ashvein's Sixth Ward, whose family kept their names on a wall. Any of that he could've gotten out of Jude in five minutes if he'd asked.

He hadn't asked. He didn't want it from Jude. He wanted it from her, the way she'd given him the first one, standing at the gate with her chin up like she was daring him to do something with it.

He didn't know what he was supposed to do with it. He kept turning it over anyway.

He thought about the coin going back and forth across the line between them, more times than either of them needed. He thought about *slower*, and the look on her face when he'd said it, like she was about to laugh at him and had decided he hadn't earned that either. He thought about her fist closing over the coin, and *Ask*, and how he'd taken his hand back from the brass slow so she'd have room.

He thought about *your fire girl*, and that one he couldn't stop thinking about at all.

---

He told Remy before he told anybody else.

He came down the stairs from 3C in the early evening, and the door to the second floor was open, and Remy was in the kitchen trying to make tea in a pot that had been Camille's before it was Silas's and had a lid that didn't fit anything. Melena was on the couch with her boots off and her feet pulled up under her, reading the paper Camille had left on the table last night with the crossword half done. She'd filled in two more answers. Silas checked. They were right.

"Back here," Silas said, and Remy came.

They stood on the landing outside the door with Camille's thread humming low in the frame beside them, and Silas told him. Runners behind a lye vat on Bath Row. The package came in on Ora's name, and it burned, and the people above Ora were going to charge it to somebody. If not the den, then the fire girl.

Remy didn't say anything for a while. His thumb went to the bracelet on his wrist and rubbed at it.

"Who told you?"

"Somebody who heard it."

"Somebody." Remy looked at him. "That's a whole new kind of vague, coming from you."

"It's good. I trust it."

"Since when do you trust *somebody*?"

Silas didn't answer that. Remy looked at him a while longer, at whatever was or wasn't in his face, and then let it go, which was unusual enough that Silas noticed. Remy didn't usually let anything go. He just put it in a pocket for later.

"So what now?" Remy asked.

"Now she doesn't leave this building. Not for the market. Not for air. Not because she's going stir-crazy and wants to walk to the corner." Silas kept his voice low. "Ma's thresholds hold her, and they hold anybody coming up those stairs who isn't us. That's all we've got. Dante pulled my marks."

"I heard."

"Course you heard."

"Lorraine told my mama. My mama told me. Everybody in Shadowmere probably knows by now that Graves got grounded." Remy almost smiled. It didn't take. "I'll stay."

"I didn't ask you to."

"You didn't have to." Remy looked through the door at Melena on the couch with the newspaper, her hair falling over one shoulder, frowning at the crossword like it had personally offended her. His face went gentle, the way it had at the door of the Gutterlight house that first morning. "I wasn't going anywhere."

---

She asked for the blackroot around the time the lamps came on.

Silas kept a bottle in 3C, on the shelf above the sink where Camille had decided to keep her ward ledgers and his unpaid tram notices. He'd mentioned it once, at the table, without thinking. Melena had looked up from the crossword and asked what it was, and he'd told her, and she'd been quiet for a while, and then she'd asked if she could have some.

"No," Remy said.

"I wasn't asking you."

"I know. I'm answering anyway."

She turned to Silas. "Is it like the Hush?"

"No." He thought about it. "It's a drink. You put it in tea or you drink it straight. It takes the edges off. It doesn't take anything else."

"Will it make the fire worse?"

"I don't know."

"Will it make it better?"

"I don't know that either." He looked at her. At the shadows under her eyes, which had been there since Dante's office and had gotten deeper every day since. At the way she was holding the pencil, too tight. "When's the last time you slept?"

She didn't answer.

"Before the memorial," she said finally. "I think."

Remy looked at the floor. Silas went up and got the bottle.

He poured her a little in a cup and watched her drink it, and Remy watched her drink it, and both of them sat there in Camille's kitchen with their hands on the table, ready to move, waiting for the lamps to flicker or the thread under the window to start to smoke. Silas kept his eyes on her fingers. He'd been watching them for three days. He knew the first sign by now better than he knew anything else about her, the thin black line coming up at the quick of the nail, and he waited for it.

It didn't come.

What came instead was quieter. Her shoulders loosened. Her hands, wrapped around the cup, lost some of their grip. She sat back on the couch and pulled her feet up and let her head rest against the cushion, and she looked at the ceiling, and her face did something Silas hadn't seen it do yet, which was nothing at all. Not grief, not fear, not fire. Just a girl, tired, in a borrowed sweater from the drawer Camille had filled, with a cup of blackroot in her hands and her hair coming down.

"Oh," she said, softly. "That's nice."

Remy let out a breath like he'd been holding it for an hour.

She drank the rest. She asked for a little more, and Silas gave her a little more, less than she asked for, and she noticed and didn't argue. The lamps stayed steady. The thread stayed dark. After a while, Remy got up and poured himself some too, and then he poured Silas some, and Silas didn't stop him.

They sat there like that. It was the closest thing to a regular evening any of them had had in days. Remy told a long, winding story about his mama and a neighbor's goat that Silas had heard at least four times, and it got worse every time, and Melena laughed at the end of it, a real laugh this time, low and surprised, and didn't cover her mouth. Silas sat at the end of the couch with his bad arm in his lap and his burned hand on his knee and his cup going warm, and he listened to the radiator knock between the floors every time a tram went by underneath the street, and for a little while he didn't think about anything.

Then Melena reached into the sweater and took out the envelope.

---

She didn't open it right away. She held it in her lap and looked at it, the way she had at the Gutterlight house that first night, turning it over and running her thumb along the broken seal. Remy went quiet. Silas set his cup down.

"You can read it," Silas said. "We'll go."

"No." She didn't look up. "Stay. Both of you. Please."

So they stayed. She took the letter out and unfolded it. Silas could see it from where he sat, the careful, close handwriting in dark ink, the single word on its own line at the top. He'd seen it through the paper two nights ago. He'd been carrying it around in the back of his head ever since, next to *Maven*, next to the stones on her collar.

Melena put her finger on that word.

"Elias," she said.

She said it quietly, like it was a name she hadn't said out loud in a long time and wasn't sure she still had the right to. Remy looked at Silas. Silas didn't look back.

"That's how it starts," Melena said. "Elias. Not me." She laughed, and it wasn't a laugh at all. "I carried this down seven tiers. I wore a veil through four of them and walked through a hundred people who would have sold me for my collar if they'd seen it. I held it in my hand in Dante's office while I set his room on fire. I have been holding it for weeks." Her voice was very steady. "And she didn't write it to me. She wrote it to him."

"Who's Elias?" Remy asked, gently.

Melena didn't answer. She looked at the letter for a long time.

"Somebody who isn't here," she said finally.

That was all. Silas watched her decide it was all, and he watched Remy watch her, and neither of them asked again. Whoever Elias was, she'd put him somewhere behind her ribs where nobody in Shadowmere was ever going to get at him, and Silas thought maybe that was right. He'd never heard the name. Not from Dante, not from Lorraine's ledger, not from anybody who'd ever come through the Mercer office or a Gutterlight cage or his mother's kitchen. He had a feeling nobody in this whole tier had ever heard it.

"Her last letter," Melena said. "The last thing she ever wrote. And she wrote it to him." She pressed her lips together. "And I brought it all this way anyway, because I didn't know what else to do with it. Isn't that stupid?"

"No," Remy said.

"It's very stupid."

"It's not stupid," Remy said again, and something in the way he said it made her look at him.

Silas looked at the window.

"She told me to come here," Melena said. "In the letter. It's the only part that's for me, the very end, after all the rest. She says if anything happens to her, I'm to find Maven, and Maven will know where to send me. She gave me the route phrase. She said it hadn't been used in twenty years and nobody would be listening for it." She laughed again, worse than before. "Somebody was listening."

*Maven.* Silas filed it next to the rest and then, for the first time, understood where it went.

"Mother Rae," he said.

Melena nodded. "That's what everybody here calls her. My mother only ever called her Maven."

"Your mother knew Mother Rae."

"My mother knew everybody." Melena folded the letter closed. She didn't put it away. She held it in both hands in her lap and looked down at it, and then she looked up, at Silas, and her face was very calm.

"You want to know who I am," she said. "You've wanted to know since your father's office. You asked him. You asked me. You looked at my collar every time I wasn't looking, and you took the stone back from that man and didn't give it to me, and I know you asked your father about it, because you came home with your face like that." She tipped her head toward him. "So I'm telling you."

Remy had gone very still beside her.

"My name is Melena Lockwood," she said. "My mother was Lucia. I'm the Imperial Princess of Remnia."

Nobody said anything for a while.

Silas sat there and let it go through him. He'd known it was something. He'd known since the office, since the heat came off her and the seals answered and there was no cold underneath it, no mark, no pull, nothing from the space between living and dead. Fire belonged to Remnia. Everybody knew that much. He'd known she'd come down from farther than any tier he had a name for, and he'd known she'd come carrying somebody else's mail.

He hadn't known it was this.

Beside her, Remy was staring at her like she'd told him the sky had come down into Shadowmere and was sitting on the couch. Then, slowly, his face changed. It didn't go afraid. It didn't go stupid. "Okay," Remy said.

Melena looked at him.

"Okay," he said again. "That's a lot. That's a whole lot." He rubbed his thumb on his bracelet. "You still want more tea?"

She stared at him for a second. Then she laughed. It came out of her before she could catch it, sudden and real, and she put her hand over her mouth, and her eyes filled up, and she nodded.

Remy got up to make more tea.

Dante had known. Of course Dante had known. Dante had known when he poured that glass at noon three days ago and kept his shoes on and his keys on his belt. He'd known when he said *she ain't regular cargo*. He'd known when he said *she's too busy grieving to see its mouth open*, and he'd known, last night, standing at his desk with the mourning stone in front of him and his face going soft for one second before it closed.

"Lucia," Silas said.

Melena looked at him.

He'd been turning it over since she said it. He hadn't recognized it at first, not the way you recognize a name you've heard a thousand times. He'd heard it said in Lorraine's voice, at the desk, a woman's name written in the ledger in Lorraine's careful hand, with a line of route marks beside it longer than any he'd seen before.

"More than once."

"She never told me." Melena looked down at the letter. "She never told me. He didn't have anything for that. Neither did Remy, standing in the kitchen doorway with a teapot in his hand.

Remy brought the tea. He set Melena's cup down in front of her and sat down beside her on the couch, close, closer than he'd sat since the first morning, and he didn't touch her. He just sat there with his own cup in his hands and his shoulder an inch from hers.

"Yeah," he said. "I'll stay."

She just leaned, slow, until her head was on his shoulder and her hair was spilling down the front of his shirt, and Remy went still for a second, and then he didn't. He shifted a little so she'd fit better. He set his cup down on the floor where he could reach it. He didn't put his arm around her. He just sat up straight on Camille's couch and let her lean on him, like it was the most ordinary thing in the world, like it wasn't anything to make a fuss about.

She was asleep in about five minutes.

He watched her breathing slow down and her hand go loose on the letter, and Remy reach over very carefully and slide the letter out from under her fingers and fold it and set it on the table where she'd see it when she woke up. He watched Remy settle back against the cushions with his head tipped back and his eyes open, staring at the ceiling, not moving a muscle, like a man holding a bird on his shoulder who'd decided he'd rather stay up all night than risk it flying off.

Silas felt nothing but relief.

He looked for something else. He looked for it the way he'd look for a bruise after a fight, pressing the places where it might be. He'd watched Remy do in five minutes with a mint wrapper what he couldn't do with a night and a knife. He'd watched him get her to laugh, and eat, and sleep, things Silas hadn't managed to get her to do in three days of trying. He'd watched her lean on Remy without asking, after she'd told Silas twice not to touch her.

He'd thought, that first morning, that there might be something sharp in that for him. Something with an edge.

There wasn't. He pressed on every part of it, and all he found was relief, wide and plain and almost embarrassing. Somebody had her. Somebody who wasn't him, who knew how to do the thing she needed, who'd gotten down on the floor without being told. She'd come seven tiers carrying a letter that wasn't for her, and she'd finally put it down, and she was sleeping. Silas didn't need to be the one. He'd never needed to be the one. He just needed somebody to be.

He got up quietly. Remy's eyes came down from the ceiling and found his.

"I'll take the stairs," Silas said, low.

Remy nodded. Then, as Silas reached the door: "Si."

Silas turned.

Remy didn't say anything for a second. His face was doing something complicated in the lamplight, something Silas had seen it do maybe twice in their whole lives. Then he just shook his head.

"Nothing," he said. "Go to bed."

He thought about Lucia in the chair by Dante's window with a scarf over her hair, smiling at a seventeen-year-old boy like his name was worth something. He thought about Dante closing the door. He thought about people being gone, out, not dead, and how he'd stood in the hall and watched it happen and never once asked his father where *out* was.

He thought he probably should've asked.

His phone buzzed. He picked it up too fast, and knew it, and looked anyway.

Jude: I got Blue Six tonight.
