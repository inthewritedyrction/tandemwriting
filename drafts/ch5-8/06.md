# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Asha missed dinner, and in the Wraithmoor house, missing dinner without sending word was about one step below murder.

Selah set her plate out anyway. Cornbread, greens, the last of the smoked turkey neck cut up in the beans so it'd stretch. She put the plate at Asha's place at the end of the table, between Tevin and the wall, and laid a clean cloth over it like Asha was gonna walk in any second and sit down.

Nobody said anything about the plate. Everybody looked at it.

Omari came to the table last, the way he always did, winding his watch. He'd set it right that morning and it was already eleven minutes behind, the same as every day. He sat down at the head of the table, looked at the cloth over the plate, and looked at Isola.

Isola passed the cornbread to Micah.

"Where your cousin?" Omari said.

"Errands," Selah answered for her, and said grace before anybody could ask what kind.

Omari let her get through all of it. Thanks for the food, thanks for the roof, keep the names on the wall and the Shrouds off the block, amen. When she was done, he picked up his fork and didn't use it.

"Errands," he said.

"That's what she told me this morning." Selah spooned greens onto his plate without being asked. "Eat."

He ate. That was the thing about Omari. He'd let you have your little lie as long as you served it to him hot.

Across the table, Tevin was eating like somebody was gonna take the plate. He'd stayed in the spare room all afternoon with the door shut, and he'd come out the second he smelled cornbread, which Isola had known he would. You couldn't keep a grown man mad on an empty stomach. She'd learned that from Selah before she was ten.

His locs were still pulled back neat in front. He hadn't been out. She'd have heard the door.

"You good?" Isola asked him.

Tevin didn't look up. "I'm eating."

"I see that."

"So why you asking?"

"'Cause you eating like the beans owe you money."

Micah laughed into his cup. Tevin cut his eyes at both of them, and something in his shoulders let go a little.

"Didn't go nowhere," he said. "If that's what you asking."

"I know you ain't."

"How you know?"

"'Cause I'd be at the east gate right now, cussing out a boy with a red armband, and I'm not. I'm eating greens."

Tevin snorted and went back to his plate.

Micah's phone buzzed.

It was in Isola's skirt pocket, on the side without the coin, where she'd kept it since noon. Micah's whole body went up like a dog hearing a can opener.

"That's Danika," he said.

"You don't know that."

"Who else texting me?"

"Half of Sixth Ward, probably. Every girl you ever lied to." Isola pulled it out and looked at the screen anyway, because she wanted to know too.

Danika: Packet's here. Reading it tonight. Tell Kai I said don't get excited.

Isola read it twice. Something in her chest that had been clenched since twelve-oh-four eased just a little.

"Well?" Micah said.

"She said don't get excited."

"That mean she got it?"

"That mean she got it."

Micah put both fists in the air like he'd won a fight. Selah said "Micah Amari" without looking up from her plate, and his fists came down. He was still grinning.

Isola slid the phone across the table to him. He looked at it, then at her, suspicious.

"For real?"

"Packet's gone. Danika got it. I can't hold your phone hostage for the rest of your life."

"You could try," Tevin muttered.

"I could." Isola picked up her fork. "Don't make me."

---

She and Micah did the dishes after.

That was the deal. Selah cooked, the kids cleaned, Omari checked the locks. He was doing it now, out in the front room. Isola could hear him. The deadbolt slid, slid back, slid again. A pause, where she knew his fingers were resting on the upper lock. After that, his boots back down the hall and his door clicking closed.

Micah washed. Isola dried. He'd gotten too tall for the sink a year ago and had to bend over it like an old man, and he complained about it every single night like it was news.

"So," he said. "You find out anything down there?"

She'd been waiting for that since dinner. "Down where?"

"Shadowmere. You came back smelling like rain. Ain't no rain in Ashvein."

"I went for Rae's salt."

"Uh-huh. And you came back with the evening cut in double paper." He handed her a bowl. "Mama asked you if you found Asha. I heard her. And you said crowd."

Isola dried the bowl. "I saw her."

He stopped scrubbing.

"In the Back Market. By the bend behind the freight sheds. She had on a coat I ain't never seen before in my life, Kai. Long. Fitted. And a bag." She shook her head. "Expensive. Like Ironcross-expensive."

"Asha?"

"Asha."

"Where she get money for a coat like that?"

Isola picked up the next plate and didn't answer, and he watched her not answer. He'd been watching her not answer things his whole life. He was getting good at reading it.

"So what happened?" he said. "You just let her walk off?"

"I didn't *let* her do nothing. Some man backed into me and knocked the salt out my hand, and by the time I got done cussing him out, she was gone."

"What man?"

"Some Shadowmere man." She put the plate on the stack harder than she meant to. "Tall. Like, *tall* tall. Somebody had beat his face in recently, he had a busted lip and his eye all swole, and his hand was wrapped. Locs. Beard. Standing there in the rain letting it run all in his beard like he ain't have the sense to wipe it."

Micah had stopped washing.

"And he had a girl with him," Isola went on. "Behind him. Hiding. Red hair. And he paid Pell four Veils for my salt like it was nothing. *Four*. Pell charged me eighteen. And he looked at me like—"

She stopped.

Micah was looking at her with a slow, terrible smile spreading over his whole face.

"Like what?" he said.

"Like nothing. He was rude."

"Why you know what his beard look like?"

"Because it was *in front of me*, Micah."

"Pell call him anything?"

Isola thought about it. "Graves."

Micah dropped the sponge in the water and laughed so hard he had to hold onto the counter.

Isola waited. She folded her arms. She waited some more. He didn't stop. He laughed until he was wheezing, and Selah called "What's so funny?" from the back room, and Micah called back "Nothing, Mama!" in a voice that was still cracking, and went right back to laughing.

"I'm glad you think this is funny," Isola said.

"Isola." He wiped his eyes with his wet wrist. "Isola. That's *Silas*."

"Who?"

"Silas Graves. Jude's brother." He waited. She gave him nothing. "Dante's *son*. My godfather's son. Isola, come on."

She knew who Dante was. Everybody in this house knew who Dante was. He'd stood up in front of a whole room of witnesses and spoken Micah's name, and Asha's, and every year on their name days a package showed up on the step with no note and no return route on it. But Dante's world was Micah's world, and Asha's. Isola had Rae. She'd never gone to the godfamily dinners, and she'd never once wanted to.

"I never met Dante's kids," she said.

"Well, you met one today." Micah picked the sponge back up. He was still grinning. "Jude bring me to Gutterlight sometimes. You know what they call Silas at the Ring?"

"Graves, apparently."

"They call him Graves 'cause of what happens to the other man." Micah said it light, the way he said everything. "He fights for Rae sometimes. When somebody did something bad enough that it needs settling. Last night was Jerrick. Lowtorch man. The boards up here took more money on that fight than anything all month."

Isola stopped drying.

"He killed somebody," she said. "Last night."

"In the Ring. It's legal." Micah thought about it. "Mostly."

She thought about the man in the lane. The swelling under his eye. The split in his lip. The bandage across his knuckles. *Somebody had hit him recently.* Somebody had. And then that somebody had died.

And then he'd stood in the rain and paid for her salt.

"You let Silas Graves pay for your salt," Micah said, delighted. "Isola. You know how many girls in Sixth Ward would throw themselves in front of Silas Graves on purpose?"

"I didn't *let* him do nothing. I told him I ain't want his money."

"You told him *no*?" Micah started laughing again. "Oh, I gotta tell Jude."

"You're not telling Jude nothing."

"I'm telling Jude everything."

She snapped the towel at the back of his legs. He yelped and laughed harder. She snapped it again. Somewhere behind them Selah said "Y'all break one of my plates, I'm breaking something back," and they both shut up and finished the dishes in a silence so full of Micah trying not to laugh that Isola wanted to drown him in the rinse water.

She dried the last cup and hung the towel on the oven door.

*He killed somebody last night.* She'd felt it in her stomach when Micah said it, a cold little drop. That was the right reaction. That was what a sensible woman was supposed to feel about a man like that.

She was also still thinking about the rain in his beard.

That was not the right reaction. She hung the towel up a second time, straighter, like that would fix it.

---

Asha came home after final bell.

Isola was waiting for her. She'd sat down in the dark front room in Omari's chair at quarter till and hadn't moved since. The house was asleep around her. Tevin snoring through the spare-room door. Micah's light finally off. Selah's humming had gone quiet an hour ago, and Omari's door had stayed shut.

The lock turned.

Asha came in slow, the way you came into a house when you were trying not to wake anybody, and closed the door behind her with her hip. She wasn't wearing the coat. She was in her old brown one, the one with the patched elbow, and she didn't have the tote. Wherever she'd been, she'd changed back before she came up the stairs.

She bent down to unlace her boots.

"You missed dinner," Isola said.

Asha jumped so hard she hit her head on the coat hooks.

"*Damn*, Isola." She held her head. "Why are you sitting in the dark like Daddy?"

Isola didn't answer. She reached over and turned on the lamp.

Asha looked bad. Not hurt. Tired in a way Isola didn't recognize on her, like she'd been holding her breath all day and only just let it out on the stairs. Her eyes were bloodshot. Her hair was pulled up in a wrap that had started to slip. And she'd shoved her sleeves back to unlace the boots, and on the cuff of her right shirt sleeve, there was a smear of something bright.

Silver. Wet-looking. It caught the lamp and threw it back.

Quicksilver.

Isola knew it the same way she'd known funeral grade by feel before she knew it by name. She'd been staring at quicksilver on the face of a coin all day.

"Mama saved you a plate," she said.

"I ate."

"Where?"

"Out."

"Out where?"

"*Out*, Isola." Asha yanked the second boot off and stood up. She pulled her sleeves back down while she did it, both of them, fast, and Isola watched her do it. "What are you, the Synod now? You gonna write me up?"

"I'm asking where you been all day."

"Running errands."

"For who?"

"For me." Asha picked up her boots and held them against her chest. "Some of us got lives outside this house. Some of us don't want to sit up in the dark waiting on everybody else to come home so we can count 'em."

That landed. It was supposed to. Isola let it sit right where it hit and didn't flinch.

"What's on your cuff?" she said.

Asha didn't look down. That was how Isola knew she already knew what was on her cuff.

"Paint."

"Paint."

"Yes, paint. I helped somebody paint a sign." Asha moved toward the hall. "Goodnight, Isola."

"Asha."

Her cousin stopped at the mouth of the hall with her back to the lamp.

Isola could've said it right then. *I found the coin. I know what it is. A woman in the Back Market wouldn't even touch it.* She had the whole thing lined up. She could feel the coin in her skirt pocket, heavy against her hip.

She thought about the five bags.

"Mama saved you a plate," she said again.

Asha's shoulders came down half an inch. "I'll eat it in the morning."

She went down the hall. Her door opened and closed. Isola sat in Omari's chair with the lamp on and listened.

For about a minute, there was nothing.

After that, she heard the bag.

She knew which bag. She heard the side zip go, slow, the way you'd unzip something in a house full of sleeping people. A pause. A rustle, then a longer rustle, then fabric, then a sound like somebody's hand going all the way into a torn lining and finding nothing there.

Silence.

Another zip. A different bag. Faster this time. Another. Things hitting the floor. Isola sat completely still and counted five bags in the dark, and after the fifth one, the room went quiet, and stayed quiet, and didn't make another sound.

Asha didn't come out.

Isola didn't go in.

She sat there long enough for the lamp to start ticking as it heated, and she thought about Selah at the counter this morning with cold coming off her skin. *Whatever it is, don't go looking.* She thought about the assayer shutting her scale case with two fingers.

She got up and went to the name wall.

---

The wall looked different at night. In the daytime it was just the wall. Four generations of names in handwritten chains, the portrait of her great-grandmother crooked over the middle, Micah's folded packet tucked behind his strip. At night, with only the lamp, the funeral thread running through every name caught the light like veins.

Asha's strip was on the left side, below Tevin's. Asha Noelle Wraithmoor, in Omari's handwriting, with the block mark and her godbinding to Dante stitched in black under it.

Isola put two fingers against the metal.

She'd done this a thousand times. She'd done it this morning for Micah. Omari had taught all of them the cadence when they were small enough to stand on a chair to reach their own names, and he'd made them practice until they could say it in their sleep. Low. Even. Not loud. You didn't shout at a name. You reminded it whose it was.

"Asha Noelle Wraithmoor," she said.

Nothing came back.

No warmth under her fingertips. No pressure from the other side of the metal. The funeral thread running through Asha's name didn't pull taut the way Micah's had. It did the opposite. Under her fingers, she felt it give, like a rope somebody had been holding on the other end and just let go of.

Isola took her hand off the wall.

She looked at the thread. In the lamp light, it was hanging. Not broken. Just slack, curved down between the stitches like a clothesline with nothing on it.

She put her fingers back on it and said the name again, lower, slower, exactly how Omari taught her. "Asha Noelle Wraithmoor."

The thread sagged a little more.

Her stomach dropped so hard she had to put her other hand on the wall to stay upright.

Omari had told them once. Only once, when Isola was twelve and Danika was getting ready to move to Ironcross, and Isola had asked if Danika's name would still answer from that far away. He'd sat her down on the couch and taken her hand and told her that distance didn't matter. Ironcross didn't matter. You could go to the bottom of Nightroot and your name would still answer on this wall, because a Wraithmoor name held on to you whether you were in the next room or the next tier.

The only thing that made a thread go slack, he said, was if you were letting go of it. If you were walking out of the name on purpose.

Isola had asked, *Why would anybody do that?*

And Omari had looked at the wall for a long time and said, *Pray you never find out, baby.*

She stood in the front room in the dark and stared at Asha's strip until her eyes burned.

Five bags. Two years. A coat she couldn't afford and a bag to match. A coin built to lie.

Asha wasn't running errands. Asha was leaving.

---

Asha's boots were by the hall door where she'd dropped them on her way out of the front room.

She'd left them. In all that tearing through the bags, she hadn't come back for the boots, and Isola looked at them for a full minute before she let herself pick one up.

The right one. Asha always shoved things in the right one. Rings, notes, money. She'd done it since they were kids, ever since Tevin found her stash of sweets in her pillowcase and ate every one.

The insole lifted at the heel. Under it, somebody had cut a slot in the sole, and in the slot was a strip of something black and stiff, folded twice.

Isola pulled it out and unfolded it under the lamp.

It was a route strip. Black, with a blue cord stitched through one end, and writing on it in white ink that looked like it had been stamped instead of written. Six boxes ran along the top. All six had been punched through. Under the boxes, a number: *180.*

And under the number, in smaller white letters:

*Name clears when balance closes.*

Isola read it three times. The words didn't change.

Under that, a line by itself, stamped fresher than the rest, the ink still sharp.

*Next: final bell. Blue Circle.*

She knew Blue Circle. Everybody in Ashvein knew the name the way you knew the name of a disease. A den in Shadowmere, under an old bathhouse somewhere near Gutterlight, where people went to buy Hush and didn't come out the same. Tevin had a friend who went in there once and came out not able to remember his own mama's face for a week.

Six deliveries. A hundred and eighty Veils. And at the end of it, a new name.

Asha wasn't just leaving the house.

She was buying her way out of being a Wraithmoor.

Isola held the strip in both hands and stood perfectly still in the middle of the front room, because if she moved, she was going to walk down that hall and kick Asha's door in, and the whole house would wake up, and Omari would lock everything down, and Asha would be gone before morning with a slack thread and five bags and a name nobody on this wall had given her.

So she didn't move.

She memorized it. Every line. *Name clears when balance closes. Next: final bell. Blue Circle.* She folded the strip back exactly along its creases and slid it into the slot in the sole. She pressed the insole down and set the boot back next to the other one, exactly where Asha had dropped it, toes turned in, the left lace trailing.

Down the hall, the back-room latch clicked.

Isola froze.

Omari's door didn't open. She stood with her hand still on the boot and waited, and it didn't open, and after a long moment the latch clicked again, softer, closing.

She didn't know how long he'd been listening. She didn't know if he'd heard her say Asha's name at the wall, or if he'd heard the bags, or if he'd just heard his daughter standing in the dark and wanted her to know he was awake.

That was the thing about Omari. He let you make your choice first.

Isola turned off the lamp and went to bed, and she didn't sleep. She lay on her back and listened to Asha not making a single sound on the other side of the wall, and she counted the hours until final bell.
