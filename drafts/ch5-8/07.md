# CHAPTER 7: SILAS

Remy knocked on the dark house at first bell carrying enough food for six people and wearing a green coat you could see from the Ring.

Silas opened the door with his knife already in his good hand. He'd slept maybe forty minutes, sitting up on the cot with his back to the wall, and every one of those minutes had been about Shrouds.

Remy looked at the knife. He looked at the mitten of cloth on Silas's other hand. He looked at the slag running down the back-room door at the end of the hall, which you could see from the front step if you knew where to look, and Remy always knew where to look.

"Wow," he said. "Y'all had a whole night."

"Get in here."

Remy came in sideways with a sack in each arm and a messenger bag across his chest. His locs were tied back off his face, and the silver cuff on his ear caught what little light the front room had. He set the sacks on the cold stove and started unloading like he was in his mama's kitchen. Fried dough wrapped in paper, still hot enough to steam. Boiled eggs. A jar of something orange. Two flasks of tea and one of coffee, because Remy knew Silas wouldn't drink tea unless he was dying, and even then he'd complain.

"How'd you get past Lorraine?" Silas said.

"I didn't get past Lorraine. Lorraine gave me the address." Remy unscrewed the coffee and handed it over. "She told me to tell you she counted six watchers on the front of the Mercer building this morning instead of two, and she wants to know what you did."

"I didn't do nothing."

"That's what I told her. She laughed at me." Remy looked down the hall. "Where is she?"

"Back room."

"She sleep?"

"I don't know. She burned through the lock and the service door last night and went out in the street looking for sky. Shrouds came down Lamp Street under Silence and almost walked right into her."

Remy stopped unwrapping the dough.

"I got her out," Silas said.

Remy's eyes went to the mitten. "How?"

"How you think?"

Remy didn't say anything for a second. When he did, it was quiet. "She let you?"

"No."

"Damn, Si."

"I know."

"No, I mean—" Remy shook his head and went back to the food. "Nothing. Eat something before you fall over. You look like Jerrick won."

Silas ate a piece of dough standing up. It was the best thing he'd put in his mouth in two days, and he'd die before he told Remy that.

---

Melena came out about ten minutes later.

She was still in the mourning dress, but she'd pulled the last of the pins out, and her hair hung loose and wild down her back. She'd slept in Rhea's boots, from the look of it. She had the letter in her hand.

She stopped in the mouth of the hall when she saw Remy. Her whole body went careful.

Remy didn't stand up. He'd sat down on the floor by the stove with his back against the wall and his knees up, a boiled egg in one hand, and he stayed exactly where he was. He smiled at her, easy, like he'd known her for years.

"Morning," he said. "I'm Remy. I brought food. I don't know what you like, so I brought everything."

Melena looked at Silas.

"He's with me," Silas said. "He's safe."

"*With me*," Remy repeated. "Listen at him. Like I'm a dog he walk."

"You eat like one."

"Your mama says I eat like a growing boy."

"You're grown."

"And still growing, Silas, look at me." He held out the egg toward Melena at arm's length, without getting up. "Egg? They're boiled. I don't know how to cook them any other way, my mama gave up on me."

Melena didn't take the egg. She didn't leave, either. She lowered herself onto the very end of the sofa, as far from both of them as the room allowed, with the letter flat on her knee and her other hand pressed on top of it.

Silas watched Remy watch her.

He'd known Remy since they were six. He knew every face Remy owned. The face he used to talk Bookkeepers out of their money and the face he used on Camille when he wanted seconds and the face he used at burials, which was the realest one. He'd never seen this one before. Remy was looking at Melena like somebody had handed him a lit lamp and told him not to drop it.

*Oh no,* Silas thought.

He drank his coffee.

Melena ate half a piece of dough, slowly, without looking at either of them. Remy talked. Remy could talk to a wall and make the wall feel interesting. He talked about the tram line and the price of eggs in Gutterlight versus eggs in the Back Market and how Silas had once tried to boil an egg in a kettle and set off every ward in Camille's kitchen. Melena didn't laugh. Once, at the kettle part, the corner of her mouth moved.

Remy saw it. He didn't make a big deal of it. He just kept talking.

She'd been unfolding the letter while he went. Silas didn't think she'd noticed she was doing it. Her fingers had found the creases on their own and opened it a fold at a time, and now it lay open across her knee, three pages, the looping slanted handwriting he'd seen last night.

She read a line. And another.

The room got warm.

Silas set his coffee down.

It came on faster than it had in Dante's office. Faster than on Lamp Street. One second Melena was reading, and the next her breath had gone short and jagged and the black was climbing her fingers like ink spilled on a page, and the letter between them had started to brown at the edges. The air in the front room went thick. The thread Camille had laid across the doorframe last night started to hum, high and thin, like a kettle about to go.

Silas came off the wall.

"Don't," Melena said.

He stopped where he was.

Her eyes were wet and wide and furious. "Don't touch me."

"I won't." He held up both hands, the good one and the mitten. "But you gotta put the letter down."

"I *can't*." It came out of her like something tearing. "It's the last thing she— I can't put it down, I can't—"

The pages started to smoke.

Silas didn't know what to do. That was the plain truth of it. He knew what to do with a man coming at him with a knife. He knew what to do with a runner who'd been beaten half to death, with a body that needed carrying, with the dead that sometimes wanted things from him. He didn't know what to do with a girl on a sofa who was about to burn up the last words her mother ever wrote because she couldn't stop holding them, and he couldn't touch her, and he couldn't make her let go.

Remy reached into his coat.

He didn't stand up. He didn't move closer. He came out with one of his mints, the cheap kind that came in silver wrappers, and he held it up between two fingers, out in front of him, where she could see it.

"Hey," he said. "Melena. What color's this?"

She stared at him like he'd lost his mind.

"Humor me," Remy said. "What color?"

Her breath hitched. "Silver."

"Good. What color's my coat?"

"I don't—"

"You know. Look at it."

Her eyes went to his coat. "Green."

"Ugly green. You can say it, everybody does. What color is Silas's shirt?"

"Black."

"Black. Good. Where are you?"

"I don't know where I am. Shadowmere."

"Close enough. What room?"

"A house. A dark house. It has no windows in the back."

"That's right. Who's in the room with you?"

"You." Her voice was shaking but slower. "And him."

"Me and him. That's it. Nobody else." Remy set the mint on the floor between them and left it there. "Put your heels down for me. Both of them. Flat on the floor. Press."

She did. Silas watched her feet in Rhea's boots press down into the boards.

Remy started tapping on the floor with two fingers. It wasn't even. *Tap-tap. Tap. Tap-tap-tap.* Six beats, uneven, and then the same six again. He didn't explain it. He just did it, steady, like a heartbeat that couldn't make up its mind.

"Breathe with that," he said. "Out longer than in."

Melena breathed.

The black on her fingers stopped climbing.

"Can I hold the letter for you?" Remy said. "Just till your hands cool off. I won't read it. I'll hold it face down. You can watch me the whole time."

She looked at him for a long moment.

She held it out. Not to him, not close, just out, at arm's length, and Remy got up onto his knees and reached across the gap and took it by the very corner, careful as you'd take something from a sleeping baby, and laid it face down on his own knee and put his hand flat on top of it.

"There," he said. "It's right here. Ain't going nowhere."

He kept tapping with his other hand.

It took about four minutes. Silas counted. Four minutes of colors and heels and six uneven beats, and the black backed down off her fingers to her nails and stayed there. The thread on the doorframe went quiet. The room went back to being cold and damp and smelling like fried dough. Melena sat on the end of the sofa breathing out long, and her hands lay open in her lap, shaking, normal colored.

Silas had stood outside that door all night. He'd chased her down a street and pinned her hands to his chest until his palm blistered. He'd been taking care of people for half his life.

Remy had done it with a mint.

Silas didn't know what he felt about that. Grateful, mostly. Something under the grateful that he didn't feel like looking at. He picked his coffee back up and drank it and let it stay under there.

"Where'd you learn that?" Melena asked.

"My mama." Remy was still sitting with his hand on the letter. "She had nerves bad when I was little. That's what the healer taught her. Colors, where you are, who's there, feet down. The tapping's mine, though. I just do that."

"Why six?"

"No reason. I can't count to seven."

Her mouth moved again. Almost a smile. Gone in a second.

"Your mama," she said. "Does she still—"

"Every now and then. She prays mostly now. She thinks prayer can stop anything." Remy shrugged. "Sometimes it does."

Melena looked at the letter under his hand for a long time.

"I want it back," she said.

"Your hands cool?"

She held them up. She looked at them herself, turned them over, front and back. "Yes."

He held it out to her by the same corner. She took it. She didn't open it. She folded it once and put it inside the bodice of the mourning dress, against her chest, and pressed her hand flat over it.

---

They ate after that. All three of them. Melena ate two pieces of dough and a whole egg and drank half a flask of tea, and when she finished, she looked surprised at herself, like her body had done it without asking.

"So," Remy said, wiping his hands on his coat. "That's gonna keep happening."

"Yes," Melena said.

"Every time you think about her?"

She hesitated. "Not every time. When it gets—" She pressed her hand to her chest. "When it gets too big."

"And then it comes out your hands."

"Yes."

"Did it do this before?" Silas said. "Before your mother?"

Melena looked at him. For a second he thought she was going to tell him to ask her something else.

"A little," she said. "When I was young. Small things. Candles that wouldn't go out. A curtain once. My father taught me how to—"

She lifted her hands. They came up in front of her in a shape Silas had never seen before. Palms down, fingers curved, wrists turned out, like she was holding a bowl of water upside down and trying not to spill it. It was precise. It looked like something she'd done a thousand times, something somebody had stood behind her and fixed until she got it right.

She stopped. She looked at her own hands in the air like they'd betrayed her.

She put them back in her lap.

"It doesn't matter," she said. "It doesn't work anymore. It stopped working when she died."

Silas thought about *My dearest Elias* at the top of a letter from a dead woman. He thought about a father who'd taught his daughter to hold her hands like that.

He didn't ask. She'd told him to ask her something else, and he'd heard her.

Remy was looking at Silas over her head. Remy had seen the hands too. He didn't ask either.

"Okay," Remy said. "So it's tied to how you feel. It gets big when the feelings get big."

"I suppose."

"So what you need is something that turns the feelings down."

Silas knew where this was going before Remy got there. "No."

"I ain't even said it yet."

"You were about to say Hush."

"I was about to say Hush," Remy agreed. "Hear me out."

"That shit's poison, Remy."

"In the wrong amount. Everything's poison in the wrong amount. Your mama's stew is poison in the wrong amount, I've seen it." Remy leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. "Look. I know what Hush does. I know *exactly* what Hush does. It turns everything down. Grief, fear, all of it. People go into the dens to get one hour where nobody dead can touch them, and the den sells it to them by the drop, and then it charges them for it later in ways they don't see coming." He said it flat, the way you'd say something you'd watched happen. "That's what it does if you let it."

Melena was watching him.

"But one drop," Remy said. "Measured. In tea. With somebody who knows what they're doing standing right there. One drop might turn it down just enough to stop the fire before it starts. We test it. We watch her. If it works, we know what we're dealing with. If it doesn't, we know that too."

"And if it makes it worse?" Silas said.

"We'll be standing right there."

"In a Hush den."

"In Ora's den." Remy said the name like it settled something. "She measures clean. She doesn't cut it. And she owes me."

"Everybody owes you."

"That's 'cause I'm nice to people, Silas, you should try it." Remy looked at Melena. "It's your call. Not his. Not mine."

Silas looked at Melena too.

She'd gone quiet. Her hand was flat over the letter under her dress. She was looking at the slag on the back-room door down the hall, the brass tear running down the wood, the black line where Camille had sewn the service door shut again last night.

"Your mother said the ward won't hold a week," she said to Silas.

"She said that."

"And if I do that again—" She nodded at the door. "Out there. Somewhere with people."

"People get hurt."

"People get hurt," she agreed. She looked at Remy. "One drop?"

"One drop."

"And if I want to stop?"

"We stop. You don't owe nobody nothing."

She sat with it. Silas watched her sit with it, and he thought about everything Camille had said last night about what was in this girl, how it didn't answer to death or Graves thread or anything his family knew how to hold. He thought about Dante's face if he ever found out his son had walked a girl the Synod was hunting into a Hush den.

And he thought about Lamp Street. Her hands glowing through his coat. The Shroud turning its head.

He didn't have a better plan. He'd been up all night and he didn't have one.

"Tonight," Melena said. "Can it be tonight?"

Remy looked at Silas. Silas looked at the ceiling.

"Dante can't know," Silas said.

"Dante ain't gonna know," Remy said. "Nobody's gonna know. We go in, we come out. In and out." He was already getting up, brushing crumbs off his coat. "I'll go see Ora this afternoon. Set it up. Y'all stay here and don't burn nothing."

"We'll try," Melena said, and that time she actually smiled. Small and quick, like it surprised her, and Remy looked at it like he'd been handed the lamp all over again.

*Oh, no,* Silas thought again.

He walked Remy to the door. On the step, in the rain, Remy turned around and lowered his voice.

"One more thing."

"What?"

"Ora takes anything as payment. And I mean *anything*. Money, favors, teeth, memories, your mama's recipes." Remy glanced back through the door at Melena, still sitting on the end of the sofa with her hand over her heart. "Don't let her pay for nothing. Not one thing. Whatever it costs, it goes on me."

"Why?"

"'Cause I know how Ora collects." Remy pulled his collar up against the rain. "Lock the door."

He left. Silas locked the door and turned around.

---

The afternoon took about a year.

Silas soaked his shoulder in the bathroom sink with water he heated on a camp coil he found under it, which was about as much good as talking to it. Melena sat on the sofa. He sat on the cot. The Ring roared behind the rooftops every now and then, and the rain kept coming, and neither of them said anything for most of an hour.

His phone buzzed once.

Jude: Token's not real.

Silas sat up.

Silas: How not real

Jude: Real good. Not real. Somebody did work on it.

Silas: Who

Jude: Working on it. Mind your business.

Silas looked at that for a long time. A fake Dawnveil token good enough to fool a Bookkeeper, sitting in a dead man's locker, and a fifteen-year-old still missing behind it. Somebody had gone to a lot of trouble to sell three kids down a route. He put the phone face down on his knee.

"Who's Jerrick?" Melena said.

He looked up. She was watching him from the sofa.

"Remy said I looked like Jerrick won," Silas said. "That what you mean?"

"Yes."

"Man I killed two nights ago."

She didn't flinch. He'd give her that. She looked at him for a while, at the swelling under his eye and the split in his lip, and at the bandage on his right hand under the mitten on his left.

"Do you always say it like that?" she asked.

"Like what?"

"Like you're telling me it's raining."

He thought about it. "It's a job. He sold a route with three kids on it. One's dead. One made it. One nobody can find." He shrugged with his good shoulder. "Somebody had to answer for that. Rae picked me."

"And you just do it."

"I do it clean."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's what I got."

She was quiet. He thought she'd go back to staring at the slag on the door. Instead she pulled her feet up under the skirt of the mourning dress and wrapped her arms around her knees, and for a second she looked about fifteen.

"Are you afraid of me?" she said.

"No."

"You should be. Your mother is."

"My mother ain't afraid of nothing. She's careful." He leaned his head back against the wall. "There's a difference."

"Is there?"

"Careful means she's paying attention." He closed his eyes. "Afraid means she'd have left you on the street."

When he opened them again, she was still looking at him. Something in her face had changed. He didn't know what and didn't try to figure it out.

She looked down at her lap. She was running one finger along the collar of her dress, where a row of dark stones was sewn into the silk, too fine for anything in Gutterlight. She was counting them. Silas watched her lips move.

She looked up and caught him watching, and her hand dropped into her lap.
