# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Isola had never asked anybody for help in her life, and she'd been practicing how to do it since four in the morning.

She'd practiced in bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to Asha not make a sound on the other side of the wall. She'd practiced in the kitchen, where Selah was already up at the stove before first bell, humming her six notes into a pot of grits and not asking where Isola was going with her coat on. She'd practiced all the way down the cliff stairs in the dark, and across the chasm bridge with Rae's token in her fist, and through the back lanes of Shadowmere with the coin riding her hip like a stone in her shoe.

*Remy, I need you to—*

No.

*Remy, I got a problem, and I don't—*

Absolutely not. She sounded like Micah asking for money.

By the time she got to the bottom of Rae's stairs, she'd decided she was just going to walk in and put the coin on the table and let it talk for her. Coins talked. This one had made an assayer close up shop in front of witnesses. It could handle one godbrother.

Mother Rae's house sat behind a chandlery on the east lanes, at the top of a crooked iron stair that rang under your boots no matter how soft you walked. Rae liked it that way. Nobody came up those stairs without her knowing, and anybody who tried to come up quiet got noticed twice. There were bells over the door, three of them, small and silver like the ones on Rae's wrist. Isola had been coming up these stairs since she was five years old, and she'd never once heard those bells ring.

The door wasn't locked. It never was. Isola let herself in.

The front room smelled like candle wax and salt and the bitter orange peel Rae burned in a dish by the window when she wanted the air to behave. Everything was where it always was. The black velvet chair nobody sat in but Rae. The shelves of jars with no labels. The long table under the window where Isola had done her letters as a girl while Rae worked a route map on the other end of it and never once explained what she was doing.

The house was dark except for the kitchen.

Somebody was in the kitchen singing. Badly. Very badly. Off key, too loud, and wrong words to a song Isola had heard on every tram speaker in Shadowmere last summer.

She stood in the doorway and listened for a second, because some things in life were too good to interrupt.

Remy was at the stove with his back to her. He had his locs tied up on top of his head, a dish towel over one shoulder, and his green coat thrown over a chair, and he was frying dough in a pan of oil like it had personally offended him. There were already three paper sacks on the counter. A pot of eggs was boiling over on the back burner. He hit a high note so hard and so wrong that a cat somewhere outside started yelling back.

"That ain't how it goes," Isola said.

Remy jumped so high he almost put his hand in the oil.

"*Shit*—" He spun around with a fork held up like a knife. When he saw her, he put it down and slapped the dish towel against his own chest. "General. Damn. You can't be walking up on folks like that."

"I came up the stairs like a normal person."

"You came up the stairs like a *Shroud*. Ma's bells ain't even move." He looked at the clock over the door, then back at her. "It ain't even first bell. What you doing down here?"

Isola opened her mouth to say it. All four hours of practice.

"You burning your dough," she said.

He swore again and turned around.

---

He made her sit down. That was the thing about Remy. You couldn't come into his kitchen without him feeding you, and if you tried to say no, he'd put it in front of you anyway and stand there until you ate it out of embarrassment.

He put a plate of fried dough in front of her with honey on the side, and a boiled egg, and a cup of tea he'd made without asking how she took it because he already knew. Two sugars. No milk. He'd known since she was eight.

"Eat," he said, and went back to the stove.

She ate. It was good. It was always good. Remy had been cooking in this kitchen since he was tall enough to see over the stove, mostly because Rae would forget to eat for two days at a time if nobody put food in front of her, and somebody had to.

Isola watched him work. He was loading the paper sacks. Dough in one. Eggs in another. Two flasks, tea and coffee. A jar of something orange. Enough food for at least four people, and he was packing it like he was about to walk out the door with it.

"Who all that for?" she asked.

"Friends."

"At first bell?"

"My friends are early risers."

"Your friends are drunks."

"Some of 'em." He grinned at her over his shoulder. "Some of 'em just had a long night."

She looked at him harder. He looked tired. Not regular tired. He had on yesterday's shirt, and there was a smudge of something grey on his collar, and the skin under his eyes had gone dark like it did when he hadn't slept. He kept touching the ward-thread bracelet on his left wrist, turning it, the way he'd done since he was a boy whenever something was eating at him.

"Where's your mama?" Isola asked.

"Out."

"Out where?"

"Working." He put the lid on the eggs. "She got called last night. Late."

"By who?"

"Isola." He turned around and leaned back against the counter with his arms crossed. "Did you come all the way down here before first bell to ask me where my mama is? Because I can tell you right now, I don't know half the time either."

He was right. She didn't know why she'd asked. She'd asked because it was easier than the other thing.

She put her hand in her skirt.

Remy watched her do it. He watched her hand stay in there. His face changed a little, the grin going somewhere, and he didn't say anything. He just waited.

He'd always been good at that. When Danika left for Ironcross three years ago, Isola had come down here and sat at that long table for six hours without saying a single word, Remy had sat across from her the whole time doing his homework. He didn't ask what was wrong. Every hour or so, he slid a mint across the table to her in a little silver wrapper. By the end of the day she had a pile of six, and she'd finally said, *She didn't even say goodbye to me.* And he'd said, *I know,* and kept doing his homework.

Isola took the coin out of her pocket and set it on the table.

---

Remy didn't move for a long time.

He looked at it from where he stood. Dark silver. Black center. The Veiled One's face, and the prayer around the edge. He didn't come closer to look. He didn't need to. Remy had grown up in Shadowmere in a house where routes got opened and closed on the kitchen table. He'd seen more Veils than Isola had seen in her life.

"Where'd you get that?" he said quietly.

"Tilt it."

He came to the table. He didn't pick it up. He bent down and looked at it sideways, with his face close to the wood, the way you'd look at a bug you weren't sure was dead. The quicksilver flashed near one corner and died before it crossed.

He straightened up.

"Isola," he said. "Where did you get that."

"I took it to the assayer in the Back Market yesterday." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter. "The one under the arch. She looked at it and shut her scale case and said she don't weigh High Heresy. In front of everybody. She told me to get it off her counter."

Remy sat down across from her. Slowly. He put both hands flat on the table on either side of his plate.

"High Heresy," he said.

"High Heresy."

"Isola. You know what that—"

"I know what it means, Remy. I was there." Her voice came out harder than she wanted. "Mama held my shoulder so I couldn't turn around. I watched her fingers on the stones. I know exactly what it means."

He didn't say anything. He just looked at her, and she couldn't stand it, so she looked at the coin instead.

"It's not mine," she said.

"I didn't think it was."

"I found it."

"Where?"

She'd practiced this part too. She'd practiced it the most. *I found it in the street. I found it at the market. Somebody paid me with it.* She'd had a whole list of lies, good ones, ones that would hold up, and she sat there at Rae's long table with the honey going cold on her plate and couldn't get a single one out of her mouth.

"Asha's bag," she said.

Remy closed his eyes.

---

She told him all of it. She hadn't planned to. It just came out of her once she'd started, like it had been sitting in her chest all night waiting for somebody to open the door.

She told him about the five bags against the wall. Packed. Buckled. Two years. She told him about the torn pocket and the coin rolling under the desk, and Micah's courier coin ringing low and full next to it, and the counterfeit ringing flat. She told him about putting it back, and taking it back out, and deciding she'd rather carry it than leave it under Omari's roof. She told him about twelve-oh-four and no Asha. And then Shadowmere, and the assayer, and Pell.

She told him about Asha at the bend in a coat Isola couldn't afford with three months of courier money, and a bag to match, standing like somebody had told her exactly where to wait.

"And then she was gone," Isola said. "I looked everywhere. I walked the whole back wall of Gutterlight. She was just gone."

"Did she see you?"

"I don't think so."

"Did she come home last night?"

"Sometime after three. I heard the door. She didn't come out of her room this morning." Isola pressed her thumbs against her eyes. "I didn't knock. I stood there with my hand up and I didn't knock, Remy. I didn't know what I'd say if she opened it."

"What would you have said?"

"I don't know. That's the problem. I *always* know." She dropped her hands. "Since I can remember, I always know. Micah gets in trouble, I know what to do. Tevin wants to fight somebody, I know what to say. Mama gets one of her headaches, I know what she needs before she does. Daddy locks the doors twice, and I know which nights he's gonna do it three times." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And now my cousin's walking around with High Heresy in her bag and a coat that costs more than our rent, and I got nothing. I don't know what she's doing. I don't know who she's doing it for. I don't know if telling Daddy saves her or gets her Silenced. I don't *know*."

She stopped. Her face was hot.

Remy reached across the table and slid something toward her.

A mint. In a little silver wrapper.

Isola stared at it.

"You're so stupid," she said, and her voice cracked right down the middle.

"I know," Remy said.

She picked it up and held it in her fist and didn't cry, because she was a grown woman and she was not about to cry in her godmother's kitchen over a mint. She held it until the wrapper crinkled.

"Okay," she said finally. "Okay. So. What do I do?"

---

Remy didn't answer right away. He got up and poured more tea for both of them, and he took the burned dough off the stove and dumped it, and he stood at the window for a minute looking out at the lane. Isola let him. She'd known him long enough to know when he was thinking and when he was stalling, and this was thinking.

"You can't tell Omari," he said.

"I know."

"I mean it. Not yet. Your daddy finds that in his house, he's gonna do what your daddy does, and he's gonna lock everything down and go find whoever gave it to her. And then it's a Wraithmoor problem out loud, and everybody in Sixth Ward knows by second bell." He came back and sat down. "Whoever's paying Asha, they hear Omari Wraithmoor's asking questions, they're gonna cut her loose. And they ain't gonna cut her loose nice."

Isola's stomach turned over. "What does that mean?"

"You know what it means."

She did.

"And you can't tell my mama," Remy went on.

That surprised her. "Why not? She's the one who—"

"Because Ma's already got her hands full." He said it flat. He didn't look away from her, but he wasn't telling her the whole thing, and they both knew it. "And because the second Ma knows, it becomes Ma's business. And when it's Ma's business, it ain't yours anymore. You won't get to decide nothing."

Isola thought about that. Rae, who answered questions when she felt like it, which was roughly never. Rae, who'd handed Isola a token on a cord when she was fourteen and never once told her what the marks meant.

"Okay," Isola said slowly. "So who *can* I tell?"

"Me." Remy tapped the table. "You told me. That's it. That's the list."

"And what are *you* gonna do?"

"I'm gonna ask around."

"Ask who?"

"People who move paper." He picked up the coin for the first time. He held it by the edges and turned it in the light from the kitchen lamp, and his face did something careful. "This is good work, General. This ain't some kid in a back room pressing lead. Somebody spent real money making this look right. There's only so many places in Shadowmere that would touch something like this, and I know most of 'em."

"How do you know that?"

He gave her a look. "Who you think my mama is?"

Fair.

"And you'll tell me," Isola said. "Whatever you find out. You'll tell me first."

"I'll tell you first."

"Promise me."

"Isola."

"Remy. *Promise* me."

"I promise." He set the coin back down in front of her. "And you gotta promise me something back."

She knew what he was going to say before he said it.

"Don't go looking for her," Remy said. "Not by yourself. Not down here."

Selah had said the same thing yesterday morning with cold coming off her hands. *Whatever it is, don't go looking.* Isola had gone looking within six hours.

"I'll try," she said.

"That ain't a promise."

"It's the one I got."

He shook his head at her, but the corner of his mouth went up. "You sound like Si."

---

"Who's Si?" Isola asked.

"My best friend. You never met him?"

"I know you got a best friend named Si. I've heard you on the phone with him about four hundred times. You never brought him around."

"'Cause you'd eat him alive." Remy got up and started folding the tops of the paper sacks down. "And 'cause he don't come up to Ashvein much. He's a Shadowmere boy."

"What's he like?"

Remy thought about it. "Quiet. Mean when he needs to be. Fights at the Ring sometimes. He's the one who'd walk into a burning building and then complain about the smoke."

"Sounds like a lot."

"He is a lot." Remy grinned. "You'd hate him."

Isola picked up her tea. She was feeling better. Not good. But better than she'd felt since she found the coin, and she was surprised how much of that had to do with a kitchen and fried dough and somebody else carrying half of it for a minute.

"Speaking of Shadowmere boys," she said. "Some man knocked my salt out of my hand yesterday."

Remy paused with his hand on a sack. "At Pell's?"

"Right in front of Pell's. Backed straight into me like he ain't have eyes in the back of his head. Eighteen Veils of funeral grade, Remy. Right in the puddle." She shook her head. "And then he had the nerve to pay for another bag. Four Veils. Pell charged *me* eighteen and charged him four."

"Pell's a thief."

"Pell's a whole criminal." She took a sip of tea. "And this man. Lord. Tall. Like, I had to tip my head all the way back. Somebody had beat his face in. Busted lip, eye all swole up, hand wrapped. And he was just standing there in the rain letting it run all in his beard like he had nowhere to be."

Remy had stopped moving.

"Locs?" he said.

"Yeah."

"Big? Don't blink? Talks like he's being charged by the word?"

Isola put her tea down. "*Yes*. How you—"

"Did Pell call him anything?"

She remembered. *Price is the price, Graves.*

"Graves," she said.

Remy put both hands down on the counter and laughed so hard he had to bend over.

Isola sat there and waited. She folded her arms. She waited some more. He kept going. He laughed until he was wheezing, until he had to wipe his eyes with the dish towel, until the cat outside started yelling again.

"I'm glad you're having a good time," Isola said.

"Isola." He straightened up, still breathing hard. "Isola. That's *Si*."

She looked at him.

"That's Silas. Silas *Graves*. That's my best friend."

"No."

"*Yes*."

"No, it is not."

"He knocked your salt in the puddle? *Si* did?" Remy put the towel over his face and laughed into it. "Si don't knock into nobody. Si's the one everybody else knocks into and then apologizes to. He's like a wall. Oh, I'm never letting him live this down."

"You're not telling him nothing."

"I'm telling him *everything*."

"Remington."

"He paid for your salt and you *cussed him out*? Tell me you cussed him out."

"I told him I ain't want no debt tied to somebody in Shadowmere."

Remy made a sound like a balloon losing air. He sat down hard in the chair across from her. "And what'd he say?"

"He said the debt was his." Isola heard herself say it and didn't like how it sounded. Like she'd been thinking about it. Like she'd been thinking about it all night. "And then I told him to watch where he backing up, and he told me to stand somewhere else."

Remy stared at her across the table with his mouth open.

"What?" Isola said.

"Nothing."

"*What*, Remy?"

"Nothing. Nothing. You just said it like—" He waved his hand at her face. "You remember a whole lot about his beard, General."

"Because it was *in front of me*."

"Mm-hmm."

Isola threw her egg shell at him. He caught it and laughed.

But he wasn't laughing the same way anymore. She watched it change. It happened somewhere in the middle, while he was still smiling, the way a lamp flickers before it goes. His eyes went to the paper sacks on the counter, and then to the clock, and then back to her.

"Remy," she said. "Si killed somebody at the Ring the night before last. Didn't he."

He didn't answer right away. That was answer enough.

"Jerrick," Remy said finally. "Lowtorch man. He sold some kids down a route. Ma wanted it settled." He looked at her. "Si don't do it for fun, if that's what you're asking."

"I wasn't asking."

"You were thinking it."

She had been. She'd been thinking about a man who killed somebody one night and paid for her salt the next afternoon like it was nothing, and how she'd stood there looking at his face like a fool for fifteen steps while her cousin walked away. She'd been thinking she should feel worse about the first part than she did.

"There was a girl with him," Isola said. "Behind him. Against the freight wall."

Remy went still.

He went still like somebody had told him to. He didn't stop smiling, not exactly, but the smile stopped being attached to anything.

"A girl?" he said.

"Hooded. Small. Hiding." Isola watched his face. "She had red hair. Real red. Like a coal."

"Lotta redheads in Shadowmere," Remy said. "Si's always walking somebody somewhere. He does jobs for his daddy. Probably some client." He got up and went back to the sacks. "Don't worry about her."

One. Two. Three.

Isola could read a lie in three sentences. She'd been able to since she was nine, the same summer she learned to read Rae's handwriting on the order slips. Selah said she got it from Omari's side, and Omari said she got it from nowhere, she was just nosy. Either way, she could do it, and Remy had just said three sentences in a row to the paper sacks instead of to her face.

"Remington," she said.

"I gotta go." He hoisted two sacks into one arm and grabbed the third. "These people are waiting on breakfast."

"Who? Si?"

"Some of 'em." He shrugged his coat on one-handed, the ugly green one, and pulled the messenger bag over his head. "Finish your food. Lock the door when you leave. Don't put that coin anywhere but your pocket."

"Remy. Who is the girl?"

He stopped in the kitchen doorway. He looked back at her, and for a second he wasn't smiling at all. He looked like he had three years ago, sitting across that table, sliding her mints.

"Somebody who's having a worse week than you," he said. "And I can't tell you more than that. I'm sorry. I mean it."

She believed him. That was the worst part. He meant it.

"I'll find out about the coin," he said. "I promised. I'll come up to Ashvein tonight or tomorrow, and I'll tell you what I know. Just don't do nothing till then."

"I'll try."

"*General*."

"I said I'll try."

He sighed at the ceiling like he was asking God for patience, and God was taking His time. His phone buzzed in his coat. He shifted the sacks to get it out and looked at the screen, and his whole face did something complicated.

He turned the phone around so she could see it.

*Si: Where's breakfast*

Remy grinned at her, tired and terrible.

"Want me to tell him you said hey?"
