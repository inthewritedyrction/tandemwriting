# CHAPTER 6: ISOLA

Isola had never asked anybody for help in her life, and she'd been practicing how to do it since four in the morning.

She'd practiced in bed, staring at the ceiling, listening to Asha not make a sound on the other side of the wall. She'd practiced in the kitchen, where Selah was already at the stove before first bell, humming into a pot of grits and pointedly not asking where Isola was going with her coat on. She'd practiced down the cliff stairs in the dark, across the chasm bridge and through the back lanes of Shadowmere with the coin riding her hip like a rock in her shoe. Every version came out sounding like Micah asking for money.

By the time she reached the bottom of Rae's stairs, she'd given up. She'd walk in, put the coin on the table, and let it do the talking. It had already made an assayer shut her scale case in front of witnesses. It could handle her godbrother.

Mother Rae's house sat behind a chandlery on the east lanes, at the top of a crooked iron stair that rang under your boots no matter how soft you walked. Rae liked it that way. Three small silver bells hung over the door, the same kind she wore on her wrist, and in all the years Isola had been climbing these stairs, she'd never once heard them ring.

The door was unlocked, like always.

The front room smelled like candle wax, salt, and the bitter orange peel Rae burned in a dish by the window when she wanted the air to behave. Everything sat where it had sat for twenty years: the black velvet chair nobody touched but Rae, the shelves of unlabeled jars, the long table under the window where Isola had done her letters as a girl while Rae worked route maps at the other end and never explained a single line. The only light came from the kitchen, along with somebody singing a tram-speaker song from last summer, off key and with most of the words wrong.

Isola leaned in the kitchen doorway and let him finish the verse, because some things were too good to interrupt.

Remy stood at the stove with his locs piled on top of his head and a dish towel over one shoulder, frying dough like it had insulted his mama. His green coat hung off a chair. Three paper sacks were already lined up on the counter, and a pot of eggs was boiling over on the back burner. He went for a high note and missed it so badly that a cat somewhere outside yelled back.

"That ain't how it goes," Isola said.

Remy jumped so hard he nearly put his hand in the oil, spun around with a fork raised like a knife, and lowered it when he saw her face. "*General.*" He slapped the towel against his chest. "You can't be walking up on folks before first bell. Ma's bells ain't even move."

"I came up the stairs like a normal person." She pointed past him at the pan. "You burning your dough."

He swore and turned back around.

Remy didn't let anybody stand in his kitchen without feeding them, and arguing only meant he'd put the plate in front of you and stand over it until you ate out of embarrassment. Isola knew better. She sat down at the little kitchen table and let him set her up with fried dough, a dish of honey, a boiled egg, and tea he made without asking how she took it. Two sugars, no milk.

He went back to the stove while she ate, packing the sacks as fast as the dough came out of the oil. Eggs went in one, dough in another. Two flasks, one tea and one coffee. A jar of something orange. It was enough food for four people, and he was packing it like he planned to be out the door in ten minutes.

"Who's all that for at first bell?" she asked.

"Friends who had a long night." He tossed her a grin over his shoulder, but it didn't stay long.

He looked like he'd had one too. He was still in yesterday's shirt, there was a grey smudge on his collar, and the skin under his eyes had gone dark the way it did when he hadn't slept. He kept twisting the ward-thread bracelet on his left wrist, the same habit he'd had since he was a boy whenever something was eating at him.

Isola tore her dough in half. "Where's your mama?"

"Got called out last night. Late." He set the lid on the eggs and turned, leaning back against the counter with his arms folded. "And I don't know where, before you ask, so don't. Did you come all the way down here at this hour to check on Ma? Because she'd tell you herself she don't need it."

He was right, and she didn't know why she'd asked, except that it was easier than the other thing.

She slid her hand into her skirt pocket.

Remy watched and waited, the way he always had. When Danika left for Ironcross three years ago, Isola had come down here and sat at that long table in the front room for six hours without saying a word. Remy sat across from her doing his ledgers the whole time. Every hour or so, he slid a mint across the wood in its little silver wrapper. By the end of the day she had a pile of six. She finally said, *She didn't even say goodbye to me.* He said, *I know*, and kept writing.

Isola took the coin out and set it on the table between them.

He studied it from the counter first: dark silver, black center, the Veiled One's face with the prayer around the rim. Remy had grown up in a house where routes got opened and closed on the kitchen table. He'd handled more Veilmarks before he turned twelve than Isola had touched in her life.

"Tilt it," she said.

He came and crouched beside the table until his eyes were level with the wood and squinted at it sideways, like a bug he wasn't sure was dead. The quicksilver flashed bright at one corner and died before it crossed the face.

"Where'd you get this, Isola?" He stood up slowly.

"I took it to the assayer under the arch yesterday." She said it fast, like pulling a splinter out. "She took one look, closed her case, and told me she don't weigh High Heresy. In front of a whole charm-wire man and his customer. After that she told me to get it off her counter."

Remy pulled out the chair across from her and sat down without taking his eyes off the coin.

"You know what that means," he started, and she cut him off before he could finish.

"I was *there*, Remy." Isola heard her harden and didn't bother softening it. "I know exactly what it means. It's not mine. I found it."

Remy leaned in on his elbows. "Found it where?"

She'd practiced this part most of all. She had a whole list of lies, good ones: found it in the street, got paid with it at the market, somebody dropped it on the tram. She sat at Rae's kitchen table with the honey going cold and couldn't get a single one out of her mouth.

"Asha's bag," she said.

Remy closed his eyes.

She told him all of it. She hadn't planned to. Once she started, it came out of her like the faucet in the bathroom Tevin was supposed to fix three weeks ago. .

"And then she was gone," Isola said. "I walked the whole back wall of Gutterlight. Nothing."

Remy turned his tea cup in a slow circle on the table. "She see you?"

"I don't think so. She came in sometime after three this morning. I heard the door." Isola pressed her thumbs into her eyes. "I stood outside her room with my hand up to knock, and I didn't. I didn't know what I'd say if she opened it."

"What *would* you say?" He stopped turning the cup.

"I don't know." She dropped her hands to the table. "I always know. Micah gets in trouble, I know what to do. Tevin wants to fight half the east gate, I know what to say to sit him down. Mama gets one of her headaches, I know what she needs before she does. Daddy checks the locks twice, and I know which nights he's gonna do it three times." She laughed, and it came out wrong. "And now my cousin's walking around with High Heresy in her bag and I got nothing. I don't know who she's working for. I don't know if telling Daddy saves her or gets her Silenced. I don't *know*, Remy."

Her face was hot. She stared at the coin so she wouldn't have to look at him.

Remy reached into his shirt pocket and slid something across the table. A mint in a silver wrapper stopped against her plate.

"You're so stupid." Isola exhaled.

"I know." He nudged it an inch closer with one finger.

She picked it up and closed her fist around it, and she didn't cry, because she was a grown woman and she wasn't about to cry in her godmother's kitchen over a mint. She held it until the wrapper crinkled, then set it next to her tea and breathed out.

"Okay." She wiped under one eye with her knuckle. "So what do I do?"

Remy stood, dumped the burned dough in the scrap bucket, and refilled both their cups, then stood at the window with his back to her, looking down at the lane. She'd known him long enough to tell when he was stalling and when he was thinking.

This was thinking.

"You can't tell Omari." He said to the glass. "Your daddy finds that in his house, he's gonna do what your daddy does. Whoever's paying Asha hears Omari Wraithmoor's asking questions, they cut her loose." He turned around. "And they won't cut her loose nicely. They gone want Omari to pay for whatever Asha can't finish."

Isola's stomach turned over.

"And you can't tell Ma," he went on.

Isola sat up straighter if that was even possible. "She's the first person I should—"

"She's got her hands full right now." Remy interrupted her. "And the second Ma knows, it's Ma's business. You won't get to decide nothing."

"So who does that leave?" she asked.

"Me." Remy came back to the table and tapped it twice, right next to the coin. "That's the list."

Isola pushed her plate aside. "And what are *you* gonna do about it?"

"Ask around." He picked the coin up for the first time, holding it by the edges, and turned it in the lamplight. "This is good work, General. This ain't some kid in a back room pressing lead. Somebody spent real money making it look right, and there's only so many people in Shadowmere who'd touch something like this. I know most of 'em."

She raised an eyebrow. "How you know people like that?"

He gave her a look over the coin. "Who you think raised me?"

Fair.

"You tell me first." Isola pointed her egg at him. "Whatever you find out. Before Ma, before Si, before anybody. Promise me."

"I promise." He set the coin back down in front of her and kept two fingers on it until she looked up. "And you promise me you won't go looking for her down here by yourself."

Selah had said nearly the same thing yesterday morning with cold coming off her hands. *Whatever it is, don't go looking.* Isola had gone looking within six hours.

She picked up the coin and put it back in her pocket. "I'll try."

Remy shook his head at her, but the corner of his mouth went up. "You sound just like Si."

Isola had heard the name a few hundred times over the years, usually through the kitchen wall while Remy argued with somebody on the phone. "You ever gonna bring this Si around, or is he imaginary?"

"Never. You'd eat him alive." Remy started folding the tops of the sacks down. "He's a Shadowmere boy." He glanced back at her. "You'd hate him."

She was feeling better. Not good, but better than she'd felt since the coin rolled under Asha's desk, and it surprised her how much of that came down to a kitchen, fried dough, and somebody else holding half of it for a minute.

"Speaking of Shadowmere boys," she said, "some man knocked my salt out of my hand yesterday."

Remy paused with a sack half folded. "At Pell's?"

"Right in front of Pell's. Backed straight into me like he ain't have eyes in the back of his head." She shook her head at her tea. "Then he had the nerve to pay for another bag. Four Veils. Pell put *me* down for eighteen and charged him four, Remy. Somebody had beat his face in, too. Busted lip, eye swole, hand wrapped up, and he's just standing there giving Pell instructions like he run the block or something."

Remy had stopped folding. He set the sack down very carefully. "Locs? Talks like somebody's charging him by the word?"

Isola took a sip of her tea before answering. "How do you know that?"

"Did Pell call him anything?"

*Price is the price, Graves.* She remembered it because she'd been thinking about it all night.

"Graves," she said.

Remy put both hands flat on the counter and laughed so hard he had to bend over it.

She folded her arms and waited. He kept going until he was wheezing. When he finally came up for air, he pointed at her with the towel.

"That's *Si*, Isola. Silas Graves. My best friend."

Isola set her cup down so hard tea jumped over the rim. "It is not."

"He knocked your salt in a puddle?" Remy pressed the towel over his face and laughed into it. "Oh, I'm never letting him live this down."

Isola reached across the table and snatched the towel off him. "You're not telling him a thing, Remy."

"I'm telling him *everything*." He dropped into the chair across from her, still grinning. "Please tell me you cussed him out."

"I told him I ain't want no debt tied to somebody in Shadowmere." She heard how it sounded coming out of her mouth, like she'd been turning it over all night, and kept going before he could notice. "He said the debt was his and I needed to stand somewhere else."

Remy stared at her across the table with his mouth open.

Isola pointed her egg at him again. "Whatever you're about to say, don't."

"I'm not saying nothing." He held up both hands. "I'm just noticing you remember a whole lot about him, General."

She threw her eggshell at him. He caught it against his chest, laughing.

But the laugh changed while she watched. It happened in the middle of it, like a lamp flickering before it goes out. His eyes went to the sacks on the counter, then to the clock over the door, and back to her.

Isola picked a piece of shell off her plate. "He killed somebody at the Ring the night before last, didn't he?"

Remy took a second too long to answer, which was answer enough.

"Jerrick. Lowtorch man who sold some kids down a bad route." He set the eggshell on the table. "Ma wanted it settled. Si don't do it for fun, if that's what you're asking."

"I wasn't asking," she said, picking at the shell.

Remy tipped his head at her. "You were thinking it."

She had been. She'd been thinking she ought to feel worse about the first part than she did.

"There was a girl with him," she said. "Against the freight wall, behind him. Hood up, real small, like she was trying to disappear into the brick."

Remy went still.

"Lotta redheads in Shadowmere," he said, getting up and turning back to the sacks. "Si's always walking somebody somewhere for his daddy. Probably a client. Don't worry about her."

Three sentences.

Isola could read a lie in less than that. Selah said she got it from Omari's side. Omari said she didn't get it from anywhere; she was just nosy.

"Remington," she said, and he flinched, just a little, at the full name.

"I gotta go." He hooked two sacks in one arm, grabbed the third, and shrugged into the ugly green coat one-handed before pulling the messenger bag over his head. "Finish your food. Lock up when you leave. And don't put that coin anywhere but your pocket."

She stood up too. "Who is she, Remy?"

He stopped in the kitchen doorway. For a second he wasn't smiling at all. He looked the way he had three years ago at that long table, sliding mints across the wood.

"Somebody having a worse week than you," he said quietly. "I can't tell you more than that. I'm sorry, General. I mean it."

She believed him, and that was the worst part.

"I'll come up to Ashvein tonight or tomorrow with whatever I find on the coin." He shifted the sacks higher against his chest. "Till then, don't do nothing."

His phone buzzed inside his coat before she could argue. He juggled the sacks to dig it out, read the screen, and his face did something complicated. He turned it around so she could see.

*Si: Where's breakfast?*

Remy grinned at her. "Want me to tell him you said hey?"
